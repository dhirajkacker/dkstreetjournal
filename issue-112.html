Zebra Is Paying for Memory Inflation With a 15% Price Rise, Not With Software Revenue

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Zebra's best growth quarter of the year arrived with its thinnest gross margin of the year, and the market treated the combination as a warning rather than a result. Revenue rose 22.3% in the quarter ended 3 October while gross margin fell to 45.7%, as the company's full-year memory-cost headwind grew to $145m.

The offset is price: an October increase on mobile computing worth about 1.5 points of 2027 revenue, with software still a low single-digit percentage of sales. That is a hardware business re-pricing its bill of materials and telling investors the cost pressure continues next year. Digi International, in the same corner of enterprise connectivity, books $191m of recurring revenue and expands margin as it grows; its fiscal fourth quarter lands 11 November.

Zebra Technologies beat its own quarter on 3 November and fell 8.8% in the session. The maker of barcode scanners, enterprise mobile computers and thermal label printers, sold to retailers, warehouse operators and hospitals, told investors memory prices would keep climbing into 2027, and that its answer is a price increase taken in October.

The shares had held an uptrend since the start of July, read as an enterprise device refresh cycle with recurring software riding on top of it. The quarter tests the second half of that. Zebra's full-year memory-cost headwind rose to $145m from a prior estimate of $120m, entirely because mobile-computing volumes came in higher, and on the call management put software revenue at a low single-digit percentage of the total. The component shock is being absorbed on the invoice, because there is no subscription meter to absorb it instead.

The memory bill became a price list

Revenue of $1.614bn in the quarter ended 3 October, reported to the SEC on an 8-K, was up 22.3%, and operating margin improved to 14.7% from 13.9%. Gross margin went the other way: 45.7%, against 48.0% a year earlier. Conventional dynamic random-access memory contract prices rose 90-95% quarter on quarter early in 2026, with memory forecast to absorb 68% of major cloud providers' capital spending in 2027 — device makers now bid for parts against hyperscalers. Of roughly $50m of memory cost in the quarter, pricing offset about $25m, with more than $55m mitigated by restructuring, productivity and currency.

"It generates about a 15% increase on our mobile computing products," Nathan Winters, Zebra's chief financial officer, said of the October action on the 3 November call. That increase is expected to add roughly 1.5 points to 2027 revenue. On the same call, chief executive Bill Burns said: "We would expect growth, as you suggested, for 2027 to be at the high end of our long-term guidance range" — a range of 5-7%. At the top of it, by that arithmetic, about a fifth of next year's growth is Zebra's own cost inflation handed to its buyers.

Demand is real, and it is hardware demand

Not all of the quarter was price. Organic growth was about 11% of the reported 22.3%, with the Connected Frontline segment up 35% in total but 15% organically after the Elo Touch acquisition, and Asset Visibility & Automation up 7%. Transportation and logistics was the fastest-growing end market, with a refresh cycle beginning in 2027. Executives said they had seen no revenue effect from customers pulling orders forward ahead of the increase, and described demand as broad-based across regions and both segments. Inventory rose about $90m on memory stocking; five new memory suppliers have been qualified against a target of five to seven sources for each memory type. Backlog and pipeline support 10-14% growth in the current quarter, and full-year sales growth guidance went to about 17%.

What none of it produces is a recurring line. Zebra's Frontline artificial-intelligence products were described as drivers of device refresh rather than as a subscription business of their own.

Digi's meter runs on its own clock

Digi International, which sells cellular routers and embedded modules alongside a cloud device-management subscription, is the counterexample in the same niche. Its annualized recurring revenue reached $191m at the end of June, up 52% from $126m and roughly a third of annualized revenue, with gross margin at 64.8% and rising. About $20m of that recurring base arrived with the Particle acquisition, so organic growth is nearer the mid-30s percent. Digi traded below its 200-day moving average on 6 November with no company fundamentals since August; results land 11 November. It trades on 20.2x forward earnings against 48.6x trailing.

What is being repriced is 2027, not the quarter

Zebra's shares fell 15.3% over 30 days against a 5% decline in the S&P 500, and almost all of it sits in two sessions: the post-earnings drop on roughly double normal volume, after Zebra warned memory prices would keep rising, and the 5 November unwind of the artificial-intelligence hardware trade that took the index down 4.4%.

At 15.2x forward earnings against 26.5x trailing, Zebra sits below its own stated five-year history of 20-25x. The gap is what consensus expects: earnings per share of $21.24 this year, then growth of 5.7% next. The forward figure already assumes 2026 does not repeat, which is also what Burns guided to.

So the results account for the direction of the business and not for the direction of the shares. Zebra converts installed devices into reorders at a price it sets, and it has just demonstrated it can set one; Digi converts installed devices into renewals it collects monthly, at a gross margin nearly twenty points higher. The first depends on when customers decide to replace equipment. The second does not.

Zebra has qualified five new memory suppliers and is hunting more. That is a procurement answer to a margin problem, and a sound one. It still leaves the top line waiting on a decision made in somebody else's warehouse.

Okta's revenue growth has decelerated for four consecutive quarters, and the shares still more than doubled in the six months to 6 November. Revenue at the identity company, which sells single sign-on, multi-factor authentication and the acquired Auth0 developer portfolio to enterprises and governments on annual subscriptions, rose 10.6% year over year in the July quarter, the slowest of the four. What moved was margin: gross margin reached 79.6% against 76.9% a year earlier, operating margin 13.3% against 5.6%, and operating income grew 161%.

The advance in Okta and in Rubrik, which sells cyber-recovery software priced on the volume of data under management rather than per user, has been read as proof that identity and backup are the meters agentic AI feeds: every autonomous agent has to authenticate like an employee, and everything it writes has to be recoverable, while workflow software loses human seats. Neither income statement carries that revenue yet. Asked about agent-identity revenue on the 26 August call, Okta chief financial officer Brett Tighe answered "Still immaterial," and said AI-related revenue stays immaterial through fiscal 2027 with a meaningful contribution possible only in fiscal 2028 and beyond, according to the company's transcript. Okta for AI Agents went generally available on 30 April and has closed dozens of deals, several of them worth more than $1m.

Okta's order book is being guided down, not up

Current remaining performance obligation, the contracted revenue Okta expects to recognize within a year and the closest thing it prints to a forward order book, reached $2.585bn, up 14%, ahead of billed revenue. Management guided that growth back to 11-12% for the following quarter. Trailing net retention was flat at 107%. Customers paying more than $100,000 a year grew 6%, to 5,255, while those above $1m grew 22% to more than 600, so the base is concentrating rather than widening. Free cash flow was $227m, a 28% margin against 22% a year earlier. Consensus carries 9.9% revenue growth for the year to January 2028.

The fast-growing bookings line Okta does disclose is a different product set: governance, privileged access and threat protection were 30% of bookings in the quarter at roughly 40% average uplift per deal, up from about 25% in the April quarter. Agent sign-on was folded into the core product at the Oktane conference in September rather than sold as a separate machine-identity line, and chief executive Todd McKinnon told that audience on 23 September that in agent identity "there is going to be massive growth and the market share is going to be set probably in three years."

Rubrik's contracted book accelerated in the same quarter

Rubrik's subscription annual recurring revenue grew 33% to $1.66bn at 31 July, with net new subscription ARR up 35% and subscription net retention above 119%. Customers paying $100,000 or more rose 23% to 3,084 and supply 88% of subscription ARR; cloud is 89% of the mix and grew 39%. Dividing those disclosures gives roughly $474,000 of ARR per large customer against about $438,000 a year earlier, so most of the 33% is landing and graduating big accounts and about a quarter of it is deeper spend inside them.

The income statement is the weaker half. Revenue growth decelerated to 37.9%, gross margin slipped to 78.4% from 79.5% so gross profit grew more slowly than revenue, and the GAAP operating margin was still minus 16.8% on a $61.8m quarterly loss. Rubrik is also not the share leader in backup: Gartner's 2026 Magic Quadrant places Veeam, Rubrik, Commvault and Cohesity in the Leaders quadrant with Veeam ahead, and Veeam reported over $2.1bn of ARR for 2025. Its own agentic product, Code Guardian, which runs Anthropic's Claude through a security harness against cloned copies of customer code repositories, was expanded on 9 October and remains in private preview.

The faster meter carries the dearer price

Rubrik trades at 17.60x trailing gross profit, against 10.48x in early May and 15.59x in early September. It is the one multiple in the pair that kept expanding through the autumn drawdown, and it expanded faster than the 36% growth in gross profit underneath it. Okta's equivalent is 11.74x, more than double its early-May level near 5x but below 13.50x on 25 September. On forward revenue Okta stands at 8.75x against roughly 6.9x in mid-July, when the shares were $139.53.

Both gave back a fifth or more of the advance in October and November. Okta closed 27.3% below its 9 October high of $232.13 and Rubrik 22.2% below its 28 October high of $136.00, with the sharpest session on 5 November, when the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.52% on doubts about AI data-center spending and the whole security complex sold together. No company news for either name was discoverable in that window. Okta fell 11.2% on 2 November, three days after S&P Dow Jones Indices said it would join the S&P 500. Both have held uptrends since June, which Palo Alto Networks and ServiceNow lost in that single November session; Zscaler, billed per user per year, held one too, so survival does not sort by pricing basis. CrowdStrike, billed per endpoint, grew revenue 25.8% with record net new ARR up 51% in its July quarter, faster than either protagonist's contracted book.

Strip the agent premium out of both and what is left is one accelerating meter and one fading one. Okta's margins account for the profitability half of its doubling and nothing of the growth half, because its base is human seats sold against Microsoft's bundled Entra, where 107% retention is what a mature directory earns. Rubrik's 119% retention compounds instead, because protected capacity inside an account grows whether or not the headcount does, and that is the business the market has priced the more expensively of the two.

The test arrives in the same week for both. Okta reports third-quarter results on 1 December and Rubrik on 3 December, which settles the question of whose quarter had merely printed first.

DraftKings' sportsbook kept more of every dollar wagered last quarter than it did a year earlier, and the company still told investors on 6 November that this year's profit has been spent. Sportsbook net revenue at the Boston operator, which takes mobile and retail wagers in 18 states and runs online casino under the DraftKings and Golden Nugget Online brands, rose 36% on handle growth of 12%, because the margin the book earns on handle widened by about 1.2 percentage points to 6.4%, with parlay mix up three points.

For a year this corner of the market has been marked down on a belief about that number: that state taxes, promotional credits and untaxed prediction markets were stripping the revenue out of a wagered dollar. DraftKings is down 33.8% over twelve months and Flutter, owner of FanDuel, 62.3%. The third-quarter prints say the erosion is not in the hold; it sits in what it now costs to put a customer in front of the book.

The line where the profit left

DraftKings cut full-year revenue guidance to $6.35-6.55bn from $6.5-6.9bn and swung adjusted EBITDA guidance from a $700-900m profit to a loss of $175-275m, attributing the move to an increased predictions investment of $650-700m. Core-business EBITDA of $875-925m, management said, remains on track. The quarter itself missed on both lines: an adjusted loss of 53 cents against a 39-cent consensus, on revenue of $1.329bn against $1.398bn. The shares closed 21.3% lower.

The spending has a visible mechanism. New-customer promotional credits offset roughly 90% of the prediction exchange's gross gaming revenue, which is why that revenue is immaterial this year at an 11.1% take rate. Credits that large are the price of attention, and attention has repriced: betting-category media spending rose more than 60% year-on-year in September and more than 50% in October, driven mainly by prediction-market operators' advertising.

"In just a few months, we have become the clear number 3 predictions exchange by total volume. In October, annualized consumer volume reached $10.1 billion up nearly 3x from July," chief executive Jason Robins told investors on 6 November.

Total exchange volume annualised at $56.8bn, about 5.6 times that consumer figure, the gap between contracts re-traded and money a customer brought in.

Flutter gives the parlay gain back

Flutter's quarter is the cleaner confirmation. Revenue grew 2.1% to $3.874bn while gross profit fell 6.6% and gross margin went from 42.9% to 39.2%. FanDuel's US net revenue margin was 6.8% and its adjusted EBITDA fell 94% to $3m. The company guided 2027 US net revenue margin flat because promotional spend annualises at this year's elevated level.

"We are very confident that we can still grow the overall structural margins," chief financial officer Robert Coldrake said on 4 November, calling the pressure "more kind of temporary to medium term." The $300m revenue and $160m EBITDA guidance cuts were attributed to adverse sports results, the Brazil closure and UK gaming tax. The buyback is paused and net leverage is 4.6x against a 2-2.5x target. The shares sit on 18.7x forward earnings, against roughly 49.6x on that same estimate a year ago, and the estimate has itself been cut 42.5%.

Rush Street cut nothing and fell 39%

Rush Street Interactive, the Chicago operator of BetRivers and RushBet and three-quarters online casino by revenue, grew revenue 38.8% to $385.9m, lifted gross margin 2.4 points to 36.4%, nearly doubled operating income and reiterated full-year guidance of $1.56-1.6bn revenue and $245-265m adjusted EBITDA, placing October revenue at the low end. It is debt-free with $382m of cash.

It also fell 39.3% in a month.

Two items in its own disclosure explain part of that: revenue per active user down 15% to $309 on market maturation, which management ties to market-wide North American online-casino moderation while saying it is still gaining share year-on-year against listed peers everywhere but Michigan, and a Colombian bill proposing a 19% value-added tax on online wagering deposits. Forward earnings compressed from about 33x a month ago to 20.1x with no company number changed.

How much of the markdown the prints explain

Flutter's de-rating is earned by its own statements: consensus EBITDA for this year is down 24.7% and the gross-profit line is falling outright. DraftKings' is not a verdict on the business it has, where core profit is intact and it says it tied FanDuel for top US share, but on a discretionary bet whose return arrives in 2027 guidance of $800m to $1.0bn of adjusted EBITDA that consensus, at $770m, has not underwritten. Consensus revenue of $6.66bn still sits above the newly lowered guide. Rush Street's fall is the one with no operating cause attached to it, which leaves the cooling growth rate of regulated online casino as the likelier thing being repriced.

Robins gave the downside arithmetic himself: even if sports event contracts were ended in July 2028, he said, the customers DraftKings acquired this year would be fully paid back by that date, with a Supreme Court ruling in mid-2028 his base case. The money is being spent now against a judgment nobody has written yet.

Data Centers Are Analog Chipmakers' Fastest-Growing Market, and the AI Selloff Found Them

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Analog chips were supposed to be the part of semiconductors insulated from the AI trade. They are not anymore: the fastest-growing end market at Texas Instruments, Analog Devices and NXP is now the data center, which is precisely what repriced on 5 November when a Qualcomm guidance miss and US election results raised the odds of tighter scrutiny of data-center construction.

None of the three cut guidance. Texas Instruments raised on 21 October and NXP on 27 October; Analog Devices guided its fiscal fourth quarter to about 74% gross margin, up roughly 150 basis points sequentially. Texas Instruments' gross margin has climbed from 55.9% to 64.2% in four quarters as internally built 300mm capacity absorbs volume.

The business explains almost none of the two-day fall. It does explain the split in what the three cost: Texas Instruments carries the strongest operating momentum and the dearest price at 18.8x forward gross profit, against NXP's 7.8x.

Texas Instruments told investors in October that its data center business had nearly doubled from a year earlier and now runs at about 13% of revenue. Three weeks later that was the part of the company the market wanted least.

The analog chipmakers spent two years being traded as a bet on industrial and automotive restocking, a cycle with its own clock and no particular relationship to artificial-intelligence capital spending. That belief went into November intact and came out of 5 November damaged. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell roughly 9% that session, with the Nasdaq Composite down 5.5%, after Qualcomm guided current-quarter revenue below consensus and Democratic election victories raised investor expectations of tighter congressional scrutiny of data-center construction, power permitting and AI-infrastructure spending. Analog's newest socket is the one Washington was suddenly discussing.

The category's markdown was mostly the smaller companies

Over the 30 trading days to 6 November the analog shelf came apart by size rather than by fundamentals. Semtech fell 49.8%, Diodes 26.0%, Microchip 25.6%, ON Semiconductor 24.8% and Monolithic Power 17.0%. Texas Instruments lost 4.7% and NXP actually gained 5.3%. What hit the three big names was two sessions: between 4 and 6 November Texas Instruments fell 12.2%, Analog Devices 9.2% and NXP 5.4%, with Analog Devices trading 13.1m shares on 5 November against a typical two to four million. Analog Devices' highest close of the past 120 days was $429.25, set on 29 October.

What a 300mm wafer saves Texas Instruments

The fixed cost of the fabs built for this cycle is converting into margin right now. Texas Instruments, which sells power-management and signal-chain analog parts and embedded processors into industrial, automotive and electronics customers, has lifted gross margin from 55.9% to 64.2% across four quarters while revenue growth accelerated from 10.4% to 27.7% year on year. The company puts the cost advantage of its 300mm wafer manufacturing at roughly 40% against 200mm alternatives. Industrial revenue grew 40% in the September quarter, reaching its 2022 peak for the first time in four years.

"I do believe that systems that are being designed by our customers 4 years later than the COVID peak are much more rich with content," chief executive Haviv Ilan told investors on 21 October, the day the company guided fourth-quarter revenue to $5.8bn to $6.3bn.

The depreciation on that capacity is landing — $2.2bn to $2.4bn projected for 2026 after rising 23% in the first half, against capex cut to $2bn to $3bn — which is why trailing free cash flow inflected to $8.4bn from $2.4bn a year earlier, $1.6bn of it CHIPS Act incentives. It is also the most expensive stock of the three at 35.9x trailing earnings and 18.8x forward gross profit, so the strongest operating momentum has the least room in its price.

Analog Devices' channel is running below its own target

Analog Devices, whose data converters, amplifiers and isolators go to industrial, instrumentation and automotive customers, is accelerating too: revenue growth of 30.4%, 37.2% and 39.6% year on year across its last three quarters, GAAP gross margin up from 64.7% to 67.3%. Non-GAAP gross margin of 72.5% in the August quarter was 330 basis points higher than a year before, "driven by higher revenue, utilization, and favorable mix", and the company guided its fiscal fourth quarter to $4.3bn plus or minus $100m at about 74% gross margin and 52% operating margin.

Distributor channel inventory sits below its six-to-seven-week target and is expected to stay there. Management described book-to-bill as above one but "not in an unusually high space from a book-to-bill perspective" on its 19 August call, with no current signs of restocking. The trailing multiple has compressed from 57.9x in August to 42.9x; on consensus fiscal 2027 earnings, 30.6% above this year's, it is 21.6x.

NXP got the margin from utilization, not price

NXP's 58.6% non-GAAP gross margin in the September quarter was 160 basis points better year on year, and management attributed it to factories running in the mid-80s rather than to price increases. Pricing for 2026 was revised from a low-single-digit decline to neutral. Automotive revenue crossed $2bn for the first time, up 17% excluding sensors, on content per vehicle in a flattish production environment. "The book to bill remains well above 1 across all end markets and regions," chief financial officer William Betts said on 27 October. "Our backlog continues to grow in dollars and extend further into 2027."

Two things sit against that. NXP guided 2027 capex to 5% of revenue from roughly 2%, so its depreciation headwind is ahead of it while Texas Instruments' peaks. And 17% to 18% of revenue is China-for-China, about a third of it manufactured locally, with a local foundry partnership under way against Beijing's push for domestic automotive chips. At 13.5x consensus 2027 earnings and 7.8x forward gross profit, it is the cheapest of the three by a wide margin, and the only one up over three months.

What the quarters explain and what they do not

The results account for essentially none of the two-day fall. All three guided higher inside the markdown window, utilization is rising, channel inventory is thin and lead times at NXP have stretched so that 80% now exceed 16 weeks against 25% a year ago. What the results do explain is the gap between what the three cost. Texas Instruments is priced for the fall-through its own model promises on incremental revenue; NXP is discounted for automotive cyclicality, a capex step-up and China; Analog Devices sits between them, having given back a third of its trailing multiple since August with nothing visible broken.

The transmission mechanism is the thing to keep. Data center is the fastest-growing line at each of the three, and that leg is now valued the way AI infrastructure is valued rather than the way analog is. Analog Devices' fiscal fourth-quarter numbers are still to come, and they will be the first read on whether an election result changed any analog order at all.
