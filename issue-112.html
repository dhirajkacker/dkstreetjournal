Keysight's Orders Grew 56% on AI Test Demand, and That Exposure Is Why Its Shares Fell

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Keysight booked more orders than it shipped last quarter and has since lost more than a quarter of its market value, with no guidance change, no export-control action and no contract loss to explain it. Orders reached $2,091m against $1,846m of revenue, and reported revenue growth accelerated through the fiscal year to 36.5%.

Inside the same test-instrument group, the markdown sorted by how much artificial-intelligence revenue each name carries. Fortive, whose Fluke franchise management puts at low single digits of sales for data centers, rose 14% over the 30 days to 6 November; Keysight, whose AI business ran at $500m-$600m in the first half, fell 28.6%. Ralliant and Teledyne both raised full-year guidance inside the window.

Keysight's forward earnings multiple compressed to 19.7x from roughly 27.7x a month earlier. The business explains none of the move; the AI label explains most of it.

Keysight Technologies took in more orders last quarter than it shipped: roughly $2.1bn against $1.8bn of revenue. The orders were up 56% from a year earlier and were the second consecutive quarter above $2bn for the company, which sells oscilloscopes, radio-frequency and optical test systems and design software to communications, defense, automotive and semiconductor engineers. Since early October the shares have lost more than a quarter of their value, with no guidance change, no contract loss and no regulatory action attached to the company.

General-purpose instruments are supposed to be the insulated corner of the electronics complex: they are paid for out of engineering and research budgets at design time, well before anyone pours concrete for a data center. The past month tested that belief inside a single group of vendors, and the result was not flattering to it. What determined whether a test-instrument maker was marked down was how much AI revenue its name carried, not how its order book was running.

The half of Keysight nearest the buildout is the half growing

Keysight's Communications Solutions Group did $1,345m of revenue in the quarter ended 31 July, up 43%, on 56% growth in commercial communications and 14% in aerospace, defense and government. The Electronic Industrial Solutions Group did a record $501m, up 21%. Gross margin reached 65.9% against 61.7% a year earlier, and operating margin 25.0%.

"The scaling challenges associated with AI data center deployments are driving a multiyear industry road map for new architectures, evolving technologies and new standards," chief executive Satish Dhanasekaran said on the August 18 earnings call. On the same call he sized the AI business at the "$500 million-$600 million range" for the first half of the fiscal year and said it was "largely in the wireline segment," where orders more than doubled.

Two things complicate the growth. Reported revenue is flattered by the $1,415m purchase of Spirent Communications, net of cash acquired, closed in the prior fiscal year's fourth quarter. And the recurring mix thinned: software and services were about 33% of revenue against 36% a quarter earlier, with annual recurring revenue at 24% against 27%, diluted by hardware growing faster than either.

The de-rating is wide. Keysight trades at 19.7x the $13.77 of fiscal-2027 consensus earnings per share, against roughly 27.7x on the same estimate at the October 7 close, and 37.4x trailing against 59.2x in May 2026.

Fluke's owner went up through the same sessions

Fortive, which owns the Fluke handheld-instrument and calibration business, is the counter-case within the group. Core growth at its Intelligent Operating Solutions segment accelerated to 9% in the third quarter, Fluke orders accelerated and book-to-bill held above 1.0 on data-center, defense and technician demand. Management put data-center revenue at only low single digits of sales. "We are supply constrained, frankly, at this point in terms of the talent and the capacity we want to build, because the market is certainly there for us," chief executive Olumide Soroye told investors on October 28, describing a specialized data-center sales team for Fluke. Fortive raised full-year earnings guidance to $3.10-$3.14 and rose 14.0% over the 30 days to November 6. It trades at 21.2x forward earnings against 30.8x trailing.

Ralliant raised guidance and was sold twice

Ralliant, the Tektronix and Keithley owner spun out of Fortive this year, grew its Test and Measurement segment 21% organically to $244m with segment margin up 670 basis points to 19.3%. The composition matters: diversified electronics up 48%, communications up 4%, semiconductor down 7%. Book-to-bill ran above 1.1 in both segments, and management reported channel inventory clearing on sell-through. In September Tektronix launched a fully regenerative dynamic test rig scaling to 1.92 megawatts to validate the 800-volt direct-current power architectures appearing in AI data centers by emulating graphics-processor load swings.

Ralliant raised 2026 guidance on November 4 to $2.34bn-$2.36bn of revenue and $3.10-$3.16 of earnings per share, with net leverage at 1.5x. The shares fell 4.8% that day and 7.5% the next. On the raised midpoint the forward multiple is about 21.6x. One caveat belongs beside the margin: a $7m tariff refund was worth 115 basis points of the reported gross-margin gain, and management said roughly $14m of such refunds will not repeat next year.

Teledyne's instruments line disagrees with its own order book

Teledyne held its price, and its results undercut any claim that these four businesses move for a single reason. Its electronic test and measurement revenue declined 6.3% in the September quarter on order timing, even as orders there rose 18% to a record 1.23 times billings. The growth carrying the company comes from elsewhere: Digital Imaging up 14.3% organically, with infrared detectors for space imaging up more than 25%, and defense electronics up 7.4%. "Our third quarter sales and operating earnings were the strongest in Teledyne's history," executive chairman Robert Mehrabian said on October 21, raising full-year guidance for the third consecutive quarter. The stock rose 0.7% over the 30 days and sits at 24.2x forward earnings against 28.3x trailing; it never got expensive and it never fell.

What the results account for, and what they do not

Nothing in the reported numbers accounts for Keysight's drawdown. Two of these four companies raised full-year guidance inside the window, the third set its guidance in August and has not touched it, and nobody disclosed a channel inventory build. The drawdown instead lands on a handful of sessions with named market-wide causes: October 22, when Lam Research's warning about 2027 supply-chain constraints pulled the Nasdaq Composite down 2.18%, and November 5, when that index fell 5.5% and roughly $3tn of S&P 500 market value was erased on doubts that hyperscaler capital spending converts to cash flow.

So a pricing event in the AI complex passed through a group of instrument vendors in proportion to their AI labels. The open question sits on the other side of the ledger: consensus has Keysight growing revenue 14.7% next fiscal year after 33.3% this one, which already assumes the order surge fades, and the wireline AI business carrying that surge is concentrated among a handful of hyperscale customers. An order book running 1.13 times billings is a claim about the next two quarters, not the next five years.

Keysight reports again on November 19. The order line, which has now run ahead of revenue for two consecutive quarters, is where a reader will learn whether the past month was ever about instruments.

Chip Designers Want Their Own CPU Cores: Synopsys Sold Its Processor IP, Arm Now Sells Silicon

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The layer that gets paid before any silicon ships has split into two businesses with opposite economics. Arm's licensees shipped more than 10 billion chips in a single quarter for the first time and paid only 8% more in royalties, so the blended rate per chip went backwards; licence revenue hit a record, which is the pipeline being sold up front.

Arm's answer to that ceiling is to sell its own data-center processor at a guided gross margin in the high 30s to low 40s, against roughly 97% on licensing. Synopsys took the other exit, selling its processor-core business to GlobalFoundries and citing customers' preference for developing their own processor IP.

The toolchain is not in the same argument. Cadence's backlog stands at a record $8.2bn and its recurring revenue growth doubled to 18%, while the shares de-rated with everything else in the complex.

Arm's partners shipped more than 10 billion Arm-based chips in a single quarter for the first time, and the royalties Arm collected on them grew at the slowest rate in eight quarters. Royalty revenue rose 8% to $670m while shipments rose by 1.5 billion chips year on year. Divide one by the other and the blended rate works out near 6.5 cents a chip, down from roughly 7 cents a year earlier — a derived figure, but the direction is not in dispute, because Arm guided December-quarter royalty growth to the low single digits.

Custom accelerators, chiplets and hyperscaler in-house silicon were supposed to multiply the money collected at the design layer. What the autumn prints show is that the design layer is indeed being paid more, through the invoice for tools and verification hardware rather than through the per-chip rate — and the two companies that owned licensable CPU cores have each found a way out of selling them.

The core is the part customers now build themselves

Synopsys, which sells chip-design software and a large silicon IP catalogue, completed the sale of its Processor IP Solutions business to GlobalFoundries on 1 June 2026 for $443.3m — the ARC and ARC-V RISC-V cores, the digital-signal and neural-processing IP, and the MetaWare toolkits. Chief executive Sassine Ghazi cited "customer preference for developing their own processor IP." The divestiture is why Synopsys' backlog slipped sequentially to $10.9bn in the July quarter even as reported revenue grew 42%, most of that from the Ansys acquisition.

What Synopsys kept is the plumbing an in-house design team cannot write itself. Its Design IP segment returned to growth at $474m, up roughly 11%, on a 95% win rate in PCIe 7 interfaces and 25 design wins for the next memory standard.

GlobalFoundries, the specialty foundry that bought those cores, is using them to sell design wins rather than only wafers: it booked roughly $1bn of lifetime revenue in third-quarter wins including a RISC-V real-time control processor and a data-center board management controller, with communications infrastructure and data center reaching 17% of revenue, up 81% year on year. "We are absolutely booked out through 2027," chief executive Tim Breen told investors on 4 November.

Arm traded a 97% margin for a 40% one

Arm's response to the same ceiling was to build the chip itself. Its AGI data-center processor, launched on 24 March 2026 with Meta as debut customer, now carries a target of up to $2bn of revenue across fiscal 2027 and 2028, raised from $1bn-plus. "In the past 90 days, we have signed purchase orders from multiple customers and signed supply agreements with companies in our supply chain," chief financial officer Jason Child said on the 4 November call. The guided gross margin is high 30s to low 40s initially, against the 97.1% the licensing business earns.

The per-socket story is real where it works. Data-center royalties doubled year on year, and chief executive Rene Haas told investors that Arm-based server share at the top hyperscalers "has more than tripled from 15% in calendar 2024 to more than 50% in calendar 2025." It is not enough to lift the blend, because the incremental billion-plus units are low-rate edge silicon and because elevated memory prices inflated handset bills of materials, squeezing the smartphone royalty base. Meanwhile licence revenue jumped 50% to a record $773m: four new compute-subsystem deals that convert to royalties in 12 to 24 months, booked as cash today.

Cadence got paid for the same design starts, under contract

Cadence, which sells design software alongside its Palladium emulation and Protium prototyping machines, grew September-quarter revenue 22% and ended the quarter with record backlog of $8.2bn, $4.2bn of it due inside twelve months. Recurring revenue growth doubled to 18% from 11%. The emulation hardware line — the piece that is a capital order from the same hyperscalers whose spending is now being questioned — set another record, and full-year guidance went up to 21% growth with the earnings target lifted $0.30 to $8.40. Reported operating margin fell to 26.2% from 33.8%, which is the Hexagon design-and-engineering integration carrying about $160m of revenue at minimal operating profit this year, against a 44.1% non-GAAP margin.

Chief executive Anirudh Devgan gave the mechanism on 26 October: "TSMC showed their process road map and said in the next 5 years, the chip size will go up by 48x. 48x." Verification work scales with die area whoever owns the core.

Where the markdown was deserved and where it was not

Over thirty days Arm fell 40.7% and sits 61.4% below its 52-week high; Synopsys fell 10.5% and Cadence 10.8%, the two shallowest declines on the shelf, and over three months Synopsys is up 8.3%. Most of the late damage was one session, the 5 November selloff in which the Nasdaq fell 5.52% into correction on the midterm result's implications for the AI buildout, already the complex-wide event this paper described when analog chipmakers caught it.

Arm's de-rating is doing rational work. At $174.62 the shares still cost 78.7x consensus 2027 earnings against 200.7x trailing, while consensus models revenue up 36.8% in fiscal 2028 on a royalty line growing 8% — and the company faces a Qualcomm trial over its licensing terms expected this quarter plus a US Federal Trade Commission antitrust inquiry into its handling of licensees.

Cadence's does not. Its forward earnings multiple is 38.1x on this year's consensus and 32.5x on next year's, against 63.2x trailing and the roughly 50-55x forward it commanded in May, on a contracted book that is larger and converting faster. The split has a cause: Arm's price per chip was fixed years before tape-out and is exposed to what memory costs a handset maker today, while Cadence's is a multi-year subscription plus a machine the customer must buy before the chip exists.

Arm's data-center royalties doubled in the quarter and its blended rate per chip still went down. The licensable core keeps getting more valuable in the sockets where it survives, and fewer designers keep paying per chip for someone else's — which is why the two firms that held those cores each took a different way out, and why the companies selling the tools to build the replacements never had to choose.

Zebra Technologies beat its own quarter on 3 November and fell 8.8% in the session. The memory bill became a price list

Revenue of $1.614bn in the quarter ended 3 October, reported to the SEC on an 8-K, was up 22.3%, and operating margin improved to 14.7% from 13.9%. "It generates about a 15% increase on our mobile computing products," Nathan Winters, Zebra's chief financial officer, said of the October action on the 3 November call. That increase is expected to add roughly 1.5 points to 2027 revenue. On the same call, chief executive Bill Burns said: "We would expect growth, as you suggested, for 2027 to be at the high end of our long-term guidance range" — a range of 5-7%. At the top of it, by that arithmetic, about a fifth of next year's growth is Zebra's own cost inflation handed to its buyers.

Demand is real, and it is hardware demand

Backlog and pipeline support 10-14% growth in the current quarter, and full-year sales growth guidance went to about 17%.

What none of it produces is a recurring line. Zebra's Frontline artificial-intelligence products were described as drivers of device refresh rather than as a subscription business of their own.

Zebra's shares fell 15.3% over 30 days against a 5% decline in the S&P 500, and almost all of it sits in two sessions: the post-earnings drop on roughly double normal volume, after Zebra warned memory prices would keep rising, and the 5 November unwind of the artificial-intelligence hardware trade that took the index down 4.4%.

The first depends on when customers decide to replace equipment. The second does not.

Zebra has qualified five new memory suppliers and is hunting more. That is a procurement answer to a margin problem, and a sound one. It still leaves the top line waiting on a decision made in somebody else's warehouse.

Texas Instruments told investors in October that its data center business had nearly doubled from a year earlier and now runs at about 13% of revenue. Three weeks later that was the part of the company the market wanted least.

The analog chipmakers spent two years being traded as a bet on industrial and automotive restocking, a cycle with its own clock and no particular relationship to artificial-intelligence capital spending. That belief went into November intact and came out of 5 November damaged. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell roughly 9% that session, with the Nasdaq Composite down 5.5%, after Qualcomm guided current-quarter revenue below consensus and Democratic election victories raised investor expectations of tighter congressional scrutiny of data-center construction, power permitting and AI-infrastructure spending. Analog's newest socket is the one Washington was suddenly discussing.

The category's markdown was mostly the smaller companies

Over the 30 trading days to 6 November the analog shelf came apart by size rather than by fundamentals. Semtech fell 49.8%, Diodes 26.0%, Microchip 25.6%, ON Semiconductor 24.8% and Monolithic Power 17.0%. Texas Instruments lost 4.7% and NXP actually gained 5.3%. What hit the three big names was two sessions: between 4 and 6 November Texas Instruments fell 12.2%, Analog Devices 9.2% and NXP 5.4%, with Analog Devices trading 13.1m shares on 5 November against a typical two to four million. Analog Devices' highest close of the past 120 days was $429.25, set on 29 October.

What a 300mm wafer saves Texas Instruments

The fixed cost of the fabs built for this cycle is converting into margin right now. Texas Instruments, which sells power-management and signal-chain analog parts and embedded processors into industrial, automotive and electronics customers, has lifted gross margin from 55.9% to 64.2% across four quarters while revenue growth accelerated from 10.4% to 27.7% year on year. The company puts the cost advantage of its 300mm wafer manufacturing at roughly 40% against 200mm alternatives. Industrial revenue grew 40% in the September quarter, reaching its 2022 peak for the first time in four years.

"I do believe that systems that are being designed by our customers 4 years later than the COVID peak are much more rich with content," chief executive Haviv Ilan told investors on 21 October, the day the company guided fourth-quarter revenue to $5.8bn to $6.3bn.

The depreciation on that capacity is landing — $2.2bn to $2.4bn projected for 2026 after rising 23% in the first half, against capex cut to $2bn to $3bn — which is why trailing free cash flow inflected to $8.4bn from $2.4bn a year earlier, $1.6bn of it CHIPS Act incentives. It is also the most expensive stock of the three at 35.9x trailing earnings and 18.8x forward gross profit, so the strongest operating momentum has the least room in its price.

Analog Devices' channel is running below its own target

Analog Devices, whose data converters, amplifiers and isolators go to industrial, instrumentation and automotive customers, is accelerating too: revenue growth of 30.4%, 37.2% and 39.6% year on year across its last three quarters, GAAP gross margin up from 64.7% to 67.3%. Non-GAAP gross margin of 72.5% in the August quarter was 330 basis points higher than a year before, "driven by higher revenue, utilization, and favorable mix", and the company guided its fiscal fourth quarter to $4.3bn plus or minus $100m at about 74% gross margin and 52% operating margin.

Distributor channel inventory sits below its six-to-seven-week target and is expected to stay there. Management described book-to-bill as above one but "not in an unusually high space from a book-to-bill perspective" on its 19 August call, with no current signs of restocking. The trailing multiple has compressed from 57.9x in August to 42.9x; on consensus fiscal 2027 earnings, 30.6% above this year's, it is 21.6x.

NXP got the margin from utilization, not price

NXP's 58.6% non-GAAP gross margin in the September quarter was 160 basis points better year on year, and management attributed it to factories running in the mid-80s rather than to price increases. Pricing for 2026 was revised from a low-single-digit decline to neutral. Automotive revenue crossed $2bn for the first time, up 17% excluding sensors, on content per vehicle in a flattish production environment. "The book to bill remains well above 1 across all end markets and regions," chief financial officer William Betts said on 27 October. "Our backlog continues to grow in dollars and extend further into 2027."

Two things sit against that. NXP guided 2027 capex to 5% of revenue from roughly 2%, so its depreciation headwind is ahead of it while Texas Instruments' peaks. And 17% to 18% of revenue is China-for-China, about a third of it manufactured locally, with a local foundry partnership under way against Beijing's push for domestic automotive chips. At 13.5x consensus 2027 earnings and 7.8x forward gross profit, it is the cheapest of the three by a wide margin, and the only one up over three months.

What the quarters explain and what they do not

The results account for essentially none of the two-day fall. All three guided higher inside the markdown window, utilization is rising, channel inventory is thin and lead times at NXP have stretched so that 80% now exceed 16 weeks against 25% a year ago. What the results do explain is the gap between what the three cost. Texas Instruments is priced for the fall-through its own model promises on incremental revenue; NXP is discounted for automotive cyclicality, a capex step-up and China; Analog Devices sits between them, having given back a third of its trailing multiple since August with nothing visible broken.

The transmission mechanism is the thing to keep. Data center is the fastest-growing line at each of the three, and that leg is now valued the way AI infrastructure is valued rather than the way analog is. Analog Devices' fiscal fourth-quarter numbers are still to come, and they will be the first read on whether an election result changed any analog order at all.
