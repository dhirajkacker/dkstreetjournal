Vehicle-Tracking Subscriptions Now Pay Three-Quarters of Ituran's Record Revenue

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A markdown that was read as proof that device demand had rolled over has now hit the one business on the comparison that barely sells devices. Ituran reported its September quarter on 24 November: subscription fees of $80.5m, up 19% and 76% of revenue, against product sales of $26m, up 5%. The subscriber base grew by 40,000 net adds from ongoing operations to 2.68 million, and operating margin widened to 23.8% from 21.3%.

The two hardware-weighted names on the same comparison went the other way on price and the same way on results: Digi International closed fiscal 2026 with revenue up 24.4% and record recurring revenue of $197m, and Zebra Technologies raised its full-year sales growth guidance to about 17%. All three fell. The weight of the evidence points to a cost-and-rating event, not a demand one: memory inflation is measurable at Zebra, and Ituran's monthly meter is the part of the shelf it cannot reach.

Ituran Location and Control, which sells stolen-vehicle recovery and fleet-tracking services to insurers, carmakers and drivers from a base in Azor, Israel, reported its September quarter on 24 November with more subscribers than it started with. Net additions from ongoing operations came to 40,000, revenue set a record at $106.6m, up 15%, and over the following two sessions the shares closed at their lowest level in a year.

The drawdown running through edge and industrial connectivity names since late October has been read as a demand event: retail and warehouse buyers deferring devices, the connected-device cycle peaking. Ituran was the cleanest test of that reading, because almost nothing it books depends on winning a new hardware order. The base grew, the fees grew, the margin widened, and the price went down with the box sellers.

The meter is about $10 a month, 2.68 million times over

Subscription revenue of $80.5m across the 2.68 million subscribers on the books at the end of September works out, by derivation from the company's results release, to roughly $10.01 per subscriber per month. Product revenue, the in-vehicle devices themselves, was $26m. Operating income rose 29% to $25.4m, lifting operating margin to 23.8% from 21.3% a year earlier, and net income reached $18.0m. Diluted earnings of $0.91 a share, up from $0.74, still came in below consensus. The quarter's blemish was a one-time removal of 67,000 low-average-revenue subscribers after a US customer's bankruptcy, which the company put at about $1m of 2027 revenue. The board declared a dividend of $0.50 a share. Israel supplies 56% of revenue, Brazil 22%.

The annuity is now the cheapest thing in the comparison: 14.4x trailing earnings and 13.0x forward, against 16.6x trailing in August, on a 9.0% trailing free-cash-flow yield. The forward figure rests on a single analyst's estimates and carries correspondingly less weight.

Both hardware names raised their numbers into the fall

Digi International, which sells cellular routers and embedded wireless modules with a cloud management subscription attached, closed fiscal 2026 with revenue up 24.4% to $535.2m, operating income up 42.1%, and record annual recurring revenue of $197m, up 30%; it pulled its $200m recurring-revenue target forward a year, into fiscal 2027. The qualifier is the guide: 10-15% revenue growth for the new year, with first-quarter revenue of $140-144m against a $144.7m consensus. The shares fell 13.9% the session after the 11 November print. Digi now trades at 5.3x forward gross profit, against 9.6x on 18 August.

On the memory shortage, chief executive Ronald Konezny told investors on 11 November: "We don't want to take advantage of any kind of one-time or transitionary increase in pricing."

Zebra Technologies, which supplies barcode scanners, rugged mobile computers and thermal label printers to retail, logistics and healthcare customers, grew third-quarter revenue 22.3% to $1.614bn, its fastest rate of the year, and raised full-year sales growth guidance to about 17% from about 15% with per-share earnings of $21.20. Its full-year memory-cost headwind rose to $145m from $120m, attributed to higher mobile-computing volumes rather than worse pricing, and gross margin fell 228 basis points to 45.7%. Management expects memory prices to keep rising through the first half of 2027 and declined to size next year's hit. "It generates about a 15% increase on our mobile computing products," chief financial officer Nathan Winters said of the October price rise on the 3 November call. The stock sits at 14.9x forward earnings against 26.2x trailing and a five-year range of roughly 20-25x.

What the markdown was actually about

Over the 30 days to 25 November, Digi fell 30.1%, Zebra 17.1% and Ituran 15.0%. Across twelve months Digi is up 27.4% and Zebra 27.7%, while Ituran is up 7.2%; on a 180-day view the annuity is the worst of the three, down 28.8%.

The reported numbers account for almost none of that. What they do explain is why the three should not be marked identically. Zebra buys memory by volume for every mobile computer it ships, and the contract price of a 16Gb DDR5 chip went from $8.70 in October 2025 to $48 by September 2026 as artificial-intelligence buyers outbid everyone else. Digi carries the same input and chose not to pass it through, protecting the subscription relationships it is still converting. Ituran's cost base is only about a quarter hardware, and its margin went up. The rest looks like rating compression inside a general repricing: the S&P 500 fell 8.6% in the week to 20 November, leaving it 11.6% below its October high.

All three have now reported, and nothing on their calendars tests the demand question again until February. Until then, 2.68 million vehicles get billed every month whether or not memory is scarce.

Cognizant Technology Solutions, the Teaneck, New Jersey firm that sells consulting, technology and outsourcing work through an offshore-weighted delivery model, confirmed on 25 November that it is in talks to take over parts of S&P Global's Capital IQ operations, a transaction that would potentially transfer about 1,500 India-based data and operations employees to it while S&P Global reviews options for the unit including a spin-off. The work those people do is process operations — the line item agentic software is said to erase first.

Six days earlier, BofA Securities had reinstated coverage of Cognizant at Neutral with a $70 target, arguing AI is a structural rather than cyclical headwind for IT services because it erodes billable hours and shifts delivery risk onto the integrator. By 19 November the shares had fallen 15.4% from $66.54 on 5 November. The premise of that call is that an offshore pyramid gets smaller from here. The company's own disclosures, and now its deal pipeline, describe one that is getting larger.

What the delivery engine did in the September quarter

Headcount reached 359,900 at 30 September, up 10,100 from a year earlier, a 2.9% increase against revenue growth of 4.6% — revenue outgrew heads by under two percentage points, which is not what a collapse in hours sold looks like. Blended utilization excluding trainees improved to 86% from 85%. Trailing-twelve-month voluntary attrition in technology services was 12.6%, identical to a year earlier. Adjusted operating margin of 16.3% was the highest in four years and the seventh consecutive quarter of year-on-year expansion.

The shelter is contractual. Fixed-price and transaction-based work is now 58% of revenue, up 140 basis points in a year and 500 basis points since the third quarter of 2022; price the scope before the hours are worked and a productivity gain accrues to the vendor until renewal. "The pivot is switching from productivity to growth imperatives using AI. And the rebaselining of productivity has already happened to a large extent," chief executive Ravi Kumar told investors on the 29 October third-quarter call.

What is deteriorating is demand. Reported revenue growth went 5.8%, 4.5%, 4.6% across 2026, and full-year constant-currency guidance was cut to 4.0-4.4% from 4.0-5.5% on discretionary spending in health care and consumer-facing sectors plus heavier fourth-quarter furloughs. Reported operating margin fell 59 basis points to 15.40%, the gap to the adjusted figure being Project Leap restructuring, of which roughly $160m was spent this year.

Accenture fell on nothing it had disclosed

The larger name moved with it for no company-specific reason. Accenture, the Dublin-headquartered professional-services firm, dropped 17.6% from $265.16 on 5 November to $218.58 on 19 November, having said nothing new since 1 October, when fourth-quarter revenue of $18.68bn (up 6.2%) carried a 39.7% rise in operating income and record full-year bookings of $84.54bn. Managed services supplied $12.77bn of the quarter's $22.17bn in bookings at a book-to-bill of 1.4, against consulting's 1.0. BofA's November note put $288 on Accenture, on the view that clients concentrate AI spending with fewer, larger partners. Both stocks then rebounded in three sessions to 25 November, Accenture by 10.8% and Cognizant by 13.2%, the latter helped by a Wedbush initiation at Outperform with a $68 target.

So the November drawdown was a judgment about 2028, not about either September quarter. The results account for Accenture's three-month advance of 29.2% — which is mostly the single 15.9% session on results day — and for none of the subsequent fall. For Cognizant they account for the de-rating only if demand, rather than delivery, is the thing breaking: guidance was indeed cut, while utilization, attrition and margin all improved. At 11.0x forward earnings, against roughly 14-15x in May, the price now carries a structural claim the filings have not yet produced evidence for. Accenture, at 16.4x forward against 17.6x trailing, has given more of its own year back but still sits near the upper half of a 2026 range that ran from 9.2x to 21.3x on last year's earnings.

The 17 December first-quarter report is the next disclosure either company will make. Until then the most informative thing in the sector is a set of talks about 1,500 people in India whose jobs the thesis says should not exist.

Ambarella reported the best quarter in its history on 24 November and told investors the next one would be roughly a third smaller. Revenue for the quarter ended 31 October was $115.2m. Ambarella's own account is narrower and stranger. Gross margin was 59.5% on a non-GAAP basis in the October quarter and is guided to 59-60% for January even as revenue falls about 37% sequentially. The price of each part is going up; the number of parts is going down. What broke is the customer's ability to buy the commodity memory that sits beside Ambarella's chip.

Scarce memory routes to the priciest cameras

TrendForce has conventional memory contract prices rising 10-15% quarter on quarter in the fourth quarter, with suppliers in November prioritizing high-bandwidth and server memory and restricting consumer supply. A camera maker that cannot complete a bill of materials does not buy the processor either. "Customers are allocating their very scarce memory supply to their highest value products. And so that often contains our newer chips," vice president of corporate development Louis Gerhardy said on the 24 November call. Those newer parts carry per-chip prices above $20 for the CV75 and CV72 families, higher still for CV7 and CV8.

So the mix moves one way and the volume moves the other. Internet-of-things products were above 70% of revenue, split roughly evenly between consumer and enterprise, with automotive about 30%; enterprise and automotive met plan, and consumer declined sequentially in its seasonally strongest quarter. "The majority of the revenue reduction is pushed out, and there are some cancellations too," chief executive Fermi Wang told investors. "The engagement didn't slow down. It's really about how to mitigate the memory risk."

Two numbers say the customers are genuinely strained. Days sales outstanding went to 47 days from 32, which management flagged as a stress signal, and the company repurchased no stock in the quarter, suspending its $50m authorization.

Five months took two-thirds off the price

Growth had been fading for four quarters before the guide: 20.1% year on year, then 16.9%, 13.2% and 6.2%. The January range implies about a 28% decline against the year-ago quarter. Ambarella trades at 3.70x forward sales on a consensus revenue figure of $428m that predates the guide; nine months of actuals plus the guide midpoint imply about $396m, which puts the real figure nearer 4.0x. In July, at $77 a share, the same business was paid about 6.8x forward sales, and roughly 11x in May. Morgan Stanley cut the stock to Equalweight with a $42 target from $70 and Bank of America to Underperform at $30, both citing the memory squeeze.

How much of the slide belongs to the company? The single session does: a guide 37% below the quarter just reported explains a 27.9% fall without help. The weeks before it do not. Ambarella had traded below its moving-average trend since mid-September and sharply so from 21 October, a stretch in which Nvidia fell 28.6% over thirty days and the Nasdaq 100 entered a bear market on 25 November, down 20% from its October high. The sensor and specialized-silicon names fell with that complex, on timelines six weeks apart.

What the company has not done is cut its ambition. That independence is why the shares carried a premium. It is also why a shortage in another company's product line now sets Ambarella's shipment schedule.

Amkor Technology, which packages and tests other companies' finished chips under contract from a base in Tempe, Arizona, sold fewer advanced-packaging dollars in the September quarter than a year earlier and made considerably more money doing it. Advanced-product net sales came in at $1,631.6m, down 3.1% from $1,683.5m, while group revenue was flat at $1.991bn and gross profit rose 27.8%. The gross profit extracted per dollar of revenue went from 14.3 cents to 18.3 cents.

That is the opposite of what the shares have been saying. Amkor and ASE Technology Holding, the Kaohsiung group that is the largest listed packager outside the foundries, have been marked down since late October on a reading that advanced packaging has peaked and that outsourced assembly earns a thin, cyclical fee per unit. The filings and the October calls say per-package economics reached records in the same quarter, and that what deteriorated is the market's willingness to fund plants that bill nothing until 2027 and beyond.

What one package now pays

Amkor's gain came from price and mix per package, not from more advanced units: with advanced products down, the mainstream lead-frame and wirebond remainder implied by the filing grew about 18%, to roughly $359m. Computing revenue rose 25% sequentially to a record on a high-density fan-out central-processor program and a new hyperscaler program, with two further 2.5-dimensional networking and datacenter programs coming online in the December quarter. Communications fell 8% sequentially, which management attributed to memory constraints and component shortages hitting both Android and iOS handsets, plus the migration of system-in-package work to Vietnam. Automotive and industrial set a record, up 37% year over year.

"I think the pricing environment in general is still attractive related to being able to pass costs along and think about long-term investment and how we're managing that margin profile to make sure the return is there," chief executive Kevin Engel told investors on October 26. Amkor guided December-quarter sales to $1.9-2.0bn and gross margin to 18.0-19.0%, holding the gain rather than giving it back, and disclosed top-ten customer concentration easing to 66% of sales from 73%.

ASE's assembly, test and materials segment turned over TWD 151.5bn in the quarter, up 51%, on a 30.6% gross margin, eight points better than a year earlier. The electronics-manufacturing arm grew volumes 35% but ran an 8.3% gross margin, down from 8.9%, squeezed by component cost pass-throughs. The October monthly print separates the two cleanly: packaging and test revenue up 7.9% sequentially to NT$56.84bn, consolidated revenue up 0.6%, implying the remainder fell about a tenth month on month. "For 2027, we're expecting to remain as we last projected to double our LEAP revenue for the year," chief financial officer Joseph Tung said on October 29, referring to the leading-edge packaging line now guided above $4bn for 2026. Utilization is at or near 100%, with physical space named as the binding constraint.

The foundry-insourcing worry also runs backwards. TSMC has been expanding its outsourcing of the chip-on-wafer step to packagers, with the 2027 shortfall still estimated above 30% of demand. Substrate inflation is real — Ajinomoto raised build-up film prices about 30% from the third quarter — but it surfaced in ASE's low-margin manufacturing arm, not its packaging fee.

The bill, not the fee

Amkor fell 14.3% in the month to November 25 and ASE 27.3%, in a window when Nvidia fell 30.2%, Micron 30.3% and Teradyne 31.0% against 6.6% for the S&P 500 tracker. Two sessions carried most of it, including November 20, when the Nasdaq Composite dropped 5.28% and erased its year.

Earnings explain none of the de-rating and cash explains most of it. Amkor trades at 12.6x trailing earnings and 12.5x forward, against the 44.4x trailing recorded in May, with 5.9x trailing earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization and consensus still looking for 13.3% revenue growth in 2027. Its trailing free-cash-flow yield is negative 2.0% on roughly $2.5bn of 2026 capital spending, with an Arizona framework of $1.8bn for 2027; roughly $3bn of customer advance payments is expected to cover that without new debt. ASE compressed from 52.6x trailing earnings to 27.8x while raising 2026 capital spending to $11.5bn from $10.5bn, at negative 4.2% free-cash-flow yield and 66% net debt-to-equity.

The split between them is contracted work. ASE's packaging subsidiary Siliconware has Nvidia funding a tripling of dedicated capacity by mid-2029; Amkor was brought in as a third 2.5-dimensional supplier at engineering-run stage, with no meaningful volume expected in 2027. That is why ASE has doubled over twelve months and Amkor sits 6.7% below where it traded a year ago, two-thirds beneath its high, even as both post record packaging margins.

Amkor's Arizona Phase 1 completes late 2027 and Phase 2 breaks ground at the same time, a combined $12bn commitment. Until those buildings hold tools that bill, the advance payments on the balance sheet are the only evidence that the capacity will be wanted.

Sanmina's annual report for the fiscal year ended 3 October contains a sentence that decides who pays for a hyperscaler's change of mind. Customers are typically liable for the cost of materials, tariffs and components the contract manufacturer has ordered to meet the customer's own forecast and not used, provided the purchase followed an agreed procurement plan. No minimum purchase is promised; the parts bill, if the forecast moves, is the customer's.

That clause matters now because the markdown in contract electronics has been read as proof that assembling cloud racks is thin-margin work that swallows working capital. On margin the filings say the opposite. The working-capital half of the charge is real, but it lands at one of these two companies and not the other, and the reason is contractual.

The parts bill that stays with the customer

Sanmina, which builds components, subassemblies and complete systems for industrial, healthcare, defense, telecom and cloud customers, grew fiscal 2026 revenue 78.7% to $14.53bn after buying ZT Systems' data-center manufacturing business from an AMD subsidiary for $1.62bn on 27 October 2025. Inventories rose 81.5% to $3.61bn — a shade faster than sales, leaving derived days of supply roughly flat near 100. Receivables grew more slowly than revenue and cash rose to $1.68bn.

"Revenue grew 79% year-over-year to $14.53 billion. Non-GAAP operating margin expanded from 5.7% operating margin to 7.4% operating margin year-over-year," chairman and chief executive Jure Sola told investors on 9 November. The incremental gross margin on the extra $6.40bn of revenue was 10.6%, above the 9.60% group level. Guidance for fiscal 2027 is $17.5bn-$19.5bn with a 7.0%-7.5% operating margin.

The cost is concentration: two customers at 16.7% and 10.1% of sales, the ten largest at 63% against 52% a year earlier. At $129.91 the shares fetch 4.91x trailing gross profit, against 11.55x in early May, and 7.9x the midpoint of the company's own fiscal 2027 earnings guide.

Jabil holds the memory itself

Jabil, whose plants cover cloud infrastructure, semiconductor capital equipment, automotive, healthcare and packaging, grew revenue 20.6% to $35.95bn with incremental gross margin of 10.9% against a 9.23% group figure and incremental operating margin of 8.5% against 4.74%. Its fiscal 2027 guide is $44.5bn of revenue and a 30 basis point rise in core operating margin to 6.1%, with artificial-intelligence revenue up 54% to about $22.1bn and five customers each above $1bn. The legacy book is not collapsing underneath it: fourth-quarter Regulated Industries revenue rose 9% to $3.4bn and Connected Living held near $1.4bn.

What changed is the balance sheet. Net inventories reached $7.41bn from $4.68bn, a 58.4% rise against 20.6% revenue growth, which lifts implied days of supply from roughly 63 to 83. Some of that is price, not quantity: the contract price of a 16-gigabit DDR5 memory chip went from $8.70 in October 2025 to $48 by September 2026, so parts dollars can swell without a single extra box. Jabil discloses one customer at 20% of revenue and its five largest at 37%. The stock trades at 6.70x trailing gross profit against 12.77x in mid-May.

The peer with no racks fell too

Flex told investors its cloud business is steering away from low-margin standard racks, and reported inventory up 39% with customers participating in the builds. Celestica, contesting the same AMD rack-scale programme from the switch layer, guided 2027 revenue up about 90% to roughly $40bn while cutting 2026 free cash flow to $460m from $600m and calling next year a net use of cash; it trades at 11.92x trailing gross profit.

Plexus settles the argument. It serves healthcare, industrial, aerospace and communications with essentially no hyperscaler rack work, grew revenue 23% to $4.97bn, raised its operating-margin target to 6.5%-7.0% and posted a record 62-day cash conversion cycle, adding $1.5bn of annualized revenue on $250m of working capital. "We took a significant amount of share particularly through the last downturn within the semi cap market," chief executive Todd Kelsey said on 29 October. The shares still fell 13.6% over the following month, and 28.8% from the late-September high, while holding a valuation of 10.75x trailing gross profit — double Sanmina's.

So the per-unit economics test fails. Both protagonists booked the hyperscaler dollar at or above group margin, both raised guidance, and the multiple on gross profit roughly halved at each while gross profit dollars grew 95% at Sanmina and 25% at Jabil. The likelier reading is a re-pricing of how the buildout gets funded, visible across the whole complex: Dell raised its forecast for the third time this year and fell 7.2%. "Investors are focused on the big and rising cost of building AI, so good earnings and impressive earnings forecasts no longer lift sentiment," Swissquote Bank's Ipek Ozkardeskaya said on 25 November.

One open question survives the exercise, and it is Jabil's December quarter. A contract manufacturer holding eighty-odd days of increasingly expensive memory is making a bet on its customers' forecasts that Sanmina, by contract, does not have to make.

Rigetti Computing told investors it ended September with roughly $49.7m of cash. That matters because the month's markdown has been read as a financing event: companies that pay for their research by selling stock, cut off when the stock stops cooperating. On the evidence the financing is already done. IonQ, D-Wave and Rigetti hold about $4.7bn between them, summing the three balance sheets, and none of it is borrowed.

The treasuries, and how long they last

The quarter split; the shares did not

IonQ's revenue reached $173.8m, up 336%, and management raised full-year guidance to $505–515m from $450–460m, with remaining performance obligations of $1.2bn. The quality is thinner than the growth: gross margin fell to 21.9% from 48.0% a year earlier, because SkyWater Technology, the foundry it bought on 31 July for about $1.94bn, supplied $83.5m of the total.

The other two went backwards. The one nobody financed with retail stock

Quantinuum, the Honeywell-founded trapped-ion company, listed on Nasdaq in June at $60.00, raising $1.68bn from an upsized, 20-times-oversubscribed book. It holds $1.9bn, booked $98m in the third quarter and grew revenue 233% to $9.05m at a 74.6% non-GAAP gross margin. On 10 November D-Wave's chief executive Alan Baratz told investors: "As of today, we are not aware of any quantum computing company that has produced even 1 logical qubit that meets that fault tolerance standard." Then on 20 November the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.28% and Nvidia 14% as the artificial-intelligence trade unwound.

What is left to explain

Runway explains none of it. Results explain part: D-Wave's falling bookings and Rigetti's shrinking quarter are real deterioration, and D-Wave at 69.8x forward sales and Rigetti at 129x were priced for neither. IonQ's 18.9x forward sales looks like the cheap one until the margin mix is applied, at which point it is 66.6x forward gross profit, and its headline 31.5x trailing earnings is an artifact of warrant fair-value gains rather than operations. Quantinuum, at 250x forward sales, is dearest of the four and has the best bookings.

What the four have in common is that a great deal of new quantum equity reached the market in 2026 — the June listing, IQM's July Nasdaq debut, Infleqtion's, Xanadu's plan, IonQ's $2bn — while the chief executives conceded there is no demonstrated advantage yet. Without flow data the likelier reading is that more paper met a shrinking appetite for unproven duration, inside a broad de-risking of anything sold on 2030 cash flows.

IonQ's Niccolo de Masi told investors on 5 November: "I do not see headwinds, and I have not seen headwinds the last 2 years." Kulkarni, five days later, put his own company about three years from quantum advantage. Both can be right, and the companies have bought themselves enough money to find out which.

Universal Display Corporation, which licenses the patents behind phosphorescent OLED screens and sells the emissive materials panel makers evaporate into them, reported on 28 October that royalty and license fees rose to $75m in the September quarter while the materials business went the other way, to $80m from $83m a year earlier. Green emitter sales fell to $61m from $65m, red rose to $19m from $17m, and gross margin on materials was 50% for a second consecutive quarter against 73% for the company as a whole, down from 75% a year earlier.

Backing out the catch-up adjustments

Both lines carried one-offs. Material sales absorbed a $4.8m unfavorable catch-up; the prior-year royalty line had been reduced by a $9.5m out-of-period charge. Add both back and material sales were roughly $84.8m, about 2% above last year, against royalty of $75m versus $62.5m, a fifth higher. Taking royalty as the proxy for area shipped, the implied realization on each unit of emitter fell by something in the mid teens. That is a derivation from the disclosed figures, not a reported metric, but management's own ratio moves the same way: material to royalty is now 1.1:1, against roughly 1.6:1 a year ago, and the full-year expectation for that ratio was reset on mix.

Guidance agrees with the composition. Full-year revenue is tracking the low end of $630-670m and gross margin the low end of 74-76%, with higher input costs and memory pricing flagged as 2027 pressures. Consensus has 2026 revenue down 3.3% to $629.6m and earnings per share down 10.1% to $4.44.

The volume case is real; the chemistry case now has a date

Area growth is the part working. "Foldables are becoming one of the industry's most compelling growth stories," chief executive Steven Abramson told investors on 28 October, and the forecasts he leans on are area forecasts: Omdia sees foldable shipments of 45m units by 2030 from 18m in 2025, with foldables carrying two to three times the square inches of a conventional phone.

That is an alternative architecture for the blue layer, the layer Universal Display still does not supply commercially; its discovery platform has evaluated some 50m candidate molecules, and timing remains dependent on customer road maps.

Corning Incorporated, which makes optical fiber and cable assemblies alongside display glass, reported optical communications sales up 44% to $2.4bn with segment net income up 80% to $532m, guided the December quarter to sales up 19%, and took $639m of customer deposits in nine months to pre-fund the capacity. Its soft lines were glass, where notebook and handheld units fell 20% and television units 4% on device bills of materials inflated by memory prices. The shares fell 17.6% in 30 days and are 60% below their June high, yet remain 22.8% higher over twelve months, with ten sessions of 5% or more since 22 October, including a near-11% drop on 20 November in a technology rout and a 14.8% rebound on 23 November, neither on company news. At 40.8x trailing and 30.2x forward earnings, against 76.3x trailing in August, the de-rating is a change in what buyers will pay, with no order reported deferred.

The shelf's 30-day markdown does not describe Universal Display: it fell 6.5% against the S&P 500 exchange-traded fund's 5.4%, so November was market weather. The 30.5% loss over twelve months is the part its own numbers speak to, and they half-support it. Royalty growth confirms that panel makers are running more area through their lines; the material book shows that area converting into less revenue per unit, at half the company gross margin, with input costs rising into next year.

What the arithmetic cannot settle is price. Universal Display is the sole commercial source of phosphorescent red and green emitters, and its customers have just put money behind someone else's path to blue. The renewal that tests whether that matters falls due at the end of 2027, and the party holding the option is the customer.

MongoDB's Customer Commitments Grew Three Times Faster Than Its Revenue

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The standing objection to selling a database by the hour is that no customer has promised to keep the meter running. MongoDB's own filing says otherwise: contracted obligations reached $1.52bn at the end of July, up 91% against revenue growth of 30%, and the artificial-intelligence startups that could switch off abruptly are 3% to 5% of its cloud revenue.

Teradata, which sells the opposite model, is converting an on-premise subscription base into cloud contracts. Cloud annual recurring revenue added about $67m over the year while the non-cloud base shed about $45m, which is why total recurring revenue compounds at 1% to 2% while the cloud line compounds at 11%.

Both businesses firmed up. Only Teradata's shares did, up 32% in 90 days while MongoDB fell 24% through a September guidance raise, a founder's return as interim chief and a market-wide software selloff.

The worry about selling a database by consumption is that nobody has promised to keep using it. MongoDB, which sells a document database both as a licensed server and as the Atlas managed cloud service, answered the objection in its filing for the quarter ended 31 July 2026: remaining performance obligations, the revenue it has contracted but not yet delivered, reached $1,519.2m, up from $794.2m a year earlier. Revenue over the same stretch grew 30%.

That ratio is the whole question for a metered software business, and the market has been trading the other side of it. MongoDB closed at $308.50 on 24 November, down roughly 24% over 90 sessions, on a reading that consumption revenue is the most fragile kind, invoiced only as workloads actually run and cancellable without breaching anything. The disclosures describe something else: a business whose promises are growing about three times faster than its meter.

What backs 73 cents of every MongoDB dollar

Atlas was $565.9m of the $771.8m quarterly total, so roughly three of every four dollars MongoDB books is metered cloud consumption. Atlas grew about 29% and the licence line about 36%, and gross margin widened to 73.8% while free cash flow nearly doubled to $137.6m. Reported operating income swung from a $65.3m loss a year earlier to $28.4m of profit.

The company's 10-Q is candid that timing on that $1.52bn "are generally dependent upon customers' future consumption, which is inherently variable at the customers' discretion." The customers most capable of switching off are a smaller slice than assumed: at its 29 September investor day MongoDB said artificial-intelligence-native customers are 3% to 5% of Atlas revenue, and raised three-year targets to above 20% revenue growth with 80-100% free-cash-flow conversion. "Atlas is completely consumption-driven, as you are fully well aware," then-chief executive CJ Desai told analysts on the Q2 call. Four weeks later he left.

The honest offset is the near term. Full-year guidance was raised to $2.99-3.03bn with Atlas growth lifted to about 27%, but the third-quarter guide of $756-761m implies roughly 20-21% growth and a sequential decline. Acceleration is guided to stop.

Teradata's migration clears its churn by $22m

Teradata sells the inverse: Vantage, a multi-cloud analytics platform bought by banks, governments and telecoms, historically installed on their own hardware. Its unit of revenue is a subscription being moved, not a workload being metered. Total annual recurring revenue was $1.512bn at 30 September, up 1% as reported and 2% in constant currency, with public-cloud recurring revenue of $700m against $633m — cloud now 46% of the base.

Subtracting gives the mechanism. Cloud added about $67m over the year; the residual non-cloud base fell about $45m, or roughly 5%. Migration is outrunning erosion by something near $22m, which is precisely why total recurring revenue compounds at 1-2% while the cloud line compounds at 11%. Quarterly revenue still fell 4.1% to $399m and gross margin narrowed to 59.6%.

What the cash did was different. Year-to-date adjusted free cash flow rose 90% to $254m, full-year earnings guidance went to $2.86-2.90 from a $2.60 midpoint at the start of the year, and adjusted free cash flow was reaffirmed at $330-350m with the midpoint lifted to $340m. "Our hybrid and sovereign capabilities have an unmatched advantage in the marketplace and we remain really confident in our annual outlook," chief executive Stephen McMillan said on 5 November.

How much of the fall the filings explain

For Teradata, cash delivery has set the price. The shares fell 6% to $31.80 the session after the print because revenue missed a $403.66m consensus, then recovered to $35.71, up 32% over 90 sessions at 3.3x forward gross profit and 12.4x forward earnings, a roughly 10% free-cash-flow yield on the guided midpoint.

For MongoDB almost none of the decline is in the filings. Two dated sessions carry it: a 13.5% fall on 2 September, the day after a quarter that grew 30% and raised the guide, and 18.2% on 28 September, when Desai resigned for Meta after about ten months and founder Dev Ittycheria returned as interim chief with third-quarter and full-year guidance expressly reaffirmed. Mizuho and Stifel both kept their ratings. The November leg is harder to pin on the company at all: capital left high-multiple software broadly and selling intensified from 20 November, with the Meritech software index down about 10% over the same stretch. MongoDB's forward price-to-gross-profit of 11.3x now sits below both anchors in its own year, 13.3x in late July and 11.7x in early May, though 47x forward earnings is not cheap in absolute terms.

So two database businesses firmed up on different mechanics, contracted commitments at one and migration arithmetic at the other, and only one of them was paid for it. A governance shock explains a day; it does not explain a quarter of the market value of a company whose obligations nearly doubled.

The arbiter arrives on 1 December, when MongoDB reports the quarter ended 31 October after the close. The guide it set is a deceleration; whether the backlog behind it keeps outrunning the meter is the only line in that release that settles anything.
