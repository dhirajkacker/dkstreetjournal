TTM Technologies Doubled Its Near-Term Order Book While Its Shares Lost Two-Thirds

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A circuit-board fabricator whose revenue growth accelerated in every quarter of 2026 has been priced since June as though its AI orders were being withdrawn. TTM Technologies' third-quarter revenue rose 60% to $1.2bn, gross margin widened to 23.6% from 20.8% two quarters earlier, aerospace-and-defense program backlog hit a record $2.0bn, and the company added $400m of capital spending on top of $1.3bn already planned.

The only disclosed setback is one data-center program delayed on a customer's schedule. The honest negatives are financial, not commercial: net leverage of 2.8x in September after $1.1bn and $650m of cash acquisitions, and effectively no free cash flow while the plants go up.

Plexus, whose data-center work is a half-billion-dollar funnel rather than revenue, fell on a note about chips it does not build and still costs more per dollar of gross profit than TTM does.

TTM Technologies, which fabricates the printed circuit boards that sit underneath other companies' AI servers and inside American weapons programs, committed an additional $400m of capital spending in October on top of $1.3bn already planned through 2029. The money goes into four plants in China and the 800,000-square-foot Penang plant in Malaysia, built brownfield for rapid ramp, with revenue expected around 2028.

Since late June the printed-circuit and contract-assembly shelf has been marked as though the AI order book were being withdrawn. TTM's own disclosures run the other way. Revenue growth accelerated through every quarter of 2026, from 30% year on year in the first to 60% in the third; the 90-day backlog stood at $1.3bn at the October call, up 111%; and full-year guidance was raised to $4.47–4.49bn. What has been revised down is the price on those earnings, not the earnings.

The shares closed at $69.20 on 23 November against $210.57 on 25 June, a 67% fall that leaves them up only 7% over twelve months. A quarter of that came on 22 October, earnings day, when the stock fell 25% close to close and took the shelf with it: Celestica dropped 11.5%, Flex 12.4%, Sanmina 9.8%. Garmin, which shares the vendor industry label and nothing else, rose.

What the October call actually disclosed

Data center and networking was 41% of third-quarter sales and grew 115%. Inside it sits the product that explains the capital spending: N+M asymmetrical-layer boards, roughly four times the complexity of conventional ones and carrying gross margins well above the corporate average. "It is currently about 30% of the data center and networking activities, and it will grow next year to about 50% of that group," chief executive Edwin Roks told investors on 22 October.

Defense is the other half of the business and the harder one to displace. Aerospace and defense was 34% of third-quarter sales, grew 21%, and carries a record $2.0bn program backlog against $1.5bn a year earlier, on a book-to-bill of 1.6x that gives management 65% visibility into 2027 revenue. TTM describes itself as America's largest domestic manufacturer of advanced printed circuit boards, with content on more than 480 defense programs under ITAR-compliant operations. The Syracuse, New York plant serving that demand began its ultra-HDI ramp in the third quarter and targets full capacity in 2028, with no disclosed slip.

One program did slip. A single large N+M job moved on the customer's schedule, pushing fourth-quarter data-center growth guidance to about 150% year on year where Needham had modeled 195%; the firm cut its target to $150 from $175 and kept its Buy. Copper-clad laminate and T-glass lead times are running 12 to 24 weeks, a constraint on panels built rather than panels wanted.

The negatives are on the balance sheet

TTM entered October having spent $1.1bn in cash on Epiq Solutions and then announced a $650m all-cash purchase of Ophir Optics, an infrared and laser-optics maker, three days before the print. Net leverage was 2.8x at the end of September, and trailing free-cash-flow yield is 0.20% — the plants are consuming the cash the income statement generates. "This will be the end of the big stuff for a while," chief financial officer Daniel Boehle said on the same call, promising to get leverage back down.

That is the case against the stock, and it is a financing case. On earnings, the market is paying 8.80x trailing gross profit against roughly 35x in late June, a de-rating of about three-quarters over five months in which trailing gross profit grew 30%. Forward earnings are 13.9x on consensus of $4.96 a share, against consensus net income growth above 100%.

Plexus is marked for an exposure it does not have

Plexus, a fee-for-service manufacturer in Neenah, Wisconsin that builds medical, industrial and defense hardware for other companies' brands, fell 9.0% on 20 November after Ming-Chi Kuo of TF International reported that Google had cut planned 2027 shipments of Broadcom-designed accelerators by about a million units. Plexus does not build those. Its data-center work is an emerging funnel of conservatively about $0.5bn in power and thermal management, with two announced programs. Its revenue came from industrial, up 27.4% in fiscal 2026, defense up 23.0% and healthcare up 18.6%. The manufacturing funnel set a record at $4.9bn, up 33%, and the cash conversion cycle fell to 62 days. Yet at 10.75x trailing gross profit Plexus is dearer than TTM on the same measure, with forward earnings at 18.8x against roughly 20% growth and a trailing free-cash-flow yield of minus 0.54%.

How much the results explain

Almost none of TTM's two-thirds decline is explained by orders, and the part that is explained is a timing miss on one program worth about 10% of a single quarter's data-center line. What the results do not settle is whether a company at 2.8x leverage, generating no free cash, can fund $1.7bn of capacity and close three acquisitions without returning to the debt market — the one thing a 47% pre-guided revenue increase for 2027 depends on. Plexus's markdown has a simpler cause: it was sold by association on a report about silicon it never touches, which is why its drawdown is shallower and its de-rating milder.

The constraint TTM keeps describing is laminate and glass, measured in weeks of lead time. Nothing in the October disclosures suggests anyone has stopped asking for the panels.

AI Buyers Took the Packaging Lines Microchip Rents, and Its Best Orders Go Unshipped

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The analog shelf was sold through the autumn as a spent restocking trade in cars and factory equipment. The channel data says restocking never happened: Microchip's distributors held 25 days of inventory at the end of September, down from 26 in March, and Analog Devices' channel sits below its own six-to-seven-week target. What changed is supply. Foundries and assembly-and-test houses are steering mature-node and packaging capacity toward data-center customers, and Microchip's lead times have stretched from 4–8 weeks to 6–32.

Automotive is the slowest line at all three companies; industrial and data center carry the growth. Microchip grew revenue 42% year on year in September and guided December down about 1% sequentially because of orders it cannot ship. Its price-to-gross-profit has gone from 20.1x in May to 9.4x; Analog Devices, whose shares agree with its numbers, still trades at 19.9x.

The constraint on Microchip Technology's December quarter is not an order book that stopped growing. It is assembly and test capacity that data-center chipmakers rented out from underneath it.

That matters because the whole analog shelf has been traded since May as an automotive and industrial restocking cycle that had run its course, with the rollover in share prices read as evidence that distributors had finished refilling and orders would follow them down. The disclosures from Microchip, NXP Semiconductors and Analog Devices say restocking never arrived in the first place, and that the binding problem now is on the supply side of mature-node silicon, where little capacity has been added for years.

Lead times quadrupled at the top end

Microchip, the Chandler, Arizona maker of microcontrollers, analog and timing parts for industrial, automotive, computing and aerospace customers, reported 25 days of inventory in its distribution channel at 30 September, down from 26 days in March. Over the same stretch its lead times went from the 4–8 weeks that had prevailed to 6–32 weeks. Its fiscal 2027 risk disclosure states plainly that certain suppliers have notified plans to discontinue legacy manufacturing processes as industry capacity is increasingly directed toward data centers, with no assurance that necessary foundry capacity will be available on acceptable terms.

Chief executive Steve Sanghi told investors on 5 November that the company has "substantial unsupported backlog on high-margin products that our supply chain cannot fulfill", and that capacity at major outsourced assembly and test suppliers had been displaced by AI companies. Customers are not hoarding against it. "We can hardly get them to place orders with a new lead time," Sanghi said on the same call.

NXP, the Eindhoven supplier of microcontrollers, connectivity and processors to carmakers and industrial customers, ended its September quarter with distribution channel inventory at 11 weeks, its stated long-term target, while 80% of its portfolio now carries lead times beyond 16 weeks against 25% a year earlier. Its channel sell-in grew slower than its direct shipments: distributor revenue rose 8.3% sequentially to $2,244m against 8.9% for direct customers. Chief financial officer William Betts said on 27 October that "the book to bill remains well above 1 across all end markets and regions," and chief executive Rafael Sotomayor denied seeing broad-based restocking. Analog Devices' channel weeks ran below its 6-to-7-week target in the August quarter, with book-to-bill above 1 but "not in that sort of unusually high space".

Cars are the slow line, not the engine

The restocking framing also had the wrong end market. Analog Devices' industrial revenue grew 53% year on year to $1.97bn in the August quarter and communications 84%, while automotive grew 16%. NXP's Industrial and Internet of Things line rose 39% and communications infrastructure 54%, against 13% for automotive in its first $2bn automotive quarter. Microchip's industrial sales rose 50.3% and data center 55.7%; automotive, at 19.3%, was its slowest. Vehicle output is not the culprit: S&P Global Mobility's October forecast raised 2027 production for Europe by 68,000 units and Greater China by 258,000.

One order book, three prices

All three businesses are accelerating. Microchip has lifted year-on-year revenue growth for four straight quarters to 42.2%, gross margin by 10.0 points to 65.9% and operating margin from 12.8% to 28.6%. Its December guide of -3% to +1% sequentially, with gross margin stepping to 64–65%, is the cost of parts it cannot get and of $53m of one-time items that will not repeat; management said the March-quarter backlog is better than December's was at the same point.

The shares disagree. Over six months Microchip is down 37.3%, NXP 22.7% and Analog Devices 8.1%, and the week to 20 November took roughly 17% out of the PHLX Semiconductor Index before chip stocks rebounded on 23 November after Nvidia reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive.

Microchip's price-to-gross-profit has fallen from 20.1x in May to 9.4x now while gross profit grew 67.4%, and its 45.4x trailing earnings become 16.8x forward. NXP is cheaper still at 8.2x trailing gross profit, with a 5.7% free-cash-flow yield and a $6.3bn buyback authorization worth about a tenth of its market value; Microchip, at 2.36x net debt to EBITDA, cannot buy its own de-rated stock. Analog Devices is the one whose price tracks its results, and it has quietly got cheaper doing so, at 19.9x trailing gross profit against 25.7x in May.

So the split inside this shelf is about what investors will pay, not what the businesses are earning. What would break the thesis is visible and countable: distributor weeks climbing above target, or book-to-bill slipping under 1. What would confirm it is duller, and is already partly disclosed, which is backlog shifting from December into March as capacity frees up.

Microchip is absorbing the foundry and assembly price increases that start in January rather than passing them on. "We don't want to push the luck," Sanghi said. A company that is holding prices down while turning away high-margin orders is not describing a demand problem.

Medicare Priced Axogen's Nerve Graft at $8,965 and Left Inspire's Implant Without a Code

Three companies sitting on the same nerve-and-robotics shelf are paid in three different units, and the unit explains why they went opposite ways. Procept BioRobotics collects a single-use handpiece on every prostate treatment; Axogen is paid per nerve repair on a product the Food and Drug Administration licensed as a biologic in December 2025; Inspire Medical is paid once per sleep-apnea patient implanted.

Procept's Q3 procedures rose 22% to more than 13,400 with gross margin at 66.3% against 60.6% four quarters earlier, and Axogen raised 2026 guidance to at least 29% growth. Inspire's revenue fell 7.9% because providers lost the billing code used for roughly 10,000 Medicare placements, a $120m hole; its dedicated replacement code does not take effect until 2028. What one procedure pays turned out to be the whole argument in nerve and prostate devices this year. Procept BioRobotics, which places image-guided surgical robots in hospitals and sells a disposable handpiece for every Aquablation treatment of an enlarged prostate, now collects roughly $3,570 of handpiece revenue per case, up from about $3,200 a year earlier as its newer HYDROS platform displaced the older system and end-of-quarter bulk discounts were dropped.

That is one payment unit out of three on a shelf the market has been treating as a single re-rating story. Axogen is paid per nerve repair. Inspire Medical is paid once per patient, for life. Over the month to 23 November the first two rose 25% and 52%; Inspire fell 24%, and is down 36% over twelve months. The segment did not turn up together, and the reason is mechanical.

Procept's growth is coming out of installed robots, not new ones

Procept performed more than 13,400 aquablations in the third quarter, up 22% year over year, with handpiece revenue up 21% and the handpiece-to-procedure ratio at 106% against 98% the prior quarter as field inventory normalized. Revenue of $98.3m grew 18%, slower than the 37% of full-year 2025, but gross margin reached 66.3% against 60.6% in the fourth quarter of 2025.

Utilization, not capital placement, is doing the work. "In the third quarter, procedure volume at HYDROS accounts was more than 35% higher than legacy AQUABEAM accounts," chief executive Larry Wood told investors on 4 November, attributing it to surgeon migration between the two platforms. Capital pricing held at about $495,000 for a new system and $315,000 for a replacement, and management reaffirmed roughly 54,000 procedures for the year. Aquablation is still the challenger in its market: transurethral resection accounted for 53.7% of US procedures for benign prostatic hyperplasia in 2025 against Aquablation's 15.6%. The shares trade at 3.65x forward sales and 5.65x forward gross profit, against 4.09x and 6.33x trailing, and remain 74.8% below their December 2024 peak; Truist raised its target to $24 while keeping a Hold, and consensus sits essentially at spot.

Axogen's step-up was a change in what the product legally is

Axogen sells Avance, a processed human nerve allograft that bridges a severed nerve without harvesting a donor nerve from the patient. On 3 December 2025 the Food and Drug Administration approved its Biologics License Application, moving the graft from human-tissue classification to a licensed biologic. The payment followed: Medicare set 2026 facility reimbursement at $8,965 for hospital outpatient and $6,157 for ambulatory surgery centers, and commercial coverage reached about 96% of US lives after Elevance removed its nerve-gap-length criterion in August.

Third-quarter revenue of $75.8m grew 26.2%, guidance went to at least 29% growth and $290m, and breast reconstruction, now 64% of revenue, accelerated past 60% growth at roughly 11% penetration of eligible cases. Gross margin fell 180 basis points to 74.8% on costlier long-length production, the one line going the wrong way. The move itself is dated: shares jumped on the 28 October beat, with adjusted earnings of $0.21 against $0.13 expected, then climbed again after a 9 November presentation putting a $754m market behind prostate nerve reconstruction at about $7,000 per procedure, none of it in guidance. At 12.0x forward sales and 105x forward earnings, the price already discounts two more years near 30% growth.

Inspire sells the best gross margin and cannot get it billed

Inspire's hypoglossal-nerve stimulator for obstructive sleep apnea carries an 86% gross margin and is implanted once. From the first quarter of 2026 providers could no longer use the billing code previously applied to roughly 10,000 Medicare placements of Inspire V, costing about $30m of third-quarter revenue and $120m for the year. Revenue fell 7.9% and operating margin swung from positive 4.3% to minus 13.9%.

A dedicated permanent code exists on paper. "This dedicated Category I code is set to take effect on January 1, 2028, and stands to meaningfully simplify coding and reimbursement, thereby expanding access to Inspire therapy," chairman and chief executive Tim Herbert said on 2 November. The valuation process that sets the physician fee begins with a committee panel in January 2027, and management conceded the shorter Inspire V procedure could be valued lower. Chief financial officer Matt Osberg guided to "low single-digit year-over-year revenue growth for the full year 2027." The market has taken him at his word and then some: forward price-to-sales of 1.82x sits above the 1.76x trailing, which is a bet on another decline, even as the company holds $453.1m of cash, no debt and the cheapest gross-profit multiple of the three at 2.05x trailing. Barclays cut its target to $62 at underweight on 3 November and the shares have fallen every week since.

How much of this the numbers account for

For Procept and Axogen, most of it. Both moves rest on disclosed procedure counts and a raise, and in Axogen's case on a reimbursement schedule with dates attached. For Inspire the de-rating is also earned, by a coding gap that is arithmetic rather than demand, which is why its 86% margin and free cash flow sit awkwardly beside a price that assumes shrinkage through 2027. What the shelf does not have is one shared mechanism: the category average was also lifted by LivaNova and Integra LifeSciences on their own quarters, inside a November rotation out of artificial-intelligence equities into healthcare.

Axogen closes at the highest level in its record on a market it will not begin developing until 2027, and the next hard numbers for it land at a March 2027 investor day. Inspire's own fix arrives on a calendar nobody can accelerate, and the company has to fund a sales organization through more than a year of temporary billing to reach it.

The price of sending a text message in the United States went up three times this year, and the two companies best known for sending them did not get any of the increase.

Twilio and Bandwidth both reported the September quarter on 29 October, both raised full-year guidance on every metric, and both gave preliminary 2027 outlooks above their own prior frameworks. Since then Twilio has fallen 38.1% from the $301.27 it closed at on 1 October and Bandwidth 50.0% from its 9 July high of $78.44. The markdown reads as a verdict that AI agents will route around the messaging and voice rails before the rails get paid for them. The published rate cards say something narrower: the meter is intact, it is still being repriced upward, and the increases are accruing to the phone carriers.

Who gets paid on a Verizon text

Twilio lists a standard outbound American SMS segment at $0.0083 per 160 characters, the same across long codes, toll-free numbers and short codes, with carrier fees applied automatically on top. Since 1 October the terminating carrier's cut on Verizon has been $0.0050, an 11.1% rise. T-Mobile went from $0.0030 to $0.0045 on 19 January, a 50% increase, and AT&T to $0.0035 in April; on 1 November the surcharge stepped to $0.005 on six further networks. Add the two published rates and a Verizon-terminated message costs its buyer about $0.0133, of which roughly 38% never stops at Twilio. Twilio's own rate did not move.

Substitution changes the billed unit without removing it. A conversational Rich Communication Services session opens once a thread holds at least four rich messages with two genuine replies, and Verizon's session fee is $0.009, so since October a four-message exchange is cheaper billed as a session than as four messages.

Where the two part company is earned margin

Twilio's non-GAAP gross margin was 49.2% in the quarter, down 90 basis points, and chief financial officer Aidan Viggiano said it would have been up 80 without $53m of incremental US carrier fees. Messaging grew 24% as reported and 17% organically, with about seven of those points supplied by pass-through.

On that axis Bandwidth is the heavier case. Total revenue was a record $234m, up 22%, but cloud communications, its own measure of earned business, grew 12% to $158m, and messaging surcharge revenue rose $25m, or 51% out of a $42.1m total increase: 59% of the incremental dollars. GAAP gross margin fell 200 basis points to 36.2%, which management attributed solely to the surcharge line.

Strip it out and the comparison inverts. Bandwidth's non-GAAP gross margin rose 300 basis points to 60.6%, roughly 1,140 basis points above Twilio's by subtraction, on a network it owns and interconnects in about 70 countries. "SI is raising the value of that layer," chief executive David Morken said on the 29 October call of the network itself. "A legacy carrier connection cannot do that."

The unit that pays nine times more is barely metered

Twilio's ConversationRelay bills $0.07 per minute of active AI session on top of $0.0085 for an inbound local voice minute, so about $0.0785 against $0.0085 for a bare minute. That is the largest per-unit price step either company has. It is also almost empty. "The way that we characterize voice AI in our prepared remarks is that it's about 1% of what the voice interactions are so far," chief executive Khozema Shipchandler told analysts on 29 October, calling consumer AI "a fraction of that 1%." Neither company disclosed a dollar figure for AI voice revenue.

What the results account for, and what they do not

Some of the decline has fundamental content, all of it second-derivative. Twilio's organic messaging growth slipped from 18% to 17%, dollar-based net expansion from 116% to 114%, and fourth-quarter non-GAAP operating income is guided to $300–310m against $326m in the third, on higher hosting costs, which is what coverage blamed for a 19.2% two-day fall after a 12.2% earnings beat. HSBC's downgrade to Reduce, with a $211 target, argues the market already prices a generous share of future AI-agent volume into Twilio. None of that is a volume decline, a price cut or a named customer loss, and the surcharge steps this autumn ran the other way.

The rest is a rating change imposed from outside, arriving in a week when the S&P 500 confirmed a correction on 20 November on doubts about AI spending durability, and both names bounced with the complex on 23 November, Twilio 8.3% to $186.46 and Bandwidth 14.6% to $39.25. Twilio now trades at 9.8x forward gross profit against the 13.0x on the Desk's record in August, while gross profit grew 20%. Bandwidth is at 3.8x trailing gross profit against 6.5x in May, a 42% compression while GAAP gross profit grew 14.9%, and its balance-sheet risk has gone the wrong way for the bears: all but about $7m of the 2028 convertible notes is retired and net leverage is 1.3x.

The asymmetry is in who holds the pen on price. Twilio and Bandwidth set what a message and a minute earn them and have held those rates flat; the carriers reset their cut whenever they choose, and chose four times in eleven months. Whatever an AI agent ends up paying for access to a phone number, the surcharge on the text it sends will have moved again first.

Ceva, which licenses signal-processing, artificial-intelligence and wireless connectivity designs to chipmakers and collects a royalty each time a licensee's chip ships, told investors on 9 November that its customers shipped 623 million Ceva-powered devices in the September quarter, matching its all-time quarterly record, with record cellular internet-of-things and record Wi-Fi 6 volumes inside it. Its shares are down 40% from their 26 May high.

The drawdown across this group has been read as the physical demand for edge-inference silicon turning down. The disclosures say something narrower: unit volumes are at or near records at the two companies that have reported since the decline started, and the single business that is actually decelerating blames a shortage of memory in its customers' bills of materials.

Record units, stalled revenue

Ceva's quarter is the awkward one to read. Royalty revenue of $12.7m rose 18% sequentially on about 10% more units, so revenue per device improved — Wi-Fi 6 reached 57% of its Wi-Fi volume from 24% a year earlier, lifting the royalty per chip even as total Wi-Fi units fell 6%. Group revenue still grew just 2.4%, to $29.1m, with gross margin at 85.4% against 88.0%. A multi-year connectivity licence slipped out of the quarter and was signed in early Q4, and the stock fell 13% on the print; management trimmed full-year growth to about 11% from 13-15%.

"AI licensing revenues reached a record, accounting for more than 35% of total licensing and related revenues," chief executive Amir Panush said on the 9 November call, with six of ten deals signed in the quarter AI-related. Chief financial officer Yaniv Arieli guided to $34m-$38m: "This would represent the highest quarterly revenues in CEVA's history."

The one deceleration, and it starts in DRAM allocation

Ambarella, which sells vision chips combining video compression with computer-vision inference to camera, automotive and robotics makers, is the case that fits the bear reading. Year-over-year growth has fallen through four quarters — 31.2%, 20.1%, 16.9%, 13.2% — and the September guidance of $115.0m-$124.0m implies about 10% at the midpoint. Gross margin held at 57.7% and the operating loss narrowed 63% to -$8.1m, so this is a volume story, not a pricing one.

Asked where guidance could break, chief executive Fermi Wang told investors on 3 September: "The downside is only one, it's memory." Suppliers would not commit November allocation to his customers. Ambarella neither buys nor resells memory, so there is no direct margin hit; the transmission runs through customers whose device costs rise as TrendForce projects conventional DRAM contract prices up 10-15% in the fourth quarter and NAND up 15-20%. Forward earnings now cost 68.4x against roughly 12% consensus growth, down from about 98x in July.

The name that never turned

Impinj sells radio-frequency identification endpoint chips for individual retail, logistics and food items, with no inference on the chip. Its September quarter set records on revenue, at $112.1m, and on endpoint chip revenue of $97.3m, up 24%, with record unit volume. "We met, but were unable to get ahead of, market need," chief executive Chris Diorio said on 28 October. It is the dearest of the three at 23.8x trailing gross profit, against Ambarella's 10.0x and Ceva's 7.0x, and it is the only one whose trend never turned negative — its shares are flat since late May.

Three pay structures, three outcomes

The shared markdown has a shared cause that is not demand: all three fell 9-12% on 20 November, a week in which the S&P 500 lost 8.6% and the Philadelphia semiconductor index about 17%, and all three bounced on 23 November. Underneath that, the split follows how each gets paid. Ceva earns pennies per unit after the design is done, so a record shipment quarter can sit beside flat revenue when one licence signature moves two weeks. Impinj is capacity-rationed at the back end of its own supply chain. Ambarella sells the chip into a finished device whose other components are being price-rationed in front of it.

That accounts for the Ceva and Impinj declines poorly and the Ambarella decline well. Growth settling near 11-13% on consensus is a different company from the one that grew 37% in fiscal 2026, and a lower multiple is rational work. What the prints do not support is the group reading.

Ambarella reports its October quarter after the close on 24 November, with the call at 1:30pm Pacific. It is the one data set on this shelf where the memory squeeze could show up as orders that never arrived.
