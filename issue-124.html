A TSMC Wafer Earns Almost Eight Times the Gross Profit of a GlobalFoundries Wafer

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Divide revenue by wafers shipped and the three big pure-play foundries stop looking like one industry. TSMC keeps about $7,000 of gross profit on each twelve-inch-equivalent wafer; GlobalFoundries keeps $882; United Microelectronics keeps roughly $655 on a blended selling price it discloses at about $1,900.

That ladder decides who pays for the next fab. TSMC's September-quarter operating cash flow covered its capital spending more than twice over and it has told customers prices rise 3-6% from January. GlobalFoundries expects roughly a third of about $4bn of 2027 net capital spending to come from customer prepayments, plus about $760m of US government grants. UMC is funding $2.0bn this year and a newly approved $3.2bn itself, and its finance chief says depreciation will press on gross margin for two more years.

None of the three cut capacity plans or had forward estimates cut into November's markdown.

TSMC collected roughly $10,500 of revenue on each twelve-inch-equivalent wafer it shipped in the September quarter. GlobalFoundries, which builds microcontrollers, radio-frequency modems and power-management chips on mature and specialty nodes out of Malta, New York, collected $2,935 on each 300mm-equivalent wafer. United Microelectronics, the Hsinchu pure-play that fabricates other companies' designs at 28nm and above, collected about $1,900 — its own disclosed blended average selling price.

November marked all three down almost as a single trade, on the belief that spending on artificial-intelligence capacity had peaked and that the companies owning the fabs would wear it. Their own quarters record the opposite sequence: revenue growth accelerated at all three through 2026, gross margins widened at all three, December quarters were guided higher and none of them trimmed capital spending. What separates them is not the demand they describe but who is paying for the capacity that serves it.

The ladder from $1,900 to $10,500 a wafer

TSMC's gap is mix. Nodes at 7nm and below were 79% of third-quarter wafer revenue, and high-performance computing rose to 68% of the total. Shipments grew 9.6% from a year earlier while revenue in New Taiwan dollars grew 50.9%, which works out to roughly 38% more revenue per wafer — the quarter was priced rather than shipped. At a 66.4% gross margin, about $7,000 of each wafer's revenue stayed as gross profit.

GlobalFoundries kept $882 a wafer on a 30.1% gross margin against 24.8% a year earlier, shipping 647,000 wafers with selling prices up 4.8%. "This marked the first quarter of year-over-year blended pricing improvement since 2023," chief financial officer Sam Franklin told investors on November 4. UMC kept about $655 a wafer, on a record 1.21m wafers and utilization of 92% against 85% in the June quarter; its blended price was flat quarter to quarter, with 22nm reaching 18.3% of wafer sales.

Cash flow, prepayments, balance sheet

TSMC pays for itself. Third-quarter operating cash flow of NT$919.8bn covered NT$443.2bn of capital expenditure 2.1 times, with full-year capex reiterated at $60-64bn and 2026-2028 spending described as significantly higher than the past three years. It is also raising price: customers have been told wafer prices go up 3-6% from January 2027, with 2nm and 3nm-class nodes taking the most. "The gap between demand and supply are still quite big, and that is why we put all our strengths to increase the capacity," chairman and chief executive C.C. Wei said on October 15.

GlobalFoundries shares the bill. "We now expect net CapEx investments of approximately $4 billion into our manufacturing footprint in 2027, while continuing to deliver our free cash generation targets," Franklin said; about a third of that is to be supported by customer prepayments for incremental capacity, alongside roughly $760m of CHIPS Act grant money and a 35% investment tax credit. Third-quarter free cash flow of $136m already included about $150m of prepayments and grants. Chief executive Tim Breen put the book behind it plainly on the same call: "We are absolutely booked out through 2027, increasingly booked out through 2028."

UMC carries its own. The $2.0bn 2026 cash capex budget sits on its balance sheet, as does a newly board-approved $3.2bn for silicon photonics, deep-trench capacitors and thin-film lithium niobate, with first installations late in 2028. "For gross margins for next two years at least, depreciation, along with the rising raw material cost, is going to put pressures on our overall financial gross margin," chief financial officer Chitung Liu said on October 28. Chief executive Jason Wang's answer is price: a blended increase of about 10% year over year beginning in the first quarter of 2027.

The markdown nobody's estimates followed

Through the thirty days to November 25, TSMC fell 19.3%, GlobalFoundries 19.6% and UMC 31.2%. Three-quarters of TSMC's decline landed in two sessions, November 5 and November 20, the latter the day Nvidia dropped about 14% as the artificial-intelligence trade broke down over returns on the data-center build-out. Nvidia itself was down 30.9% over the same window. The de-rating belonged to the whole complex.

What it left is three different price levels. TSMC trades at 17.3 times trailing gross profit, against 21.9x in August, and about 14.7 times FY2027 consensus earnings. GlobalFoundries is at 9.7 times trailing gross profit against 13.8x in August and 1.67x book, having touched a 52-week low near $34.05 on November 25 as brokers cut targets — Needham to $55 from $80, Wedbush to $45 from $72 — while consensus FY2027 earnings of $2.55 a share still imply 28% growth. UMC's headline 13.0 times trailing earnings is the least reliable number of the three: a NT$30.05bn investment gain in the June quarter, an equity-portfolio mark, accounts for roughly NT$11.9 of its NT$33.95 trailing earnings per share, which puts the core figure nearer 19-20x.

So the compression sits in the multiple, and the operating record under it is intact: TSMC's October revenue rose 59.7% year on year and UMC's 18.3%. The difference the per-wafer arithmetic exposes is protection rather than growth. TSMC sets price on capacity it funds from its own cash flow. GlobalFoundries' volume sits under long-term agreements carrying take-or-pay reservations and prepayments, including a five-year, $2bn silicon-interposer agreement with TSMC that ramps in 2028, so a capacity pause elsewhere lands partly on its customers' balance sheets.

UMC has no such cushion. Its relief arrives with the January price list. The depreciation on the fabs it is building out of its own cash arrives whether the demand does or not.

Three Chip Toolmakers' Order Books Grew in the Month Their Shares Fell About a Fifth

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The market spent November pricing the end of the artificial-intelligence tool cycle. Every order book involved got bigger while it did so.

Applied Materials printed a record quarter and guided the next one to $11.1bn, up 58% year on year, saying clean-room space rather than customer demand caps how fast it grows. ASML's third-quarter release says planned 2027 and 2028 output will not fully meet customer demand, with some customers asking to place orders for 2029. Nothing in the three sets of results explains month-long declines of 18% to 21%. What the three-month split does explain is duration: the contracted lithography backlog fell least, the policy-dependent China book most.

Three of the companies that sell the machines inside a chip fab have reported since mid-October, and each one told investors its order book had grown. Applied Materials, whose deposition, etch, implant and process-control tools go into foundry, memory and advanced-packaging lines, closed its fiscal year with quarterly revenue of $10.41bn, up 53%, and guided the January quarter to $11.1bn give or take $500m.

November read this shelf as the place where artificial-intelligence capital spending runs out, and the reading was applied group-wide, to backlogged and unbacklogged books alike. What the three books describe is a supply constraint. The one switch capable of turning an equipment order off sits in Washington, and its current setting expires on 10 January.

ASML took away the number everyone watched

ASML, the Dutch maker of the lithography scanners that print circuit patterns and the only supplier of extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) machines, grew third-quarter revenue 55% to €11.66bn at a 57.4% gross margin, its third consecutive quarterly expansion. Its results release says order intake remains very strong, that backlog continues to increase across a broad mix of customers, and that some customers want to place orders for 2029 and even 2030. "Given the dynamics of this quarter, we now anticipate that our current planned levels of output in 2027 and 2028 will not fully meet the demand of our customers," chief financial officer Roger Dassen said on the 14 October call.

Investors cannot check that against an orders print, because ASML stopped disclosing quarterly net bookings in 2026, retiring the metric that had moved its shares more than any other. The company is also raising prices on its most critical high-volume products from 2027 without repricing existing backlog, which is the plainest demonstration that the backlog fixes revenue per tool years ahead. The shares now trade at 22.9x next year's consensus earnings, against 30.6x three months ago and roughly 40x in May.

Applied Materials guided up and had its targets cut on valuation

Applied's Semiconductor Systems segment turned over $8bn in the October quarter, up 64%, at a 39% operating margin, and the company logged its fourteenth straight quarter of year-on-year gross-margin expansion. It guides only one quarter ahead and publishes no backlog; the substitute it offers is a count of customer projects, more than 20 fab ramps or line expansions tracked for 2027, with more than 10 major projects added for a third consecutive quarter.

"Nobody is saying that they're not wanting the tools as fast as we can get them," chief executive Gary Dickerson told investors on 12 November. "I've been traveling quite a bit over the last couple of months, and we're constantly receiving requests from customers to accelerate our ramp and deliver earlier." Management expects industry equipment spending up more than 30% in 2027 and is targeting double its current systems output by end-2028, naming clean-room availability as the binding constraint.

The shares fell anyway, 19.7% in the month to 25 November. At least nine brokers cut price targets while keeping Buy or Overweight ratings, and Bank of America moved its target multiple from 27x to 20x 2028 earnings, citing equipment-sector multiple compression. On unchanged consensus of $19.33 for fiscal 2027, the stock is at 16.1x, against 24.7x at its 25 August close.

ACM Research's book is the one a rule can reach

ACM Research, which sells single-wafer wet-cleaning, plating and furnace tools under the Ultra C brand, grew third-quarter revenue 36.6% to $367.7m and widened its operating margin to 15.1% from 10.7%. Plating and furnace was the engine at $109.5m, up 83% and 30% of revenue. Backlog at ACM Shanghai reached RMB 17.1bn, about $2.5bn, up 88%, and on 5 November the company raised 2026 guidance to $1.24bn-$1.28bn from $1.125bn-$1.175bn.

It is also the most exposed. Substantially all of its first-half revenue came from customers in mainland China, where spending answers to domestic substitution policy; chief executive David Wang said on the same call that "China peak will be behind that [2028-2029] by a year or two." Reported GAAP earnings are not usable as an anchor here, since minority interests in the Shanghai subsidiary left diluted earnings per share at $0.02 against the $0.55 presented on the call. Against consensus, the stock sits at 13.0x next year's $3.11, versus 25.4x in late August.

The month did not sort; the quarter did

Over 30 days the decline ignored how the revenue is booked: ASML fell 18.1%, Applied 19.7% and ACM Research 21.0%, inside a selloff that took Teradyne down 31% and Lam Research 22% and included a 9.2% single-session drop in the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index in early November. Stretch the window to three months and the ordering is exactly contract duration: ASML down 25%, Applied 35%, ACM Research 49%.

That is a de-rating rather than a downgrade cycle. Forward estimates were not cut behind any of the three, equipment forecasts for next year were raised into the selloff, and SEMI projects record equipment sales of $156bn in 2027. What separates the three is not demand but who holds the permission to buy. ASML's revenue for the next two years is contracted at fixed prices. Applied's turns on fab build schedules this quarter and next, with China at 25% of October-quarter revenue and guided below 25% of fiscal 2027 shipments. ACM Research's customers are the Chinese fabs that American rules govern directly.

Applied has quantified that risk itself: the Bureau of Industry and Security Affiliates Rule, which extends controls to affiliates of listed Chinese entities, would have cost it about $600m of fiscal 2026 revenue before its suspension. On 9 November the bureau extended that suspension only to 10 January 2027.

Powell Industries, the Houston maker of custom medium-voltage switchgear and modular "E-House" substations, closed its fiscal year on 30 September with the biggest backlog it has ever reported. What a fixed-price order locks in

Corsair Gaming told investors that its memory business had just posted a record quarter and that the margin it earns on that business will be roughly half last year's. Logitech International, buying into the same component market, described the opposite arrangement: supply bought forward for the year and a price list raised in May. Both sell gaming hardware. Only one of them is reselling the shortage.

The pair has been read as a post-pandemic recovery finally turning up. The quarters say something narrower. At Corsair, the growth in the revenue line is the cost shock, arriving at the expense of everything the same customer used to buy alongside it; at Logitech, the shock is a decision about what to charge. The distinction decides which company's reported growth survives a turn in memory contract prices, which TrendForce projects rising 10-15% quarter over quarter in the fourth quarter as cloud buyers absorb capacity.

Nearly half of Corsair's revenue is now a price it does not set

Corsair, which sells peripherals and Elgato streaming gear alongside power supplies, cooling, cases, branded memory modules and pre-built PCs, reported third-quarter revenue of $367.0m, up 6.2%, with memory revenue of $167.3m. "Memory revenue was a record for a third quarter at $167.3 million, up 43% year-over-year," chief executive Thi La told investors on the third-quarter call. "Demand was strong, prices were higher, and we gained market share sequentially."

The share is gained inside a small residual: Kingston has held 62% of the global memory module market for a 23rd consecutive year, with Corsair, G.Skill, Crucial and ADATA splitting what is left behind it.

Memory is now 45.6% of group revenue against 33.8% a year ago, and the rest of the segment paid for it. Computed from the segment lines in the same report, components and systems excluding memory fell from roughly $117.1m to about $83.2m, a decline near 29%: memory inflation added some $50m of revenue while the hardware beside it lost about $34m. Management reads the weakness as deferred rather than lost, which is the same enthusiast declining a $550-plus 32GB kit and declining a case and a cooler with it.

Gross margin fell to 26.7% from 33.2% in the June quarter. Gross profit grew 5.1% against revenue growth of 6.2%, and the company recorded a GAAP operating loss of $1.6m on a quarter that beat its own revenue guidance by $17m. La raised the full-year revenue midpoint $36m. Chief financial officer Gordon Mattingly, on the same call, put the other half on the table: "Last year's fourth quarter was unusually high at about 35% due to average selling prices rising rapidly. We expect memory gross margin of approximately 18%-20% this year, which is still above our historical average."

Logitech answered with a price increase and a purchase order

Logitech's memory exposure sits in video-collaboration room systems, not in mice. Chief financial officer Matteo Anversa told the October 27 call the company had proactively secured supply, which is why inventory is elevated and turns have fallen to 4.8 from 5.2, and that memory components for video conferencing were "pretty much secured throughout the full fiscal year 2027". It raised video-collaboration prices in May; the line's 12% constant-currency growth last quarter split roughly 60% price, 40% volume.

That is the posture of a price-setter, and the share data supports the claim to be one: Logitech leads gaming peripherals at roughly 19% against Razer's 16%, with Corsair near 7-10%. Mouse share rose 320 basis points in the quarter on management's own count.

It did not spare the stock. Logitech reported September-quarter sales of $1.22bn, up 3%, with non-GAAP gross margin of 45.2% and a beat on earnings, then guided the December quarter to down 5% to flat with gross margin of 41-42%, about 200 basis points below last year. Roughly 150 of those points are inflation, split about half freight and half materials. The guide also absorbs $70-80m from a semiconductor supplier's manufacturing incident that closed a facility in late June, cut from an initial estimate of up to $200m through secondary sourcing and redesign. The shares shed about $2bn of market value in a single session, which Vontobel's Michael Foeth attributed to the margin guide and soft European demand.

The market paid for the pass-through and marked down the list price

Over twelve months Corsair has nearly doubled and Logitech has fallen about 10%, and the earnings do not carry much of either. Corsair's price-to-trailing-gross-profit is 2.79x against roughly 1.72x a year ago, so only about 19 percentage points of that advance came from gross-profit growth. Its 13.0x on fiscal 2026 consensus of $0.91 becomes 14.2x on the fiscal 2027 consensus of $0.83, which is 9% lower: the street already treats this year as the peak. Logitech trades at 17.9x trailing and 17.5x forward, cheaper than the 19.0x it closed its last fiscal year at, but consensus has fiscal 2028 earnings only about 3% above fiscal 2027, and the company returned $406m in the September quarter against a $14.4bn market value.

The cause of the split is the invoice. Corsair's growth is denominated in a price the chipmakers set; Logitech's is denominated in a list it publishes, which is why its gross profit grew 6.3% on 2.8% revenue growth while Corsair's did the reverse. Neither escapes the demand side: IDC cut its 2026 global PC shipment forecast to a decline of 11.3% while still expecting market value to rise to $274bn on 17% price growth — fewer units, dearer ones, which is precisely the shape Corsair reported.

A pass-through runs both ways. In late November the memory complex broke hard, with Micron down about 13% in a session as the same researcher trimmed its price outlook. Cheaper kits would release the builds that buy Corsair's cases and coolers, and deflate the line that now supplies nearly half its revenue, and mark down the memory it deliberately stockpiled for the holidays. Nothing in the company's disclosure says which leg lands first.

Texas Instruments told investors on October 21 that its industrial chip revenue had reached its third-quarter 2022 peak for the first time in four years, and that analog capacity outside China and Taiwan is tight because almost none has been added over that span. Back-end test and assembly tool lead times are extending on top of that. Three weeks later the shelf those parts sit on was being marked down as though the opposite were happening.

The markdown was read as a second analog inventory correction arriving: order books cooling, distributors stuffed, the 2024 trough repeating. Every channel figure the four largest Western analog and embedded-control makers disclosed this autumn points the other way. The binding problem is that high-margin orders already placed cannot be built, because the packaging, test and foundry capacity they need has been taken by artificial-intelligence customers paying for it first.

Nobody is building a buffer

Microchip Technology, which sells microcontrollers, analog and timing parts into automotive, industrial and data-center systems, reported on November 5 that its distributor inventory stood at 25 days, flat sequentially and at the low end of a ten-fiscal-year range of roughly 17 to 43 days. Its own lead times have stretched from a historical four to eight weeks out to six to 32, and high-margin orders it could not fulfil in the December quarter were pushed into March. Of the 184 days of inventory on its own balance sheet, 12 days are last-time buys of long-life-cycle parts whose manufacturing capacity its suppliers have discontinued.

Analog Devices, which sells data converters, amplifiers and power management into industrial, automotive, defense and communications sockets largely through distributors, said on November 24 that channel inventory had returned to six to seven weeks, back inside its target model after sitting below six, and that book-to-bill was "nicely above 1x" in almost all end markets. It attributed roughly 30% of revenue — aerospace and defense, automated test equipment, data center — running above consumption to better end demand, with no material restocking detected.

NXP Semiconductors makes the same claim from a different base. "The book to bill remains well above 1 across all end markets and regions. Our backlog continues to grow in dollars and extend further into 2027," chief financial officer William J. Betts told investors on October 27. Eighty percent of NXP's lead times now exceed 16 weeks, against 25% a year earlier, and chief executive Rafael Sotomayor said customers discuss safety stock but that it has yet to occur.

That is three independent confirmations that the channel between the fabs and real consumption is not refilling, against a fourth company reporting its industrial line at a four-year high.

The markdown sorted by cash, not by orders

In the 30 days to November 25, NXP fell 1.2% and Texas Instruments 9.4%, while Analog Devices fell 18.3% and Microchip 24.0%. The order books moved together; the prices did not.

What separated them is who has to spend. Analog Devices guided fiscal-2027 capital expenditure to $1.6bn-$1.9bn against $733m for clean-room and back-end test capacity whose first output arrives as early as fiscal first quarter 2028 — roughly 11% to 12% of a revenue base guided at about $4.4bn a quarter, derived from its own guidance. Trailing free cash flow yields 3.4% against a standing commitment to return all of it. Its shares fell 17.4% close-to-close on November 24, a day the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index dropped 7.88% after rising 14.03% the session before, and coverage attributed the fall to the spending plan and a flat sequential guide rather than to the quarter, in which revenue grew 42.2% year on year and operating margin reached 42.7% from 30.7%.

Microchip's constraint is its balance sheet rather than its budget. Net debt runs at 2.36 times earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization, buybacks are suspended pending further deleveraging and the dividend is held flat. Its margin recovery is pure absorption: non-GAAP gross margin climbed from 52% in the March 2025 quarter to 60.5% by December and 66.5% in September as under-utilization charges shrank with volume. The guide down to 64%-65% is $53m of one-time items booked at full margin not repeating, plus a seasonally lower-utilization quarter.

What the results account for, and what they do not

The reported businesses account for none of the markdown. Both protagonists grew revenue 42.2% year on year in their latest quarters, each the fourth or fifth consecutive acceleration, and neither cut on demand. What the results do not cover is the cash: a doubled fab budget at Analog Devices with no revenue attached until 2028, and a leveraged Microchip absorbing foundry and assembly price increases from January rather than passing them on again. On forward earnings Analog Devices trades at 19.5 times against 32.4 times trailing, and against roughly 58 times trailing in August. Microchip's 16.0 times forward sits against 43.2 times on trough earnings of $0.22 a share in fiscal 2026.

Chief executive Steve Sanghi was blunt about the demand everyone else is buying. "I have been saying it, that data center is in the bubble," he told analysts on November 5 — from a company where data center is 16.7% of sales and industrial grew 50.3%.

Both companies report again around early February. The test is narrow: whether the parts customers have been waiting four months for can be shipped before anyone decides they no longer need them.

Plexus, the Neenah, Wisconsin company paid a fee to assemble other firms' medical devices, semiconductor tools and defense electronics, added roughly $1.5bn of annualized revenue in its last fiscal year and committed only $250m of its own working capital to carry it. Customers funded much of the inventory, and the cash conversion cycle finished the year at 62 days, one day shorter than a year earlier.

That is close to the opposite of the balance sheet November's selloff has been punishing. Plexus has traded below its moving averages since late October and is down 15% over 30 days, to $188.20; its worst session of the stretch was 20 November, when it fell 9% as a report of reduced 2027 volumes for Broadcom-designed accelerators hit the artificial-intelligence hardware complex. On the same shelf, TTM Technologies has fallen a further 16% since 23 November, to a 52-week low of $58.17.

Nothing in Plexus's own disclosures has changed since 29 October.

Ten cents on the assembly dollar, and a target raised anyway

Plexus keeps about a tenth of what passes through it. Gross margin was 10.2% in the September quarter and has sat within a third of a point of that all year, because the model is a fee for building someone else's product rather than a price for a box. The leverage shows up below that line: fiscal fourth-quarter operating income grew 62% on revenue growth of 35%, operating margin reached 6.1%, and the company lifted its target to 6.5–7.0% from a prior 6.0% goal.

"Revenue per employee expanded by approximately 20% over the same time period," David Abuhl, senior vice president and chief financial officer, told investors on 29 October, crediting investments in operational efficiency. Return on invested capital reached 15.8% in the quarter, above the company's own 15% threshold.

The funnel grew where the downturn was

The qualified manufacturing opportunity funnel reached a record $4.9bn, up $1.2bn or 33% year over year. Industrial revenue grew 27% for the year with semiconductor capital equipment up 50%, which management traces to share captured when that market was weak. "We took a significant amount of share particularly through the last downturn within the semi cap market," chief executive Todd Kelsey said on the October call. Aerospace and defense wins totaled $490m for the year, and healthcare grew 19%.

The counts are not uniformly up. Fourth-quarter wins of $290m across 36 programs ran marginally below the roughly $300m quarterly average implied by $1.2bn of full-year wins, and December-quarter revenue is guided flat sequentially as defense demand normalizes and semi-cap orders time out, with industrial down low single digits. Even so, the guide of $2.46–2.62 in non-GAAP earnings per share came in above the $2.34 consensus, and the shares fell 8.9% that day.

Plexus discloses wins and a funnel, never a book-to-bill ratio, so the nearest external read on whether fee-for-assembly order books have cracked is the Global Electronics Association's North American figure: 1.72 in October, with bookings more than doubling year over year.

Cash is the one place the growth costs something

Trailing free-cash-flow yield is −0.58%, and the company forecasts fiscal 2027 free cash flow above $100m on capital spending at the upper end of 2–3% of revenue. Customer funding softens the burden without erasing it: incremental working capital ran at 17% of the revenue added, inside a 15–20% target. Top-ten customer concentration rose to 56% of revenue from 49% a year earlier.

Plexus offers no equivalent tell. Symbotic's Backlog Excludes October's Walmart Order, and Rockwell's Orders Grew Mid-Teens

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Industrial automation was marked down through November on the belief that the physical-AI premium was deflating out of factory and warehouse budgets. Neither company's order book agrees.

Symbotic's year-end backlog fell for a third straight quarter, but its own filings attribute that to revenue recognition, and the Walmart agreement signed in late October adds up to $6.9bn of remaining performance obligations only in the current quarter. The company earned its first annual profit on revenue up 24.8%. Rockwell's fourth-quarter orders rose in the mid-teens and its fiscal 2027 earnings guidance brackets consensus.

The one soft line anywhere is Rockwell's organic recurring revenue, growing 5%, which management tied to delayed projects and customers' own information-technology cost inflation. Symbotic has halved over twelve months; Rockwell's forward multiple has compressed to 25.5x from roughly 35x in May.

Symbotic closed its fiscal year on 26 September, about a month before Walmart signed the agreement that matters most to its order book. The warehouse-automation company, which sells robotic storage, retrieval and case-handling systems to retail and wholesale distributors, reported on 23 November that year-end backlog had slipped to $22.3bn from $22.5bn in June and $22.7bn in March. Its own explanation for the third consecutive decline is conversion: the book shrank because it was shipped, not cancelled.

The shelf these two sit on went into November carrying a premium for humanoids and physical artificial intelligence, and the month took a chunk of it back. The proposition being tested was that the capital budgets funding factory and warehouse robots had cracked.

Two dated reports, one from each company, say otherwise.

The order Symbotic signed after the cut-off

On 29 October Symbotic signed an Enterprise Master Automation Agreement with Walmart, an initial commitment of roughly $6.0bn covering 25 US regional distribution centers plus each-picking and micro-fulfilment deployments. The company said the same agreement could raise remaining performance obligations by up to $6.9bn in the first quarter of fiscal 2027. That falls after the year-end boundary, which is why the reported backlog reads flat.

"So when you sign a $7 billion contract with Walmart, that's the second largest automation contract ever signed," founder and chief executive Rick Cohen told investors on 23 November. "The first one was the $11 billion contract we signed with Walmart." Walmart supplied about 87% of fiscal 2026 revenue and about 47% of the $22.3bn backlog; adding the new commitment to that book implies Walmart near 60% of roughly $29bn of contracted work, a figure computed from the filings and not disclosed by the company.

The year beneath it inflected. Revenue rose 24.8% to $2.805bn, gross margin went from 18.8% to 22.1%, and operating income turned to $100.2m from a $92.1m loss. Management reported $133m of net income under generally accepted accounting principles against a $57m loss, its first full profitable year; the share attributable to Symbotic Inc. after the holdings split was $27.6m. Systems in deployment reached 84, with 59 operational against 56 three months earlier, and first-quarter revenue guidance of $805m to $825m sits above the $777.6m just delivered.

What has not been settled is the estimate underneath systems revenue. Symbotic restated fiscal 2024 after finding errors on milestone timing and non-billable cost overruns, a $30m to $40m reduction to system revenue and gross profit. No fresh revision came with this report. At 28.4x forward gross profit against 37.4x trailing, the stock is cheaper than it was while the business improved, and the sell side is openly split: Needham raised its target to $65 and kept a Buy; Goldman Sachs cut to $33 and kept a Sell, below the 25 November close of $38.02.

Rockwell's one soft line

Rockwell Automation, the Milwaukee maker of controllers, drives and factory software sold through distributors, lost 14.2% close-to-close on 5 November, its largest session in either name this month. Reported fourth-quarter revenue of $2.332bn grew 0.7% and missed consensus, though a customer refund provision of $85m cost roughly four points of growth. Full-year core sales rose 9%, adjusted earnings per share rose 27% to $13.42, and enterprise operating margin reached 22.2%. "We achieved our long-term enterprise operating margin target of 22% three years after setting the target," chairman and chief executive Blake Moret said on the call.

Fourth-quarter orders were up in the mid-teens and Lifecycle Services book-to-bill was 0.97, which the company called above historical fourth-quarter seasonality. Fiscal 2027 guidance of 6% to 9% organic growth and $14.50 to $15.50 of adjusted earnings brackets the $15.08 consensus. No distributor destocking was mentioned.

The blemish is recurring revenue, which grew 5% organically against 7% guided for the coming year. Moret tied it to two mechanisms: delays in the projects that carry attached recurring contracts, and information-technology equipment inflation, with one food and beverage customer halving its planned plant deployments. The memory-cost squeeze already visible in the distribution channel now shows up as a tax on the customer's automation budget.

What the prints account for

Both troughs fell on 20 November, the day the S&P 500 closed in correction 11.6% below its 6 October record, with the index proxy down 6.4% over the month. Symbotic then rose 22.3% in three sessions across its report. Against that, the income statements explain very little of the twelve-month damage: Symbotic is down by half while gross profit grew 46.7%.

The de-ratings have different causes. Rockwell's compression from roughly 35x forward earnings in May to 25.5x did rational work, because consensus has fiscal 2027 revenue growing 5.5% and management describes the US capacity cycle as "around the fourth inning" with no broad capital-expenditure release assumed. Symbotic's is the harder case: revenue is recognised on deployment throughput against estimates the company has had to restate once, and the customer paying it grows more dominant.

The one piece of Symbotic's contracted work that has genuinely underperformed is the joint venture it does not consolidate. Exol, the warehouse-as-a-service vehicle in which it holds 35%, reaches the end of its initial contract term next year still short of its $7.5bn target, and the first Atlanta site now has to prove itself for the five more under construction.
