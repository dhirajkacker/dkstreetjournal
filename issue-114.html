Vertiv Will Stop Reporting Orders, Leaving Prepaid Customer Cash as the Demand Record

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The clearest demand signal in the AI power chain has just been withdrawn. Vertiv told investors on 28 October it will no longer publish order or book-to-bill figures, keeping backlog only in its annual report. What remains is cash already collected: current deferred revenue of $4,120.5m at 30 September against $1,814.7m at the end of 2025, equal to roughly 28% of guided 2026 sales.

The three links of the electrical path are paid at very different points. GE Vernova takes a non-refundable deposit of about 20% years before delivery and sits on $197bn of backlog; nVent carries $2.6bn, under half a year of shipments, and was the only one of the three whose gross margin fell year-on-year, to 35.8%.

All three raised 2026 guidance in late October. All three were then marked down between 17% and 35%, which reads as a repricing of orders not yet placed rather than of the contracted book.

Vertiv Holdings, which sells the uninterruptible power supplies, busway and liquid cooling installed inside data-center white space under the Liebert, Geist and Powerbar brands, told investors on 28 October that it will stop publishing how many orders it takes. Chief executive Giordano Albertazzi said the company is "not planning to share orders data going forward", arguing that lumpy large orders create "excessive volatility" unrepresentative of sustained performance. Dollar orders, book-to-bill and backlog have not been disclosed since the February call covering the fourth quarter of 2025; the annual Form 10-K backlog figure is what is left.

The timing matters because the markdown of the power-equipment shelf has been read as demand deceleration along the electrical path between the utility interconnect and the rack. The companies' own October filings say the opposite about what is already sold. What separates them is where in that path each one gets paid, and for how long its revenue is already contracted.

The line Vertiv's order book left behind

Vertiv counts backlog narrowly. Albertazzi said on the same call that the company is "very strict in what [it] calls backlog" and books only "what is squarely a purchase order" — a capacity agreement does not qualify. The last disclosed figure, $15.0bn, is about one year of the $14,650m of 2026 sales the company now guides to at the midpoint, a raise accompanied by adjusted earnings guidance of $7.15 a share. September-quarter sales of $4,313m were 61% above a year earlier, 55% of that organic, and gross margin edged up to 38.1%.

The harder number is money received. Current deferred revenue stood at $4,120.5m at 30 September against $1,814.7m at 31 December 2025, cash paid at order and at design milestones to hold manufacturing priority. Vertiv's own filings note that orders can be cancelled or rescheduled, which is why the prepayment balance now does more work than the backlog line it effectively replaces.

Paid in 2026 for turbines delivered in 2031

GE Vernova, the gas-turbine and grid-equipment maker spun out in 2023, collects earlier than anyone else in the chain. A customer converts a reservation into a production slot by posting a non-refundable deposit of typically about 20% of the turbine price, years ahead of delivery. That mechanic produced a $5.3bn working-capital benefit inside $5.4bn of third-quarter free cash flow, and total backlog of $197bn, $104bn of it equipment — against revenue guidance of $46.5bn to $47.5bn, roughly 4.2 years of cover by our arithmetic. Power equipment pricing rose more than 20% in the quarter and segment EBITDA margin reached 21.4%.

One gauge in that disclosure points down. The slot-reservation queue fell from 63 GW to 58 GW while firm gas equipment backlog climbed from 53 GW to 64 GW: the funnel ahead of the contracted book thinned as the book filled.

Five months of cover, and a $105m tariff bill

nVent Electric, the smallest of the three at about $18bn of market value, sells enclosures, cabinets and liquid-cooling hardware largely through electrical wholesalers. Its backlog is $2.6bn, under half a year of the $5.79bn of 2026 revenue consensus carries, so costs land in the same quarters as the shipments they burden. Price and productivity more than covered $60m of quarterly inflation, including about $20m of tariffs against a full-year tariff estimate lifted to roughly $105m, but gross margin still fell to 35.84% from 37.45% a year earlier. Operating margin rose anyway, to 18.6%.

nVent raised guidance hardest of the three. "At the midpoint, adjusted EPS is expected to grow 60% versus last year," chief financial officer Gary Corona said on 30 October. Its organic orders grew only low-double-digits against a 65% comparison, with management calling October orders "very strong."

What the selling is actually pricing

Over the month to 9 November, Vertiv fell 17.4%, GE Vernova 17.5% and nVent 34.9%, against 5.6% for the S&P 500 tracker and 26.3% for Nvidia. nVent is down 3.0% over twelve months, the worst of the three despite the biggest guidance raise. Gross-profit multiples compressed accordingly: Vertiv to 15.0x trailing from 29.1x in early May, GE Vernova to 22.0x from 36.6x, nVent to 9.4x from 15.1x.

Nothing in the three sets of October disclosures contains a cancellation, a pushout or a cut. What repriced sits with the buyers: CoreWeave's 2032 bond yielded above 16% in the week to 6 November with the 30-year Treasury at 5.25%, and the paper has already noted the post-election scrutiny of data-center siting in Ohio, Texas and Georgia. A higher hurdle rate changes which projects get sanctioned next year, not which turbines ship in 2029. On that reading the de-rating prices the funnel, and GE Vernova's 58 GW of reservations is the only funnel number any of the three still reports.

Albertazzi told investors on 28 October that "the demand environment remains strong heading into 2027", naming power availability, permitting and skilled construction labor as the pacing items. Having retired the quarterly order print, Vertiv has made its deferred revenue line the thing shareholders must read it by — and has left itself one scheduled chance a year to prove the book is still growing.

Carriers Took Three-Fifths of Bandwidth's New Revenue and None of Its Gross Profit

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A fee that American wireless carriers set, not a price that software vendors negotiate, now drives the reported revenue line at both listed communications-API companies — and it arrives at zero margin, which makes the revenue growth and the reported gross margin equally misleading.

Bandwidth's September quarter added $42m of revenue year on year, of which $25m was messaging surcharge collected and remitted to carriers. Reported gross margin duly fell to 36.2%. Yet non-GAAP gross profit rose 18% to $96m and margin widened to 60.6%, and every full-year guidance metric went up. Twilio showed the same pattern in milder form: gross-profit dollars grew faster than revenue, with the per-quarter fee drag easing to $53m from $71m.

Both were sold hard after reporting on 29 October. The profit line does not explain it; decelerating fourth-quarter guides and a 2027 that laps the fee increases do.

America's big wireless carriers raised the fee they charge for delivering a text message repeatedly this year, and that money passes through the books of the companies that sell messaging and voice interfaces to software developers before it leaves again. It inflates revenue, deflates reported gross margin, and touches gross profit not at all. Both listed pure-plays reported on 29 October and both were sold hard afterwards, which raises a narrower question than the share prices suggest: whether the carrier is now taking the increment, or merely routing through it.

The market read the quarters as evidence that the meter is breaking — that volume is growing while the price of a delivered message falls toward the carrier's own cost. The profit lines say the opposite, and the split between the two companies is about what comes next, not about what happened.

What a message costs before anyone earns on it

The fee is set upstream. Across four pricing updates in 2026, six separate US SMS and MMS fee increases took effect: T-Mobile lifted its outbound pass-through for registered business messaging from $0.0030 to $0.0045 per segment in January, a 50% step, and Verizon raised outbound SMS on 1 May and again from $0.0045 to $0.0050 on 1 October. The blended fee went from roughly $0.0033 to about $0.0043 a segment in under a year. The increases were applied across registered campaigns, toll-free and short-code traffic alike, and Verizon's October change also lifted its rich-messaging fee.

Bandwidth's own meter is not the one that slowed

Bandwidth, which runs its own carrier network and sells voice and messaging interfaces plus session-initiation trunking to large enterprises and contact centers, reported revenue of $234.0m for the quarter ended 30 September. Cloud communications revenue — total less surcharges — rose 12% to $158.5m while surcharge revenue rose 51% to $75.6m. On the quarter's $33m of programmable messaging revenue, that is roughly $2.29 of remitted surcharge for every dollar the company keeps, on figures the desk has divided out.

Reported gross margin fell 222 basis points to 36.2%. Underneath it, GAAP gross profit grew 14.9%, an acceleration from 9.0% and 9.6% in the first two quarters; non-GAAP gross profit rose 18% to $96m and non-GAAP gross margin widened nearly 300 basis points to 60.6%, an 86% yield on the growth in cloud communications revenue. Adjusted EBITDA rose 43% to $35m and operating income turned positive at $1.8m. "We're raising the midpoint of our full year 2026 outlook across all of our guidance metrics," chief executive David Morken told investors on 29 October, adding that the company has "growing confidence in achieving double-digit cloud communications revenue growth in fiscal 2027." Bandwidth was also named the first certified bring-your-own-carrier partner for Salesforce's contact-center rollout across more than 25 countries, which management models at roughly a $20m annual run rate by end-2028.

Twilio's fee drag shrank while its expansion rate eased

Twilio, whose roughly 1,800 usage-billed interfaces let developers embed voice, messaging and email in their own applications, carried $53m of incremental US carrier pass-through fees in the quarter, below the $71m it absorbed in the second quarter. Non-GAAP gross margin of 49.2% was down 90 basis points but would have risen 80 without the fees; GAAP gross profit grew 20.3% against revenue growth of 19.9%, and free cash flow reached a record $370m. Full-year organic growth guidance went to 15% from 13–13.5%, and "we expect full year 2027 organic revenue growth of at least 10%, well above our 2025 Investor Day framework of 7% to 8%," chief financial officer Aidan Viggiano said on the same day.

The honest offsets sit in the demand data rather than the margin: dollar-based net expansion eased to 114% from 116%, organic messaging growth slipped to 17% from 18%, and the fourth quarter is guided to 11–12% organic. The AI voice business widely credited with re-pricing the category is barely visible. "The way that we characterize voice AI in our prepared remarks is that it's about 1% of what the voice interactions are so far," chief executive Khozema Shipchandler said, calling mainstream adoption something that "is going to take some time."

What the selling was actually about

Bandwidth has fallen 32.9% in the 30 days to 6 November and Twilio 27.2%, with both companies beating on revenue and earnings. Bandwidth dropped 13% because full-year guidance matched consensus rather than exceeding it, and Twilio fell on its fourth-quarter outlook and margin commentary rather than the quarter. A chunk is beta: on 5 November, the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.5% and Bandwidth lost 10.4%, Twilio 7.2%. Neighbours moved far less — RingCentral down 6.5% over the 30 days, Five9 down 15.9%.

So the quarters account for almost none of the drawdown and the forward guides account for most of it. Bandwidth's own full-year range implies fourth-quarter growth of 17–19%, against 22% in the third, and consensus has 2027 revenue growing 7.7% as the fee increases lap — the reported line deflates precisely because the pass-through stops compounding. At 3.84x trailing and 3.69x forward gross profit, the appropriate anchor when surcharges make revenue non-comparable and GAAP earnings sit near zero, Bandwidth is below the 4.9x recorded in late August and 5.9x in late September. Twilio's 11.03x trailing has unwound the entire spike to 15.6x in September but remains well above roughly 7x in February, and its gross-profit growth is guided to slow. One has de-rated past its own history on a 2027 that management says is already largely signed; the other has given back a re-rating it earned three months ago.

The fee increases are the one input neither company controls and both are indifferent to in profit terms. They will lap, the reported growth rate will halve, and the business underneath will look exactly as it does now.

NAPCO Security Technologies, which makes intrusion and fire alarms and the cellular radios that report them to monitoring stations, grew revenue just 1.7% in its September quarter, the slowest in two years of reported quarters. The same three months produced the most profitable revenue mix the company has ever had: recurring service fees of $26.1m at a gross margin of 90.1%, now 52% of the total, which lifted group gross margin to 58.7% from 56.6%.

ASML's September quarter carried an admission capital-equipment vendors rarely make: it has sold more machines than it intends to build. The Dutch company, the only producer of extreme-ultraviolet lithography scanners, moved its order-book disclosure onto the balance sheet after discontinuing quarterly net bookings, and the substitute line moved violently — customer advances for tools due more than a year out rose €5.73bn in the quarter, reaching €9.30bn. "Given the dynamics of this quarter, we now anticipate that our current planned levels of output in 2027 and 2028 will not fully meet the demand of our customers," chief financial officer Roger J. Dassen told investors on October 14.

The thirteen listed suppliers of wafer-fabrication equipment fell between 20.6% and 48.9% in the month to 6 November. The drawdown has been read as a memory shortage failing to convert into tool orders: bits added by node migration and conversion inside cleanrooms that are already built consume far less equipment than new wafer starts, so record memory capital budgets need not become record tool sales. The last reported quarter at four of these vendors locates the constraint somewhere else entirely — in the cleanrooms, not in the willingness to order.

The prepayments arrive before the floor space does

ASML's record net sales of €11.7bn grew 55.2% year on year, with gross margin widening to 57.4% from 51.6% and operating income more than doubling. Of €8.4bn in system sales, €4.1bn was EUV. Low-NA EUV output is going to roughly 65 systems this year from 44 in 2025, with 30% growth planned for 2027 in both low-NA EUV and immersion deep-ultraviolet, and management is investigating going beyond that for 2028 because demand exceeds the plan. Prices on the most critical high-volume products rise from 2027, described as one-time rather than annual; existing backlog is not repriced.

Installed-base management revenue — upgrades and service paid on tools already running — was €3.3bn in the quarter and is guided to grow about 50% this year against roughly 36% for the group. That is the one piece of the bearish mechanism that holds: at ASML, the running fleet is earning faster than the new one. At 34.6x forward earnings, the shares are below the 40-48x forward recorded in May.

Lam capped its own December guide on parts

Lam Research, which sells the etch and deposition tools that cut and fill the high-aspect-ratio structures in 3D NAND and high-bandwidth memory stacks, grew revenue 54.4% to $8.219bn on a record 52.3% gross margin, its fifth consecutive revenue record. Deferred revenue rose $227m to $2.66bn. "Our preliminary 2027 WFE outlook is for more than $200 billion of spending, with percentage growth led by DRAM, followed by leading-edge foundry logic and NAND," chief financial officer Doug Bettinger said on October 21, a figure KLA independently corroborated alongside record quarterly revenue of $4.14bn and its first operating margin above 46%.

The shares fell as much as 11% on the day, because Bettinger said the $8.6bn December guide would have been higher but for near-term supply-chain constraints, with deferred shipments pushed into the first quarter of 2027. Lam also told the market that limited cleanroom space and parts shortages would stop the industry meeting demand into 2027. Service revenue of $2.5bn grew 40%, slower than the group. China rose to 35% of revenue from 26%. The forward earnings multiple is 20.8x against roughly 38x in May, while reported growth more than doubled.

What 100,000 wafer starts costs in implanters

Axcelis Technologies, which builds ion implanters, supplies the arithmetic the thesis needs. Roughly $150m to $200m of implant equipment serves 100,000 wafer starts a month, and its top three memory customers are adding more than 1m starts a month over four to five years. Memory is about 20% of system sales, nearly all DRAM and HBM. September-quarter bookings of $222m gave a 1.5x book-to-bill, the best since the fourth quarter of 2023, and chief executive Russell J. Low named the gate on November 5: "we are watching very, very carefully the availability of kind of greenfield clean room space because clearly, you know, today, our customers are often 4 wall constraints." Megafabs open "starting in 2027, but they are they are gonna be in phases." The stock trades at 24.2x forward earnings, with consensus 2026 earnings per share of $4.11 down 8.7%.

That capacity is being built. Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron are expected to spend $146bn in 2027, 3.4 times the $43bn of 2024, and SK Hynix has pulled completion of its Yongin cluster forward by twelve years. J.P. Morgan Global Research estimates DRAM prices will have risen more than 400% from the start of 2024 to the end of 2026.

Onto's leg is not reportable yet

Onto Innovation, which sells macro defect inspection and 3D bump metrology into advanced packaging, is the unverifiable case. Its June quarter grew 35.3% with operating income up 97.1%, off a 2025 that rose 1.8%. It raised its 2026 advanced-packaging growth outlook to roughly 80% from 50%, crossed $1bn of backlog, and closed a volume purchase agreement estimated above $240m with a leading HBM manufacturer. But 64.7x trailing earnings compresses to 21.4x forward only on consensus 2027 revenue of $1.44bn, up 43%, and the company guided the September quarter to $380-400m and reports between 9 and 12 November.

What the quarters explain, and what they do not

Nothing in these results explains the ordering of the selling. Onto, the purest HBM metrology name, fell 42.2%; Ultra Clean's gas-panel assembly fell 41.7%; ASML, the most lithography- and memory-levered name of all, fell least at 20.6%. The sequence tracks market value and liquidity. Roughly two-thirds of the damage was done in October, after Applied Materials declined to put a number on 2027 equipment spending at its 13 October investor day — Applied's own July quarter grew 24.8% and it now trades at 17.7x forward against 28.5x trailing — and the rest in the week to 6 November, when concern over tighter oversight of data-center construction hit the whole AI complex, a move Bank of America characterized as multiple compression rather than earnings cuts. Applied sits 54.0% below its 52-week high and Lam 53.7%. Of the names here, only ASML still holds the uptrend all thirteen held in May.

The bear case is not wholly empty. China's share of world equipment spending fell from 42.3% to 36.5% in 2025 with billings down about 11%, so the pull-forward is normalising at industry level even as it failed to appear sequentially at Lam or Axcelis. And a cycle whose gating item is construction phasing is a cycle whose revenue can slip a quarter at a time without any customer changing its mind.

Which is the live question for 2027, and it is a question about concrete and electrical capacity rather than about bits. The three memory makers' output for all of next year is already reported sold out. The tools to make more have been ordered and, at ASML, partly paid for. What nobody in this chain controls is when the doors open.

Appian raised its full-year guidance on 5 November after revenue growth accelerated in the September quarter, and its shares rose in a week when every large enterprise-software name around it fell. Between 4 and 6 November, ServiceNow lost 7.2%, Pegasystems 8.3%, Salesforce 5.9% and Snowflake 5.1%. Appian gained 3.7%.

The year-long mark-down of enterprise software rests on one mechanism: if an AI-assisted employee does the work of three, a per-seat invoice falls by up to two-thirds. Bain puts the cost of that repricing at roughly $2trn of market value over twelve months. Appian, which sells a low-code platform that generates workflows, forms, data models and interfaces instead of hand-coded applications to banks, insurers, life-sciences companies and government agencies, was read as an early casualty: a process-automation layer with no infrastructure underneath it, facing ServiceNow, Pegasystems and Microsoft's Power Platform. Over the year to 6 November, Pegasystems fell 45.0% and ServiceNow 40.6%. Appian rose 34.5%.

The upsell that reads as retention

Appian's AI money arrives as a price increase. Most of the quarter's uplift came from customers upgrading to the Advanced Tier, which carries a 25–35% premium to Standard, with AI usage up more than 50% from the prior quarter and consumption overages growing but not yet material. The effect lands in the company's disclosed retention meter: cloud net annual-recurring-revenue expansion hit 120%, against 115% in the prior quarter and 112% a year earlier, which management called the strongest in more than five years. Cloud subscription revenue of $136m grew 20%; total subscriptions, including self-managed software, grew 28% to $188m.

"Appian has claimed a distinctive and valuable place in the AI stack," chief executive Matthew Calkins told investors on 5 November. "We connect the world's most important technology to the world's most important work."

Four verticals — financial services, insurance, life sciences and the public sector — supply 80% of recurring revenue, and more than 70% of the business comes from customers spending over $1m a year, chief financial officer Serge Tanjga told the William Blair growth conference. That concentration is why one US Cabinet agency's seven-figure procurement deal moves a quarter.

Margin widened; the cash went the other way

GAAP operating income rose 62.4% to $21.3m, a 9.1% margin, and adjusted EBITDA of $43m beat a $30–33m guide. Full-year EBITDA guidance went to $116–119m, up about 53%, and revenue guidance to $873–877m. The base is low: 2025 was Appian's first positive GAAP operating year, at $0.6m on $726.9m of revenue.

Three things complicate the inflection. GAAP gross margin fell 2.9 points to 72.9%, while management described gross margin as 74% on a non-GAAP basis, flat year on year. Professional-services gross margin fell to 24% from 31% as implementations scale. And operating cash flow was minus $15m against positive $19m a year earlier, with cash and investments down to $89m from $187m at the end of 2025; $83m of that decline is cumulative buybacks under a $100m authorization. Management called the cash timing-driven and said October collections were among the strongest months on record.

The January retrial is not the engine

The shares have held an uptrend since 12 August, six sessions after the 6 August second-quarter beat-and-raise. The retrial of Appian's vacated $2.036bn trade-secrets award against Pegasystems opens on 11 January 2027, after the Supreme Court of Virginia threw out the 2022 verdict in January 2026; on 16 October the court let Appian present a reasonable-royalty damages theory it was barred from using at the original trial. Appian then rose 27.2% in a week. But Pegasystems rose 5.4% in the same window and ServiceNow 12.0%, so the likelier reading is a broad software rally that Appian led at roughly twice the move. Short interest of about 3.79m shares, some 5.3% of shares outstanding, is too small to be the cause.

Dearer than it was, cheaper than ServiceNow

Price-to-gross-profit has gone from 3.07x in early May to 6.05x, against gross profit that grew 20.8%: the price has roughly doubled relative to the profit it buys. On forward earnings, Appian's 47.7x is nearly twice ServiceNow's 25.8x.

Measured against forward gross profit the order inverts. Appian's 6.00x sits below ServiceNow's 8.92x, where 20.5% revenue growth came with GAAP operating income down 32.7%, and far below Snowflake's 22.97x, where 35.1% growth comes with a $263.0m quarterly operating loss.

Pegasystems, at 3.91x forward gross profit, is the cheapest of the four, and 3.1% revenue growth with 7% annual contract value growth says why. "Our technology is differentiated and very relevant to the times we are in. However, candidly, our execution this year has not kept up," founder-chief executive Alan Trefler said on 21 October. Salesforce, at 4.92x, grew 10.8%.

What the quarter can and cannot pay for

The operating results account for the direction and the timing of the advance, and for Appian being the one name in its neighborhood that gained in the week its peers gave up ground. They do not account for the full doubling of the valuation, because the acceleration is one quarter old. In the June quarter revenue growth slowed to 19.1%, GAAP gross margin compressed to 71.2%, and unsatisfied performance obligations of $625.3m grew 13%, slower than revenue. The past month has tested that reading. Buyers of these shares are paying for gross profit already banked rather than for signed backlog: on forward gross profit, five listed space names span roughly 10 times at York Space Systems to 89 times at Rocket Lab.

The leverage is real; the catalyst moved

Rocket Lab, the only listed company that both reaches orbit and builds the spacecraft and components that fly there, is converting. June-quarter gross profit rose 82.3%, faster than revenue, and gross margin reached 36.1% against 32.1% a year earlier. Annual gross margin has climbed from 9.0% in 2022 to 34.4% in 2025. The credentials behind that are specific: a prime contract worth $816m for 18 missile-warning satellites under the Space Development Agency's third tranche, plus solar cells, reaction wheels and star trackers sold into other primes' missions.

It still lost $57.5m at the operating line in the quarter and consumes cash, with a trailing free cash flow yield of -1.17%. Consensus does not have net income turning positive until 2028. Launch, the activity the equity story is named after, recognized about $7.4m per flight in the June quarter, a figure derived from segment revenue and the six Electrons flown; fifteen Electron missions flew in 2026 through 26 September. At 88.9 times forward gross profit the shares cost five times Planet Labs' 17.6 times.

Neutron was the asset meant to close that distance.

Planet turned cash-positive as its multiple fell by two-thirds

Planet Labs, which sells daily global imagery from a constellation of more than 150 satellites to governments, agriculture, finance and insurance customers, moved the opposite way. Revenue growth accelerated for four consecutive quarters to 58.1% in the July quarter, on gross margin of 56.6%, and the operating margin narrowed to -11.6% from -24.5%. The company posted adjusted EBITDA of $13.9m and guided fiscal 2027 revenue to $430m-$441m. Trailing sales now cost 11.3x, against 39.4x in early May.

The limit on that case is the order book. Backlog was $814.9m, up only 11% year on year, far behind revenue; one-time satellite handovers, led by the first Pelican delivered to the Swedish Armed Forces, were 12% of July-quarter revenue against 1% a year earlier, and existing customers expanded spend by 9%. Rocket Lab's quarter does not explain its 25.6% fall over thirty days, but its calendar does: a premium written against a first Neutron flight loses its justification when that flight moves a year, leaving a hardware business earning 36% gross margins priced at nearly nine times what Redwire's gross profit costs. Grid Dynamics, a San Ramon, California engineering firm that builds digital and AI software for Fortune 1000 retailers, banks, telecom carriers and consumer-goods companies, closed September with a smaller workforce than a year earlier and the biggest revenue quarter in its history. Revenue reached $114.3m, up 9.7% year on year. Headcount fell to 4,559 from 4,838, reported gross margin hit 40.3% against 33.3%, and revenue per employee passed $100,000 for the first time.

For two years the market priced enterprise systems integrators as generative artificial intelligence's first casualty: fewer billable people, compressed pyramids, clients building in-house. October reversed that. All eight names on the enterprise consulting shelf rose over the 30 days to 6 November, a median of 31.4%, while the S&P 500 exchange-traded fund fell 5.4% and Nvidia fell 21.4%. The trigger was Accenture's 1 October results, which lifted the battered group on the reading that corporations still need outside help fitting AI into older systems. The order books support that reading. The revenue guidance does not, and only one firm on the shelf has shown the conversion arriving in the numbers.

The quarter where headcount and revenue came apart

Grid Dynamics' margin gain was priced, not cut. Non-GAAP gross margin of 40.6% was 370 basis points wider than the prior quarter, which management attributed to AI project mix, higher billing rates and better utilization; the company hit its annual 300-point expansion goal a quarter early. Non-GAAP EBITDA of $22.9m, a 20% margin, beat its own guidance range of $16.5m to $17.5m. AI revenue grew 45% year on year for a third consecutive quarter and now approaches 32% of the total, with 93% of engineers trained on AI software-development methods.

"In a sector where headcount and revenue have historically moved in lockstep, Grid Dynamics is demonstrating that the two can and should be decoupled," chief executive Leonard Livschitz said on the 29 October call. "12 months ago, AI-native delivery was the direction we were heading toward. Today, it's how we deliver." Management guides 2027 to healthy double-digit growth after a run of single-digit quarters.

Why the big offshore firms are conceding the same savings

At the large names, the order book and the revenue line point in opposite directions. Infosys, the Bengaluru firm behind the Topaz AI suite and the Finacle banking platform, won 19 large deals worth $5.2bn of total contract value in its September quarter, 62% of it net new and its second-highest quarterly haul in five years. It then cut fiscal 2027 revenue growth guidance to 1-2% in constant currency on 23 October. "Growth was below our expectations," chief financial officer Jayesh Sanghrajka told investors that day. "Volume was softer, and price increases were lower than anticipated amidst high competitive intensity." Infosys added 4,400 net staff in the quarter and says walking away from deals priced below its return standards costs 1.8 to 2.0 percentage points of this year's growth.

Cognizant, the Teaneck, New Jersey outsourcer serving banks, healthcare payers, manufacturers and retailers, shows the mix improving as the total shrinks: trailing twelve-month bookings of $28.7bn were up 4% at a 1.3 times book-to-bill, but bookings signed in the third quarter alone fell 7% and full-year constant-currency growth guidance came down to 4.0-4.4% from 4.0-5.5%. New and expansion work is now 60% of the bookings mix against 48%, and fixed-price plus transaction-based work reached 58% of revenue, up 140 points on the year. "AI is a tailwind. There is no doubt in my mind," chief executive Ravi Kumar said on 29 October. The earnings line tells a quieter story: adjusted earnings per share are guided up 8-10% on dollar revenue up 4.5-4.9%, with diluted shares down from 497m to 450m since the end of 2024.

Accenture, which sells strategy, technology and managed operations to large corporations and governments, booked a record $84.5bn in fiscal 2026 and 141 deals above $100m against 129. It is also hiring, with headcount up 5% to 814,000, and it guided fiscal 2027 to 3-6% local-currency growth against 5% delivered, with 2 to 2.5 points of that from acquisitions. Its revenue per person, roughly $91,100, sits about 48% above Cognizant's and Infosys's, which is the structural line on this shelf between the firms holding price and the firms conceding it. EPAM, the Newtown, Pennsylvania platform engineering firm, is the only member to raise guidance inside the window, lifting organic constant-currency growth to 2.5-2.7% on 5 November with gross profit up 17.4% on revenue up 2.3%.

How much of the move the businesses explain

Not most of it. Globant and CGI disclosed nothing at all in the window and rose 33.1% and 18.6%; the likelier reading is a rotation out of AI hardware into the de-rated services layer, which Merrill's Chief Investment Office described on 2 November as capital moving from AI builders toward AI adopters. Grid Dynamics is the exception where the business moved first: it rose 53.6% in the session after its 29 October report, close to close, to a 52-week high, with intraday reports of about 34%. Over twelve months the shelf's median return is minus 8.2%.

The cause of the split is contractual. A firm billing person-months must hand the client the productivity it finds, because the client can see the hours; a firm selling an outcome with a smaller team books the difference as margin. That is why Infosys's utilization and offshore mix carry its margin while price falls, and why Grid Dynamics' margin came with rates going up.

The market is paying accordingly. Grid Dynamics trades at 24.3 times forward earnings and 6.7 times trailing gross profit, the dearest forward multiple of the names here, against Accenture's 17.75 times forward, itself up from 14.1 times trailing at the 31 August fiscal year-end and still 29% below a five-year year-end median of 26.9 times. Cognizant's 11.2 times forward remains below the 14-15 times it carried in May. Grid Dynamics' trailing price-to-earnings of 145.6 times says nothing, on minimal reported net income.

One thing financed the decoupling that is worth watching more than the margin. Grid Dynamics' top ten customers now supply 63.3% of revenue, up from 58.3%, which management attributes to clients cutting their vendor lists down to a few partners. Chief financial officer Anil Doradla told investors on 29 October the mandate now is to scale, with an appetite for larger acquisitions in 2027. A shorter client list is a faster way to grow revenue per head, and a harder one to defend if one of those clients decides it can do the work itself.
