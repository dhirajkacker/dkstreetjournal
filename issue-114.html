Appian Charges for AI by Moving Customers to a Pricier Tier of the Same Contract

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Software metered by the seat has been marked down for a year on the belief that AI shrinks the invoice. Appian's September quarter points the other way: revenue growth accelerated to 25.5% and its cloud net revenue expansion rate reached 120%, the strongest in more than five years, because AI adoption arrives as an upgrade to a more expensive tier of a contract the customer already holds.

The business explains the share move. The uptrend began six sessions after the August beat-and-raise, well before the procedural win in Appian's trade-secrets retrial against Pegasystems. What the business does not yet cover is the price: gross profit grew 20.8% while price-to-gross-profit roughly doubled from early May, operating cash flow was minus $15m against positive $19m a year earlier, and consensus still models 2027 revenue growth at 10.8%.

Appian raised its full-year guidance on 5 November after revenue growth accelerated in the September quarter, and its shares rose in a week when every large enterprise-software name around it fell. Between 4 and 6 November, ServiceNow lost 7.2%, Pegasystems 8.3%, Salesforce 5.9% and Snowflake 5.1%. Appian gained 3.7%.

The year-long mark-down of enterprise software rests on one mechanism: if an AI-assisted employee does the work of three, a per-seat invoice falls by up to two-thirds. Bain puts the cost of that repricing at roughly $2trn of market value over twelve months. Appian, which sells a low-code platform that generates workflows, forms, data models and interfaces instead of hand-coded applications to banks, insurers, life-sciences companies and government agencies, was read as an early casualty: a process-automation layer with no infrastructure underneath it, facing ServiceNow, Pegasystems and Microsoft's Power Platform. Over the year to 6 November, Pegasystems fell 45.0% and ServiceNow 40.6%. Appian rose 34.5%.

The upsell that reads as retention

Appian's AI money arrives as a price increase. Most of the quarter's uplift came from customers upgrading to the Advanced Tier, which carries a 25–35% premium to Standard, with AI usage up more than 50% from the prior quarter and consumption overages growing but not yet material. The effect lands in the company's disclosed retention meter: cloud net annual-recurring-revenue expansion hit 120%, against 115% in the prior quarter and 112% a year earlier, which management called the strongest in more than five years. Cloud subscription revenue of $136m grew 20%; total subscriptions, including self-managed software, grew 28% to $188m.

"Appian has claimed a distinctive and valuable place in the AI stack," chief executive Matthew Calkins told investors on 5 November. "We connect the world's most important technology to the world's most important work."

Four verticals — financial services, insurance, life sciences and the public sector — supply 80% of recurring revenue, and more than 70% of the business comes from customers spending over $1m a year, chief financial officer Serge Tanjga told the William Blair growth conference. That concentration is why one US Cabinet agency's seven-figure procurement deal moves a quarter.

Margin widened; the cash went the other way

GAAP operating income rose 62.4% to $21.3m, a 9.1% margin, and adjusted EBITDA of $43m beat a $30–33m guide. Full-year EBITDA guidance went to $116–119m, up about 53%, and revenue guidance to $873–877m. The base is low: 2025 was Appian's first positive GAAP operating year, at $0.6m on $726.9m of revenue.

Three things complicate the inflection. GAAP gross margin fell 2.9 points to 72.9%, while management described gross margin as 74% on a non-GAAP basis, flat year on year. Professional-services gross margin fell to 24% from 31% as implementations scale. And operating cash flow was minus $15m against positive $19m a year earlier, with cash and investments down to $89m from $187m at the end of 2025; $83m of that decline is cumulative buybacks under a $100m authorization. Management called the cash timing-driven and said October collections were among the strongest months on record.

The January retrial is not the engine

The shares have held an uptrend since 12 August, six sessions after the 6 August second-quarter beat-and-raise. The retrial of Appian's vacated $2.036bn trade-secrets award against Pegasystems opens on 11 January 2027, after the Supreme Court of Virginia threw out the 2022 verdict in January 2026; on 16 October the court let Appian present a reasonable-royalty damages theory it was barred from using at the original trial. Appian then rose 27.2% in a week. But Pegasystems rose 5.4% in the same window and ServiceNow 12.0%, so the likelier reading is a broad software rally that Appian led at roughly twice the move. Short interest of about 3.79m shares, some 5.3% of shares outstanding, is too small to be the cause.

Dearer than it was, cheaper than ServiceNow

Price-to-gross-profit has gone from 3.07x in early May to 6.05x, against gross profit that grew 20.8%: the price has roughly doubled relative to the profit it buys. On forward earnings, Appian's 47.7x is nearly twice ServiceNow's 25.8x.

Measured against forward gross profit the order inverts. Appian's 6.00x sits below ServiceNow's 8.92x, where 20.5% revenue growth came with GAAP operating income down 32.7%, and far below Snowflake's 22.97x, where 35.1% growth comes with a $263.0m quarterly operating loss.

Pegasystems, at 3.91x forward gross profit, is the cheapest of the four, and 3.1% revenue growth with 7% annual contract value growth says why. "Our technology is differentiated and very relevant to the times we are in. However, candidly, our execution this year has not kept up," founder-chief executive Alan Trefler said on 21 October. Salesforce, at 4.92x, grew 10.8%.

What the quarter can and cannot pay for

The operating results account for the direction and the timing of the advance, and for Appian being the one name in its neighborhood that gained in the week its peers gave up ground. They do not account for the full doubling of the valuation, because the acceleration is one quarter old. In the June quarter revenue growth slowed to 19.1%, GAAP gross margin compressed to 71.2%, and unsatisfied performance obligations of $625.3m grew 13%, slower than revenue. The raised fourth-quarter revenue guide of $233–237m implies about 16% growth, partly on a 2–3 point currency headwind, and consensus has 10.8% revenue growth in 2027 while earnings per share still rise 31%. What is being bought at 47.7x forward earnings is margin, and the sell side said so: William Blair upgraded to Outperform while DA Davidson lifted its target to $60 from $42 and TD Cowen to $58 from $50, both keeping their ratings.

Neither company's balance sheet carries the claim against Pegasystems, and nothing changes hands before a verdict. Until a jury meets in January, the invoice has to do the work on its own — and Tanjga, asked whether this quarter's expansion rate becomes the new baseline, declined to guide to the metric at all.

Originating hypothesis

single ticker multi month band reversal · subject: APPN

Appian is the quietest emerging bull in technology in this snapshot — it has climbed strongly bearish to strongly bullish on both its 180-day and 365-day bands and mildly bullish to strongly bullish on 90 days as of 6 November without ever appearing in a mover list, which is to say a multi-quarter, gradual-intensity re-rating that is still in motion rather than a pop — and it is happening in the one corner of software the market has spent a year marking down, with ServiceNow, Salesforce, Palo Alto, Cloudflare and Snowflake all stepping strongly bullish to mildly bullish in the last week, so the question is what inside one business is being re-priced: whether Appian's low-code process-automation platform has crossed into durable cloud-subscription growth with positive and widening operating margin on a delivery base it has deliberately stopped expanding, or whether a company that bills partly for professional services and carries a long-running trade-secrets award against Pegasystems is being lifted by non-operating items and a short-interest unwind rather than by its own meter.

Rocket Lab's New Rocket Slipped to 2027, Leaving Its Satellite-Parts Business to Carry It

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Rocket Lab's June quarter delivered the operating leverage the sector promised: gross profit grew 82.3% against revenue growth of 62.0%, and gross margin reached 36.1%. The shares have still round-tripped a full year, and the first flight of the Neutron rocket, the asset that underwrote the premium, has moved out a year.

That leaves the solar cells, reaction wheels and satellite buses of Space Systems to support a price of 88.9 times forward gross profit, five times what Planet Labs costs on the same measure. Planet went the other way: revenue growth accelerated four straight quarters, adjusted EBITDA turned positive, and trailing sales fell to 11.3x from 39.4x in early May.

Rocket Lab's markdown has a calendar behind it. Planet's has no equivalent cause in its own numbers, and Firefly's rally came from a licensing announcement whose upfront payment is half in a customer's stock.

Rocket Lab reports third-quarter results on 9 November with the vehicle that justified its valuation no longer part of next year. The company has pushed the first flight of Neutron, its eight-tonne medium-lift rocket, into 2027 because of problems with the carbon-fiber parts used in its fuel tanks. What has to carry the price in the meantime is hardware: Space Systems billed $189.5m of a record $234.1m in June-quarter revenue, against $44.6m at Launch Services.

Record order books across listed space have been read as contracted revenue awaiting delivery, and Rocket Lab's is the largest among these names, reaching $2.36bn in June, up 137% year on year and weighted 58.5% to Space Systems. The past month has tested that reading. Buyers of these shares are paying for gross profit already banked rather than for signed backlog: on forward gross profit, five listed space names span roughly 10 times at York Space Systems to 89 times at Rocket Lab.

The leverage is real; the catalyst moved

Rocket Lab, the only listed company that both reaches orbit and builds the spacecraft and components that fly there, is converting. June-quarter gross profit rose 82.3%, faster than revenue, and gross margin reached 36.1% against 32.1% a year earlier. Annual gross margin has climbed from 9.0% in 2022 to 34.4% in 2025. The credentials behind that are specific: a prime contract worth $816m for 18 missile-warning satellites under the Space Development Agency's third tranche, plus solar cells, reaction wheels and star trackers sold into other primes' missions.

It still lost $57.5m at the operating line in the quarter and consumes cash, with a trailing free cash flow yield of -1.17%. Consensus does not have net income turning positive until 2028. Launch, the activity the equity story is named after, recognized about $7.4m per flight in the June quarter, a figure derived from segment revenue and the six Electrons flown; fifteen Electron missions flew in 2026 through 26 September. At 88.9 times forward gross profit the shares cost five times Planet Labs' 17.6 times.

Neutron was the asset meant to close that distance.

Planet turned cash-positive as its multiple fell by two-thirds

Planet Labs, which sells daily global imagery from a constellation of more than 150 satellites to governments, agriculture, finance and insurance customers, moved the opposite way. Revenue growth accelerated for four consecutive quarters to 58.1% in the July quarter, on gross margin of 56.6%, and the operating margin narrowed to -11.6% from -24.5%. The company posted adjusted EBITDA of $13.9m and guided fiscal 2027 revenue to $430m-$441m. Trailing sales now cost 11.3x, against 39.4x in early May.

The limit on that case is the order book. Backlog was $814.9m, up only 11% year on year, far behind revenue; one-time satellite handovers, led by the first Pelican delivered to the Swedish Armed Forces, were 12% of July-quarter revenue against 1% a year earlier, and existing customers expanded spend by 9%. The recurring imagery meter is growing more slowly than the headline.

Firefly's largest order is payable partly in a customer's shares

Firefly Aerospace, which sells the Alpha small launcher, the Blue Ghost lunar lander and defense software, rose 36.8% on 6 November on about five times its prior ten-session average volume after announcing a licensing and supply agreement with the satellite-to-phone operator AST SpaceMobile. It pays $800m upfront, half cash and half the buyer's Class A stock, $250m for 36 engines and $250m of engineering services over three years. The buyer's shares are down 50.5% over twelve months, so roughly $400m of that upfront moves with a counterparty's price, and the rocket the engines are for, Blacklift, is not targeted to fly until late 2028.

Chief executive Jason Kim called it a "bold transformation deal," saying "the engine is the hardest, highest-stakes decision they make." Firefly's own June quarter scaled revenue to $117.7m while gross margin compressed to 20.3% from 25.7%, and its trailing free cash flow yield of -9.39% is the worst of this group. It reports on 10 November, with analysts near $103m after a national-security customer pulled a hardware order into the prior quarter.

Karman Holdings, which builds payload fairings and solid-propulsion units for munitions and missile-defense programs, gained 42.1% the same session on a funded quarter: revenue of $202m, up 66%, with full-year guidance raised to $755m-$760m.

Redwire converted; York's buyer changed how it buys

Redwire, which builds spacecraft platforms, sensors and solar arrays for US and European government customers, printed third-quarter revenue of $109.0m, up 5.4%, at a record 32.3% gross margin with gross profit up 109.5%, and backlog of $619.7m, up 75%. "Gross margin improvement has been a key focus area this year... Space segment on an apples-to-apples basis was at 4% last year and 18% for this quarter, whereas our defense tech business was 45% last year and 50% this year," chief financial officer Chris Edmunds told the 5 November call. The shares fell 17.6% that day and rose 16.5% the next.

York Space Systems, which delivers satellite buses to national-security buyers, is the cheapest here at 2.1x trailing sales and 0.46x book, the only one below book, and the only one where consensus expects no growth: 2026 revenue of $393.3m, up 2.5%, after it cut guidance by $180m at the midpoint when its main buyer shifted to indefinite-delivery contracts awarded slowly. Its shares have trended down without a break since 3 June.

Where the markdown matches the business

Rocket Lab's quarter does not explain its 25.6% fall over thirty days, but its calendar does: a premium written against a first Neutron flight loses its justification when that flight moves a year, leaving a hardware business earning 36% gross margins priced at nearly nine times what Redwire's gross profit costs. Planet's markdown has no equivalent cause in its own numbers, only the slower backlog beneath the accelerating revenue. Firefly's gain came from an announcement with a 2028 flight date and a stock component attached.

Direction came from outside. The Nasdaq Composite fell 5.52% on 4 November, its worst session since April 2025, as investors priced a slower artificial-intelligence buildout after the midterms. Magnitude did not: appropriated primes Lockheed Martin and Northrop Grumman rose 11.4% and 8.4% over the same thirty days while these pure-plays fell 20% to 47%, placing the markdown in speculative space and not in defense demand. On 6 November every listed space name rose while the S&P 500 tracker fell 2.4%, and Momentus, Satellogic, Virgin Galactic and Sidus Space climbed with no company news of their own, so the likelier reading of that session is a squeeze ranked by volatility.

Backlog is the easiest number in this sector to grow and the slowest to prove. Rocket Lab has guided the September quarter to gross margin below the level it printed in June, the shape of a hardware business absorbing new programs. The rocket was supposed to pay for the parts shop; for another year, the parts shop pays for the rocket.

Grid Dynamics, a San Ramon, California engineering firm that builds digital and AI software for Fortune 1000 retailers, banks, telecom carriers and consumer-goods companies, closed September with a smaller workforce than a year earlier and the biggest revenue quarter in its history. Revenue reached $114.3m, up 9.7% year on year. Headcount fell to 4,559 from 4,838, reported gross margin hit 40.3% against 33.3%, and revenue per employee passed $100,000 for the first time.

For two years the market priced enterprise systems integrators as generative artificial intelligence's first casualty: fewer billable people, compressed pyramids, clients building in-house. October reversed that. All eight names on the enterprise consulting shelf rose over the 30 days to 6 November, a median of 31.4%, while the S&P 500 exchange-traded fund fell 5.4% and Nvidia fell 21.4%. The trigger was Accenture's 1 October results, which lifted the battered group on the reading that corporations still need outside help fitting AI into older systems. The order books support that reading. The revenue guidance does not, and only one firm on the shelf has shown the conversion arriving in the numbers.

The quarter where headcount and revenue came apart

Grid Dynamics' margin gain was priced, not cut. Non-GAAP gross margin of 40.6% was 370 basis points wider than the prior quarter, which management attributed to AI project mix, higher billing rates and better utilization; the company hit its annual 300-point expansion goal a quarter early. Non-GAAP EBITDA of $22.9m, a 20% margin, beat its own guidance range of $16.5m to $17.5m. AI revenue grew 45% year on year for a third consecutive quarter and now approaches 32% of the total, with 93% of engineers trained on AI software-development methods.

"In a sector where headcount and revenue have historically moved in lockstep, Grid Dynamics is demonstrating that the two can and should be decoupled," chief executive Leonard Livschitz said on the 29 October call. "12 months ago, AI-native delivery was the direction we were heading toward. Today, it's how we deliver." Management guides 2027 to healthy double-digit growth after a run of single-digit quarters.

Why the big offshore firms are conceding the same savings

At the large names, the order book and the revenue line point in opposite directions. Infosys, the Bengaluru firm behind the Topaz AI suite and the Finacle banking platform, won 19 large deals worth $5.2bn of total contract value in its September quarter, 62% of it net new and its second-highest quarterly haul in five years. It then cut fiscal 2027 revenue growth guidance to 1-2% in constant currency on 23 October. "Growth was below our expectations," chief financial officer Jayesh Sanghrajka told investors that day. "Volume was softer, and price increases were lower than anticipated amidst high competitive intensity." Infosys added 4,400 net staff in the quarter and says walking away from deals priced below its return standards costs 1.8 to 2.0 percentage points of this year's growth.

Cognizant, the Teaneck, New Jersey outsourcer serving banks, healthcare payers, manufacturers and retailers, shows the mix improving as the total shrinks: trailing twelve-month bookings of $28.7bn were up 4% at a 1.3 times book-to-bill, but bookings signed in the third quarter alone fell 7% and full-year constant-currency growth guidance came down to 4.0-4.4% from 4.0-5.5%. New and expansion work is now 60% of the bookings mix against 48%, and fixed-price plus transaction-based work reached 58% of revenue, up 140 points on the year. "AI is a tailwind. There is no doubt in my mind," chief executive Ravi Kumar said on 29 October. The earnings line tells a quieter story: adjusted earnings per share are guided up 8-10% on dollar revenue up 4.5-4.9%, with diluted shares down from 497m to 450m since the end of 2024.

Accenture, which sells strategy, technology and managed operations to large corporations and governments, booked a record $84.5bn in fiscal 2026 and 141 deals above $100m against 129. It is also hiring, with headcount up 5% to 814,000, and it guided fiscal 2027 to 3-6% local-currency growth against 5% delivered, with 2 to 2.5 points of that from acquisitions. Its revenue per person, roughly $91,100, sits about 48% above Cognizant's and Infosys's, which is the structural line on this shelf between the firms holding price and the firms conceding it. EPAM, the Newtown, Pennsylvania platform engineering firm, is the only member to raise guidance inside the window, lifting organic constant-currency growth to 2.5-2.7% on 5 November with gross profit up 17.4% on revenue up 2.3%.

How much of the move the businesses explain

Not most of it. Globant and CGI disclosed nothing at all in the window and rose 33.1% and 18.6%; the likelier reading is a rotation out of AI hardware into the de-rated services layer, which Merrill's Chief Investment Office described on 2 November as capital moving from AI builders toward AI adopters. Grid Dynamics is the exception where the business moved first: it rose 53.6% in the session after its 29 October report, close to close, to a 52-week high, with intraday reports of about 34%. Over twelve months the shelf's median return is minus 8.2%.

The cause of the split is contractual. A firm billing person-months must hand the client the productivity it finds, because the client can see the hours; a firm selling an outcome with a smaller team books the difference as margin. That is why Infosys's utilization and offshore mix carry its margin while price falls, and why Grid Dynamics' margin came with rates going up.

The market is paying accordingly. Grid Dynamics trades at 24.3 times forward earnings and 6.7 times trailing gross profit, the dearest forward multiple of the names here, against Accenture's 17.75 times forward, itself up from 14.1 times trailing at the 31 August fiscal year-end and still 29% below a five-year year-end median of 26.9 times. Cognizant's 11.2 times forward remains below the 14-15 times it carried in May. Grid Dynamics' trailing price-to-earnings of 145.6 times says nothing, on minimal reported net income.

One thing financed the decoupling that is worth watching more than the margin. Grid Dynamics' top ten customers now supply 63.3% of revenue, up from 58.3%, which management attributes to clients cutting their vendor lists down to a few partners. Chief financial officer Anil Doradla told investors on 29 October the mandate now is to scale, with an appetite for larger acquisitions in 2027. A shorter client list is a faster way to grow revenue per head, and a harder one to defend if one of those clients decides it can do the work itself.
