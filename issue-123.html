Cisco's Growth Hit a 15-Year High and the Shares Fell Through the Guidance Raise

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Enterprise networking was marked down through November on the belief that the artificial-intelligence premium was deflating out of switch demand. The reported quarters point the other way: the selling scaled with AI exposure and skipped the companies furthest from it.

Cisco grew revenue 24% to $18.52bn in the October quarter, lifted operating margin to 30.2% from 22.6%, raised its forecast for the year to July 2027 by $2.5bn and now guides hyperscaler AI infrastructure revenue to $9bn. Its shares still fell 15% over the 30 days to 25 November, nearly all of it before the numbers were public.

The business explains none of that. What it does explain is the split: Cisco gave up 350 basis points of product gross margin to hardware mix and memory costs, while Extreme Networks, with components locked through calendar 2027, expanded margin and raised guidance, and Ubiquiti grew 39.9% with margin up too.

Cisco told investors on 12 November that it had just grown faster than at any point in more than 15 years, and by then its shares had already done most of their falling. Revenue of $18.52bn for the quarter ended 24 October was up 24% from a year earlier, operating margin reached 30.2% against 22.6%, and the company lifted its revenue forecast for the year to July 2027 by $2.5bn, to $74.8-75.8bn.

"In Q1, we delivered record quarterly revenue of $18.5 billion, up 24% year-over-year and record non-GAAP earnings per share of $1.40, up 40% year-over-year. These are our strongest top and bottom line growth rates in over 15 years," chair and chief executive Chuck Robbins said on the 12 November call. The stock closed 5.1% lower the following session.

November's markdown across enterprise networking was read as the artificial-intelligence premium draining out of switching and routing, the incumbent vendors assumed to be losing the rack to merchant silicon. The reported quarters describe something narrower. The selling scaled with AI exposure and skipped the businesses furthest from it: over the 30 days to 25 November, Ubiquiti, which ships UniFi gateways, Wi-Fi and switching outright through distributors and online retailers, rose 6.6%, and Extreme Networks, a campus and edge vendor mid-migration into subscription, rose 1.8%, while Cisco fell 15.4% and Arista Networks, whose switches go into hyperscaler AI fabrics, fell 16.9%.

The campus grew; the AI book grew three times faster

Cisco's product revenue rose 32% in the quarter. Networking was up 43% with triple-digit growth in AI infrastructure, Nexus data-centre switches for AI up 47%, and the ordinary enterprise refresh that funds the whole franchise kept growing: campus up 14%, enterprise orders up 15%, public sector up 13%. Service-provider and cloud orders rose 47%. Total product orders were up 21% year over year, with subscriptions supplying 44% of revenue, and the contracted book got bigger in the quarter the shares were marked down: remaining performance obligations of $46.2bn, up 8%, against deferred revenue of $30.1bn.

Hyperscaler AI infrastructure revenue is now guided to $9bn for the year, raised from $7.5bn a quarter earlier and more than double the prior year, on 18 design wins across five quarters. "So the top line moving from 12% all the way up to 19% and the bottom line moving from 14% growth to 27% growth," chief financial officer Mark Patterson told analysts of the raise.

What the AI ports cost, and who avoided paying

One line moved the wrong way. Non-GAAP product gross margin came in at 63.7%, down 350 basis points, which Cisco attributed to a heavier hardware mix — hardware revenue grew 50% — and to elevated memory costs. The input shock behind that is severe: the contract price of a 16Gb DDR5 chip went from $8.70 in October 2025 to $48 by September 2026. Cisco signed a multiyear memory agreement and reported no material supply constraints.

Extreme bought earlier. "Our components are secured through calendar 2027 while our competitors are quoting extended lead times," chief executive Edward Meyercord said on 28 October. Its gross margin expanded to 62.1% from 60.6%, product revenue rose 12% in a seventh consecutive double-digit quarter, software-as-a-service annual recurring revenue reached $264.7m, up 22%, and management raised full-year growth guidance from 8% to 10%. Ubiquiti, which holds no earnings calls and carries no recurring-revenue line at all, grew quarterly revenue 39.9% to $1,026.2m with gross margin up 131 basis points to 47.3%; enterprise technology supplied $941.3m of it, roughly 92%, on a payroll of 1,818.

The selling came before the numbers

Cisco went from $111.38 on 27 October to $93.85 on 6 November, six days before it reported anything. The datable catalyst in between was a valuation call rather than an order-book event: on 5 November Morgan Stanley cut the stock to Equal Weight from Overweight and its target to $120 from $135, arguing AI-driven growth was already priced and the AI margin mix left less upside. Cisco fell 7% that day, Arista 10.8%, Ciena 15%. On 20 November the group was hit again together, Ubiquiti down 10.9% and Extreme 9.2% in a single session.

So the results account for none of Cisco's decline and the guidance accounts for less. What the quarters do account for is the split inside the group. Cisco is repriced as a hyperscaler supplier because it has chosen to be one, and it books those ports at a margin concession it has disclosed; Extreme and Ubiquiti sell into campus budgets that are still spending and had their components bought in advance. Cisco now trades at 17.1x forward earnings against 24.9x trailing, against roughly 28x after its May advance, with consensus earnings of $5.51 a share for the year sitting inside the company's own raised range. Extreme carries the group's widest compression, 17.7x forward against 60.9x trailing, on a GAAP operating margin that nearly doubled. Ubiquiti is the dearest of the three on a margin-neutral measure, at 19.9x forward price to gross profit against Cisco's 7.7x, and its forward estimates rest on a single analyst.

Patterson told investors to "expect a slight gross margin headwind as we move through FY 2027 as we address these very high growth opportunities." That is the sentence the next three quarters will test. Cisco has told the market what the $9bn of AI revenue costs it; the open question is whether the campus base keeps growing fast enough to pay for it.

Linde's Take-or-Pay Gas Contracts Kept It Out of November's Chip-Materials Selloff

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Six suppliers to the same chip fabs sit on one shelf of this watchlist, and in the month to 25 November it broke in half. The two industrial-gas members rose; the four materials, chemistry and equipment members fell together by roughly a tenth. Nothing in the order books explains the break. Linde's electronics sales grew 15% last quarter, Entegris's revenue 25% and MKS Inc.'s 43%, and the two semiconductor names guided the December quarter higher still.

What differs is how the money is contracted: gas goes out under long-term take-or-pay supply agreements with no artificial-intelligence premium attached, while consumables and subsystems carry one the market can reprice. The exception is Photronics, which widened its own fourth-quarter guidance citing uncertainty and cut capital spending plans. The leverage comparison inverts too: Entegris paid $43.9m of net interest last quarter against MKS's $33m.

Linde, which pipes industrial and specialty gases to chipmakers, refiners and hospitals, told investors in late October that electronics was its fastest-growing end market, with sales there up 15% from a year earlier. Through the month that followed, its shares rose. The companies selling filters, slurries, vacuum pumps and plating chemistry into those same fabs were marked down by about a tenth.

The fall was read as the artificial-intelligence materials trade deflating. Every order book on the shelf says otherwise, which leaves the form of the contract, not the state of demand, as the thing that separated the winners from the losers. Gas for a fab is sold under long-term take-or-pay agreements: the revenue arrives whether or not the customer's capital plan accelerates, and it therefore carries no premium for an acceleration that can later be revoked. A filter qualified into a process flow, or a power supply booked into a tool build, carries exactly that premium.

Two ways to be paid by the same fab

Linde has roughly $2bn of electronics projects under construction in the United States, Korea and China, committed $1bn on 31 July 2026 to expand its Phoenix site for a major chipmaker, and lifted full-year adjusted earnings guidance to a range of $17.82 to $17.92 a share. It gained 3.4% over the 30 days to 25 November; Air Products, the other gas member, added 1.0%. Over the same window Entegris fell 8.5%, MKS 9.1%, Element Solutions 8.7% and Photronics 10.2%.

The gas pair's calm is not better news from the fabs. It is less news of the kind that can be taken back.

The consumables annuity is the levered half

Entegris, which supplies microcontamination filters, ultra-pure chemicals, polishing consumables and wafer-handling products to logic and memory makers, reported September-quarter sales of $1,006m, up 25%, with gross margin at 49.7% and operating margin at 22.9%. Book-to-bill ran above 1.0 and backlog roughly doubled year on year. Guidance for the December quarter is $1.025bn to $1.055bn, about 26% above a year earlier.

"Unit-driven revenue accelerated from approximately 6% in the first quarter to 20% in the third quarter, that is roughly twice the rate of underlying wafer start growth we expect for the year," chief executive Dave Reeder told investors on 29 October. The company says about three-quarters of its business is unit-driven and roughly a quarter to a third capital-expenditure-linked; the two figures do not sum cleanly. What complicates the annuity story is that the capex-linked line grew more than 35% in the quarter, faster than the consumption half, so a pause in fab construction would register more than the framing suggests.

It would also land on the more indebted balance sheet of the two. Entegris paid $43.9m of net interest in the quarter on $3.26bn of long-term debt, at 2.9x net leverage, against MKS's $33m, down from $45m a year earlier, at 2.4x. China fell to 18% of Entegris's nine-month sales from 21%.

MKS says its chemistry lines are full

MKS Inc., which sells vacuum and pressure control, radio-frequency power, lasers and printed-circuit-board drilling systems alongside the Atotech plating-chemistry business, grew September-quarter revenue 43% to $1,413m at a 23.1% operating margin, and guided December to $1.49bn give or take $40m. Chemistry equipment sales rose more than 75% year on year. "Current capacity is sold out through 2027, and to meet accelerated chemistry equipment demand, we are expanding capacity in Guangzhou, China," chief executive John T.C. Lee said on 5 November. On 19 October the company agreed to sell its Ophir optics unit to TTM Technologies for $650m, at more than 18x that unit's projected adjusted earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization, against MKS's own 13.0x trailing enterprise multiple.

Of the four marked-down names, one earns it on disclosure. Photronics, which makes photomasks, widened fiscal fourth-quarter revenue guidance to $207m-227m citing increased uncertainty, and cut capital spending plans to $255m-305m from $330m. Element Solutions, whose MacDermid Alpha arm competes with Atotech in plating chemistry, grew electronics sales 18% organically; its drawdown dates to the collapse of its $14.5bn sale to Solstice Advanced Materials, abandoned by both boards on 27 August with no break fee.

What the quarters do and do not explain

None of the 30-day decline is traceable to the protagonists' own reporting. Both beat and raised. The wafer-fab-equipment makers they supply fell two to three times harder over the same days, Teradyne 31% and Applied Materials 24%, and the Nasdaq 100 entered its first bear market since April 2025 on 25 November after a tariff announcement and hotter inflation data. The likelier reading is a repricing of the discount rate applied to equipment-cycle revenue, which is precisely the revenue the gas contracts do not contain.

The repricing has been large. MKS trades at 12.4x forward earnings against 20.6x trailing and roughly 29.6x in early May; Entegris at 29.2x forward against 49.9x trailing and about 41x in May, when its 2027 ramp was still a forecast rather than a reported quarter. Entegris remains the dearest name on the shelf, cheap only against its own history. Every member is still higher over twelve months, Entegris by 74%.

The next hard figures are roughly nine weeks out, and neither company has fixed a date. Before them sits MKS's investor day on 14 December, where a management team that says its chemistry capacity is committed through 2027 meets a market that has halved what it will pay for the earnings.

CrowdStrike agreed on 23 November to buy a rival in network security entirely with its own shares, and lost roughly $21bn of market value in the two sessions that followed. The target was Zscaler, the zero-trust network access vendor, valued at about $43.9bn at a fixed 1.135 CrowdStrike shares per Zscaler share, a 34% premium that leaves existing CrowdStrike holders with roughly 84.1% of the combined company. The issuance of some 191m new shares, close to a fifth of the existing count, mattered more to the market than a quarter that beat on revenue at $1.58bn with raised guidance.

That was the last act of a month in which the whole group was marked down. Okta, Cloudflare, Rubrik, CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, Fortinet and Zscaler all fell over the 30 days to 25 November, by an average of 17.1%, with Fortinet worst at -27.2% and Okta mildest at -9.0%. The fall has been read as security's AI premium deflating — as evidence that agentic software is about to eat seats and budgets. Across three companies that are paid in three genuinely different ways, the contracted revenue says that happened at exactly one of them.

The two catalysts were company-specific

Fortinet lit the fuse. Product revenue rose 37% to $765m in the September quarter, a sharp step down from 52% growth the quarter before, and the shares fell more than 20% despite the third full-year guidance raise of the year, to $8.125-8.215bn. Both events landed on top of the 5 November session, the biggest technology selloff since Covid, which repriced anything whose value sits in profits expected years out.

Cloudflare's meter ran faster than its price

Cloudflare, which bills consumption on a global edge network rather than seats, stepped down out of its strongest uptrend on 3 November, two sessions before it reported. Revenue growth had accelerated for three straight quarters, 33.5% to 35.9% to 39.2%, reaching $782.5m. Remaining performance obligations hit $2.877bn, up 34%, with the portion due inside a year up 37%. Dollar-based net retention of 122% was a four-year high, and the company passed 5,000 customers paying more than $100,000 a year, now 75% of revenue.

The beat came from variable usage: customers burned through prepaid pools of funds faster than modeled and renewed early at larger sizes. "Between July 1st and today, we added approximately 5% of the entire web to Cloudflare," chief executive Matthew Prince told investors on 5 November. "That's wild." Chief financial officer Thomas Seifert guided GAAP profitability to the fourth quarter, against an investor-day target of 2028 at the latest. On a non-GAAP basis the quarter earned $137.1m of operating income; the GAAP statement shows a $67.9m operating loss.

What the shares still carry is the level. Cloudflare trades at 47.6x forward gross profit, roughly three times Rubrik's 16.4x, with consensus revenue of $3.914bn in 2027 required to justify it. Gross margin fell 290bp to 72.4% as lower-margin developer products took more cost of revenue.

Rubrik's net-new line accelerated into a 20% fall

Rubrik, which sells multi-year data-protection and cyber-recovery subscriptions priced on data under management, fell 20.5%. Its last reported quarter showed subscription ARR of $1.66bn, up 33%, with net new subscription ARR of roughly $96m, up 35% year over year, and cloud ARR up 39% to $1.48bn as the maintenance base converts. Full-year guidance went up, to $1.88-1.885bn of ARR and $323-333m of free cash flow. The growth is organic; the Strata Identity acquisition added nothing to subscription ARR.

The case against is two lines: revenue growth has decelerated from 46.3% to 37.9% over three quarters, and gross margin compressed 250bp to 78.4%.

Okta is where the books agree with the shares

Okta sells per-user cloud identity, and per-user is the model the agentic thesis threatens. Revenue growth has slowed for four consecutive quarters to 10.6%. The twelve-month subscription backlog was $2.585bn, up 14%, guided to 11-12% growth, and net retention sits at 107%. Chief financial officer Brett Tighe told investors in August that AI-agent contribution will not be material this fiscal year; Okta discloses no machine-identity unit count at all, even as CyberArk's 2025 landscape put machine identities at roughly 82 times human ones.

What Okta did deliver is margin: quarterly operating margin went from 3.1% to 13.3% over four quarters, and full-year revenue guidance was raised to $3.216-3.226bn. At 51.4x forward earnings, against roughly 36x in mid-July when the shares were at $139.53, the multiple is paid for that margin expansion and not for growth, since consensus models 10.0% revenue growth next year.

What the reported books can and cannot explain

One of the three de-ratings has a basis in the numbers the company reported. Okta's decelerating backlog on a per-seat book is exactly the configuration that compresses an identity multiple, which is why its own guidance already embeds the slowdown and why a 9% fall barely touches a stock up 146.8% over twelve months. At Cloudflare and Rubrik the contracted lines moved the other way, and the likelier reading of their falls is duration repricing plus the dilution shock, not demand. The split has a cause in how each is paid: a consumption meter and a data-volume subscription both grow when agents proliferate, while a seat price only grows if someone buys seats.

Two of the three answer inside a week. Rubrik reports on 30 November against its own guide of $1,829-1,839m of subscription ARR, and Okta on 1 December. The company that told the market to expect less is the one whose shares have least to lose.

Arm reported record licensing revenue on 4 November, and the shares fell about 12% the next session. The reason was the other line. Royalties, the fractional per-chip fee the Cambridge company collects when a licensee's silicon ships, grew 8% to $670m against analyst estimates nearer $705m, and the finance chief guided December-quarter royalty growth to "low single digits" year on year.

The market has treated Arm as the cleanest claim on artificial-intelligence chip design: no fabs, a 97.1% gross margin, a per-chip toll on an industry drawing more silicon every year. The September quarter tested that and found something narrower. Arm's royalty is a percentage of chip value, and chip value in handsets is being squeezed from the inside by the cost of memory.

Volume went up and value did not

Licensees shipped 10.5bn chips in the quarter, up 1.5bn year on year. Arm's edge-AI royalty revenue — the fees from phones, wearables and other devices running inference outside the data center — was flat. Management's explanation was elevated DRAM and NAND prices raising handset bills of materials and suppressing mix, with smartphone units forecast flat next year on IDC and partner consensus. The read on 5 November was that mobile application processors remain a large share of royalties, so the AI buildout subtracts from Arm's royalty base through the memory market even as it adds through hyperscaler CPUs.

That second channel is real and large. "Data center royalties doubled year-over-year, and Arm-based server market share at the top hyperscalers has more than tripled from 15% in calendar 2024 to more than 50% in calendar 2025," chief executive Rene Haas told investors on 4 November. It is not yet big enough to carry the whole line.

A record licence quarter that pays in 2028

Licence revenue rose 50% to $773m, annualized contract value grew 12%, and Arm added three Arm Total Access subscriptions, taking the active count above 60, plus four compute-subsystem licences. Arm's own description of the cycle is that licences turn into design wins and royalties 12 to 24 months later. On the chip Arm is building itself, chief financial officer Jason Child said: "In the past 90 days, we have signed purchase orders from multiple customers and signed supply agreements with companies in our supply chain, giving us increased confidence in achieving up to $2 billion across fiscal 2027 and 2028." He guided its initial gross margin to the high 30s to low 40s, against roughly 97% on licensing.

So the strength is deferred and lower-margin, while the softness is current. Group GAAP net income fell 45% to $130m as spending on server silicon landed below the gross-margin line.

The contracted annuities got marked down with it

Arm has lost roughly a third of its value in a month, yet it is the only one of the three chip-design names still higher over twelve months, up about 12%. At 72.2x forward earnings — against 185.6x trailing, and the roughly 80x-plus it carried in May — the de-rating has moved an extreme multiple to a high one, while consensus still asks revenue to accelerate to $8.42bn in fiscal 2028, up 37%, from a royalty line guided to low single digits.

The design-tools pair is the opposite case. Cadence, which sells chip-design software and Palladium emulation hardware, grew revenue 22% to $1.633bn in the September quarter, lifted full-year guidance on 26 October, and reported a record $8.2bn backlog with $4.2bn of it contracted to convert within twelve months — about 57% of its own next-twelve-months revenue outlook, on 2026 guidance of $6.387bn to $6.417bn and 2027 consensus of $7.34bn. It trades at 35.1x forward earnings against 58.3x trailing and the 50x-to-55x it was granted in May. Synopsys, whose chip IP competes with Arm's, raised fiscal 2026 revenue guidance to $9.69bn-$9.74bn and carries $10.9bn of backlog; it sits at 21.4x forward against 71.1x trailing, the compression measuring how much of the Ansys-depressed earnings base is expected to normalize. Neither order book deteriorated. The likelier reading of their markdown is rate and AI-capex repricing — Mizuho cut its 2027 AI capital-expenditure forecast to $1.4trn from $1.6trn — compounded on 25 November by a report that Huawei and Phlexing demonstrated an integrated domestic Chinese design tool chain on a real chip project, which the same report says does not replace every design workflow. Synopsys fell 7.0% that session; Cadence was down 0.5%.

Arm's December quarter is the one number that will settle whether a phone's silicon content can grow while memory eats the budget. Everything else in its results is a bet on chips that have not been designed yet.

XPeng, which builds intelligent electric sedans and sport-utility vehicles in China and sells charging, insurance brokerage and financing alongside them, reported an 18.9% group gross margin for its September quarter. The cars did not earn it. Vehicle gross margin was 13.1%, a point better than the June quarter, and the 5.8-point gap to the reported group figure is supplied by a services-and-others line that is about a tenth of revenue.

For two years the three listed Chinese pure-plays have been marked down as one thing, on the view that domestic price competition had destroyed what a delivered car earns. Over twelve months to the latest close XPeng fell 59%, NIO 53% and Li Auto 35%. The reported quarters no longer support one verdict: on the cars themselves, the three are separating, and the valuations do not reflect which is which.

The tenth of XPeng's revenue that carries a third of its gross profit

Services and others brought in RMB2.65bn in the September quarter, up 13.7% year over year, and the filing attributes that increase primarily to carbon-credit trading rather than to the driver-assistance subscriptions the line is usually associated with. On reported inputs the segment ran at roughly a 67% margin and supplied about 38% of group gross profit on 10.9% of revenue; in the June quarter, at a disclosed 75.1% segment margin, it supplied roughly half.

The cars, meanwhile, earn about RMB24,000 of gross profit each: RMB21.63bn of vehicle sales at 13.1% across 118,390 deliveries. XPeng is growing hard into that thin unit economics, guiding fourth-quarter deliveries of 169,000 to 174,000 units, up 45% to 50%, and the profitable part of the growth is abroad. "Our international business is XPeng's strongest driver of growth and profitability," chairman He Xiaopeng told investors on November 16, setting a quarterly overseas target above 40,000 units. Management says overseas gross margin is more than double domestic. The group margin still fell 180 basis points sequentially on artificial-intelligence chip costs and geographic mix, and the operating line lost RMB914.5m. The shares trade at 0.71x trailing revenue but 2.09x book value, double Li Auto's asset backing on the thinner car.

Li Auto's per-car gross profit roughly doubled in one quarter

Li Auto, which sells premium family SUVs and multi-purpose vehicles under its own retail network, spent three quarters in genuine per-unit collapse: vehicle margin of 6.1% in the March quarter and 9.4% in June, against 19.4% a year before. The September quarter put it back to 15.3%, within two-tenths of a point of the prior year. Vehicle sales of RMB27.1bn across 99,964 deliveries imply an average selling price near RMB271,000, up about 11% sequentially, and gross profit per car of roughly RMB41,000 against roughly RMB23,000 in June. Deliveries rose 7.2%. Free cash flow turned positive at RMB2.0bn, the company's first such quarter, and management guided to a return to net profit in the fourth quarter.

The mix moved up, not down, and it is no longer an extended-range story: battery-electric models were more than half of sales. "The monthly production of i6 will reach 20,000 units in November," chief financial officer Johnny Tie Li said on November 25. The niche Li Auto built did contract, by 13.1% in the first half to 504,000 units, with AITO, co-developed by Seres and Huawei, taking three of the top four slots on the strength of Huawei's retail network. Li Auto's answer is vertical integration: its Lianshan Electric unit, launched in October, supplies the batteries in all 2026 i6 and MEGA trims. At 0.67x trailing revenue and 1.10x book, the shares carry the cheapest asset backing of the three; its 4.95x price-to-gross-profit is the dearest, because the denominator capitalizes the trough.

Three tax changes, pointed at different companies

From January, China halved its purchase-tax exemption: buyers pay 5%, relief capped at RMB15,000 per vehicle against RMB30,000 before. A cap bites hardest at the top of the range, which is Li Auto's range. A consumption tax on lithium-ion batteries waived since 2015 returned at 2% in September and goes to 4% a year later, hitting every domestic maker per car. And from April 2027 the export rebate on new-energy vehicles drops from 13% to 9%, which lands on exactly the lever XPeng is using. Its own management flagged all three as 2027 margin risks.

The results explain part of the separation and not the whole of it. Li Auto's 11% gain on the day of its print is a direct response to a car margin that recovered; XPeng's de-rating is consistent with the arithmetic of a 13.1% vehicle margin whose two props are legislated to shrink. NIO is the one the numbers do not reach. Its vehicle margin reached 18.5%, the fattest of the three, deliveries grew 25.4% in the September quarter and the shares still fell 22% in a month, leaving them at 0.35x revenue, the cheapest of the group. Battery-swap stations and 35,000 employees are capital the market is declining to pay for.

The open question is Li Auto's fourth quarter, where management's profit guidance and consensus do not agree: analysts still model a full-year loss of RMB4.13bn. One of the two is about to be wrong in public.
