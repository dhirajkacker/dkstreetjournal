Powell Industries Has Priced and Contracted Four-Fifths of Next Year's Switchgear Revenue

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Powell Industries closed its fiscal year with the largest order book in the company's history, and its annual report explains why that book behaves unlike most backlogs: almost all of its revenue comes from long-term fixed-price contracts recognized as costs are incurred, so next year's margin was struck when each order was quoted.

Orders of $2.6bn for the year ran at twice shipments, and the portion scheduled to convert in fiscal 2027 covers 79% of consensus revenue for that year. The shares are at 20.1x those fiscal-2027 earnings against 36.2x on the same, uncut estimate six months ago.

The November markdown across data-centre electrical equipment sorted by revenue timing rather than by sector. nVent, which ships enclosures and cooling loops against roughly five months of book, took the deepest cut and is the only one of the three disclosing order deceleration; Hubbell barely moved, and its fastest growth now sits outside the regulated utility side.

Powell Industries, the Houston maker of custom medium-voltage switchgear and modular "E-House" substations, closed its fiscal year on 30 September with the biggest backlog it has ever reported. The annual report filed afterwards explained why that figure behaves differently from most order books: approximately 96% of fiscal-2026 revenue came from long-term fixed-price contracts recognized over time on a cost-to-cost basis, so the margin on nearly everything Powell ships next year was set when the order was quoted.

That disclosure arrived in the middle of a markdown that had been applied to the whole data-centre electrical shelf as though one premium were deflating out of all of it. The three names' own books sit at very different distances from a hyperscaler construction schedule, and the price damage sorted the same way: over the thirty days to 25 November nVent fell 22.9%, Powell 3.8% and Hubbell 1.3%. Powell's shares actually rose 8.6% between 13 and 25 November, spanning its 16 November results, while nVent fell 9.6% over the identical stretch.

What a fixed-price order locks in

Powell booked $710m of orders in the September quarter and $2.6bn for the fiscal year, up 124%, taking backlog to $2.7bn — two dollars of orders for every dollar shipped. The 10-K puts $1.3bn of that book in fiscal 2027, which is 79% of the $1.64bn of consensus revenue for the year. "So at the end of 4Q, of that $2.7 billion of backlog, we see $1.3 billion of it scheduled to convert in '27," chief financial officer Mike Metcalf told investors on 17 November. "The remainder would convert thereafter."

The worry about a fixed-price book is that costs quoted a year ago meet today's copper and craft-labour bills. Fiscal-2026 gross margin instead rose 190 basis points to 31.3%, and management said pricing has been held at or above inflationary benchmarks. The fourth quarter's 34.9% flatters the trend: 230 basis points of it came from favourable project closeouts, and management put the base margin on backlog conversion at roughly 30%.

Mix has moved with it. Electric utility plus commercial and other industrial work, the latter largely data centre, reached 50% of revenue against 41% a year earlier, with the data-centre book-and-ship line running at $75-80m a quarter against about $40m two to three years ago. Powell has seen design-driven reallocations but "no significant de-books yet", and says that market carries higher cancellation risk than its legacy refining and LNG work.

nVent is paid on shipment, and the market priced the shipments

nVent sells electrical enclosures, fastening hardware and thermal-management loops under brands including HOFFMAN, RAYCHEM and SCHROFF, and since 1 October has owned Maverick Power's switchgear plants. Its September quarter grew revenue 43.7% to $1.51bn with operating margin up 286 basis points, and guidance was raised twice. "Sales of $1.515 billion were up 44%," chief financial officer Gary Corona said on 30 October. "This was well ahead of our guidance. Driven by broad based growth led by very strong data center sales."

The cracks are narrower than the move. Gross margin fell 161 basis points, organic order growth slowed to low double digits against a 65% year-earlier comparison, and fourth-quarter organic growth is guided to 26-29% from 44%. Its $2.6bn backlog is worth roughly five months of shipments, about a third of next year's consensus revenue, so the order book reads the present. At 19.2x this year's consensus earnings against roughly 30x in May, the de-rating is nearly 40% on an estimate that has not been cut; a 23 November broker note kept a positive rating while cutting its target to $125 from $200.

Hubbell's annuity is not where its growth is

Hubbell, the Shelton components maker whose Utility Solutions arm sells into regulated transmission and distribution budgets, is the name most often described as insulated from hyperscaler timing. Its own guidance revision on 27 October cut Utility organic growth to 6-7% and raised Electrical Solutions to 17-18%. "Data center sales increased over 80% on an organic basis in the third quarter," chief executive Gerben Bakker said that day. The faster half of Hubbell is now the construction-linked half. At 20.8x this year's earnings against 23.1x six months ago, it gave back the least, on the slowest consensus growth of the three for next year, 11.9%.

What the order books cannot explain

Nothing disclosed by any of the three accounts for the November repricing. Powell's multiple fell from 36.2x to 20.1x on an uncut estimate while its orders doubled; Hubbell's barely moved while revenue growth accelerated from 11.1% in the March quarter to 24.6% in September. nVent is the only partial self-explanation, and only on order pacing, not on revenue or margin. The likelier reading is the one already on the record: the 3 November ballot results and the $198bn of US data-centre projects delayed, blocked or restructured by local opposition, with UBS putting roughly 30% of expected 2028 additions at risk.

That date matters more than the dollar figure. Contested capacity sits in 2028, beyond the year Powell has already contracted and far beyond the five months nVent has shipped against. The question the filings leave open is what a fixed-price book quoted into a scarcity market is worth if the megawatts behind the 2028 orders never get permitted, and no one will see that in a backlog number for another two years.
