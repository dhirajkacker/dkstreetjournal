Falling Insurance Rates Lifted Travelers' Operating Profit 53.1% and Cut Aon's Margin

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.6

One price cycle is producing two opposite income statements, and the market is paying more for the side whose revenue is shrinking. Commercial insurance rates have now fallen for eight consecutive quarters — global rates down 6% in the June quarter, property off 12%.

That reaches a broker directly, because its commission is a share of the premium, and reaches an underwriter only through the top line, where mild losses and investment income have more than covered it. Aon's revenue grew 2.2% in the June quarter and its operating income fell; Travelers' operating income rose on revenue that barely moved.

Organic growth at the brokers has not broken: Aon and Willis Towers Watson each grew 5%, Gallagher 6%. What broke is the price of it — 15.7x forward earnings for Aon against 11.0x for Travelers, whose 2026 consensus earnings are rising a third on a falling premium base.

Commercial insurance has been getting cheaper for eight straight quarters, and the two halves of the industry that live off the premium have gone in opposite directions. Travelers, which underwrites commercial, personal and specialty property-casualty policies, lifted June-quarter operating income 53.1% to $2.88bn on revenue growth of 0.3%. Aon, the Dublin-headquartered broker that arranges coverage of the kind Travelers writes, grew revenue 2.2% in the same quarter, watched operating income fall 4.2%, and saw its reported operating margin narrow to 19.4% from 20.7%.

The two are paid in different currencies. A broker collects a commission set as a share of the premium its client pays, so a softer rate cuts its revenue on identical exposure and identical work. An underwriter is paid the premium minus the losses, and losses have been mild while the invested float earns more. Marsh's Global Insurance Market Index logged its eighth consecutive quarterly decline in June, global commercial rates down 6% and property down 12%; broker-reported account data showed average commercial premiums falling in the first quarter for the first time in nearly nine years. Fitch expects further softening at the January 2027 renewals as reinsurance capital of about $785bn outruns demand. This is a forecast, not only a history.

The brokers are still growing; the market stopped paying for it

Organic growth has held. Aon and Willis Towers Watson each grew 5% organically in the June quarter and Arthur J. Gallagher 6%, with retention in the mid-90s and Aon putting the net effect of falling rates at zero to two points of growth. The exception is Brown & Brown, the Florida retail broker, where organic revenue excluding contingent commissions ran at -0.7% and management guided catastrophe property rates down a further 15% to 35% in the second half; its shares are down 29.2% over twelve months.

What the June quarter does confirm is the cost of buying growth. Gallagher's reported revenue rose 24.3% to $4.0bn on its purchase of AssuredPartners while operating income fell 5.8% and the reported operating margin dropped to 14.7% from 19.4%, with 15.5% more diluted shares outstanding than two years ago. Its sturdiest business is the one that is not a commission at all: Gallagher Bassett, paid per claim handled for self-insured employers, grew 12% organically on a 22.3% adjusted margin. Willis Towers Watson, whose Health, Wealth and Career book is billed per project and per participant, is the only one accelerating — revenue up 9.1% year on year after 8.5% — and consensus has its 2026 earnings up 16.8% to $19.82 a share. Its stock is down 6.5% over the year.

Aon's own complication is the balance sheet. It agreed on August 30 to buy USI Insurance Services from KKR for $17.0bn in cash, funded entirely with debt, raising $13.5bn of bonds in mid-September into as much as $65bn of orders. Buybacks are suspended, S&P moved the outlook to negative on pro-forma leverage of 4.3x to 4.5x against 2.7x at end-June, and the deal is dilutive to 2027 earnings. Aon paid 14.5x EBITDA after identified synergies against the 11.3x Gallagher paid for AssuredPartners. Chief executive J. Patrick Gallagher Jr., asked on the July 30 call whether private prices for middle-market brokers had adjusted, said: "It ain't happening. Multiples are coming down."

Where the money now sits

Aon trades at 15.7x forward earnings against 20.2x at last September's high, on 2026 consensus of $18.79 a share that has not moved — compression in the multiple rather than in the estimates. Willis sits at 15.6x forward and throws off more cash against its market value than the others, a 5.9% free-cash-flow yield; Gallagher is the group's most expensive on enterprise value and thinnest on cash, at 15.7x trailing EBITDA and a 3.7% yield.

The underwriters sit a full tier below. Travelers is priced at 11.0x forward earnings with consensus 2026 earnings up 33.9% to $34.08 a share on revenue falling 1.7%, and a 14.1% trailing free-cash-flow yield. Chubb, the Zurich-headquartered global insurer that distributes mainly through brokers, grew revenue 6.3% while operating income slipped 2.4%, and trades at 12.3x. Over twelve months Travelers rose 34.8%, Chubb 24.2% and Arch Capital 9.9%; the brokers fell between 6.5% and 29.2%, against a 15.0% gain in the S&P 500.

The carriers have earned their move, but they earned it on the cycle's timing: reserve comfort and investment income arriving faster than the premium base erodes. That gap closes with a bad catastrophe year or another twelve months of rate declines. The brokers' de-rating is only partly earned — Gallagher's margin collapse and Aon's leverage are real charges against the equity, while Willis's accelerating, fee-weighted book has nothing in its accounts to explain the fall. Aon's 50-day average slipped below its 200-day on September 16, its first such reading since June, the day the Federal Reserve raised rates for the first time since 2023 — which happens to reverse the headwind on the brokers' fiduciary income, $58m at Aon in the June quarter and down 12% year on year.

The January renewals set the premium base both sides are paid from for 2027. One side needs it to stop falling; the other has spent two years proving it does not.

Appian Will Retry Its Vacated $2.036bn Claim Against Pegasystems on January 11

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.6

A courtroom holds an asset worth roughly three-quarters of Appian's equity value, and it finally has a date on the calendar. Appian's latest quarterly filing discloses that the Fairfax County Circuit Court has set the retrial of its vacated trade-secret award against Pegasystems to begin in January 2027 — superseding the position reported here a week ago, that no date had been set. Pegasystems carries no accrual for the matter.

Until the verdict, the operating business stands alone, and it is split down the middle. June-quarter adjusted EBITDA of $16.2m beat the $5–8m the company guided, while revenue growth eased to 19.1% and gross margin fell to 71.2%. Cloud net expansion of 115% is the one demand meter that held.

The profit inflection is earned. The re-rating — from 3.07x gross profit in early May to 4.77x — is not visible in the demand numbers.

Appian's Form 10-Q for the June quarter discloses something its earlier filings did not: on May 7, 2026 the Fairfax County Circuit Court set a date for the retrial of the trade-secret claim it won, and then lost, against Pegasystems. The trial begins January 11, 2027. The position reported here on September 13 — that no trial date had been set — is superseded.

The stake is unusual. Appian, a McLean, Virginia company that sells a low-code process-automation platform to banks, insurers, drugmakers and government agencies, won a $2.036bn jury verdict against Pegasystems, a rival in enterprise workflow software, and watched the Supreme Court of Virginia vacate it on January 8 over a jury instruction that shifted the burden of proof on damages. That award is about 73% of Appian's $2.77bn market value. Pegasystems' own quarterly filing records no loss accrual for the matter. Nothing is collected or paid before a verdict, so the operating business has to be judged without it.

What an Appian dollar actually is

It is not paid for "low-code" as a category. It is a cloud subscription billed per user and per application across three tiers, negotiated contract by contract, with designer licenses priced far above ordinary business users. Chief financial officer Serge Tanjga told William Blair's growth conference that four verticals — financial services, insurance, life sciences and the public sector — supply 80% of annual recurring revenue, and that more than 70% of the business comes from customers spending over $1m a year: one bank replaced six fraud systems with a single workflow; a branch of the US military runs ammunition supply-chain work on it at roughly a million transactions a month.

Artificial intelligence reaches that revenue as a price increase rather than a product line. Putting AI into production requires the Advanced tier, which carries a 25–35% license premium; Tanjga said 70–80% of customers use AI in some form and 40% already pay for it. Appian's disclosed retention meter, cloud net annualized recurring revenue expansion, was 115% at June 30, flat sequentially and up from 113% a year earlier.

The profit line and the demand line disagree

June-quarter adjusted EBITDA was $16.2m against $5–8m guided and $8.1m a year earlier, and the GAAP operating loss halved to $5.44m. That follows a first full-year GAAP operating profit in 2025, of $0.6m, after a $60.9m loss in 2024. It is not headcount surgery: chief executive Matt Calkins told investors on the August 6 call that pulling 2027 hiring forward "might be an indication of strong pipeline that's unusually strong and also our confidence of being able to win in the current environment."

The demand meters run the other way. Revenue growth slowed to 19.1% in the June quarter, from 21.5% in March. Gross margin has compressed to 71.2% from 75.8% four quarters ago, with subscription gross margin down to 84% from 86%. Unsatisfied performance obligations of $625.3m grew 13%, slower than revenue. And the raised full-year guide — 16–17% total revenue, 20–21% cloud — sits below the 23% cloud growth just posted.

One popular explanation for the margin story is wrong. The professional-services book has not been starved: services revenue grew 20%, marginally faster than the total, holding 22.4% of revenue against 22.3% a year earlier, and its gross margin improved to 31% from 29%. The compression came from the software line.

What the shares have already paid for

Appian has held an uptrend since mid-August, its 50-day average above its 200-day, after a 79% gain over three months that roughly doubled the next-best move among comparable subscription-software names. Two-thirds of it arrived before results, in a window when Freshworks, nCino and Procore all turned up too — the likelier reading is a sector-wide re-rating of beaten-down software, with the August print adding a company-specific 14% session. Price-to-gross-profit went from 3.07x in early May to 4.77x trailing, against consensus revenue for the year rising about 3% to $849.7m. Forward, at 4.47x gross profit, Appian sits at the median of that group, below construction-software maker Procore at 6.46x and above accounting-automation vendor BlackLine at 2.85x, while carrying roughly double the group's median forward growth. Stretched against May; against last September, the shares are up 14.8% on trailing revenue up 15.1%, and the multiple has not moved at all.

So the profit inflection is earned and the margin story is real; the acceleration the multiple implies appears in no demand meter except retention, and management's own guidance embeds further slowing. Against Pegasystems — whose annual contract value growth halved to 7% — Appian is taking share. Against ServiceNow and Microsoft's bundled Power Platform, there is no win-rate evidence either way.

Which leaves the one item no operating metric prices. A retrial can return a number larger than the company, or nothing at all. Fairfax County, January 11.

PayPal's Branded Checkout Grew 2% While Braintree Grew 13%; Margin Dollars Rose 1%

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.6

Payment volume is the most quoted number in digital payments and the least informative. PayPal moved 9% more money in the June quarter on a currency-neutral basis and kept 1% more gross profit from it, because the growth came from unbranded Braintree processing — a near-commodity gateway at a take rate analysts put near 1.85% — rather than from the checkout button it owns.

A second engine sits outside volume entirely: interest on customer balances, guided down to about $1,003m in 2026 from $1,225m in 2025, which the Federal Reserve's September hike now starts to reverse.

The verdict splits the two acceptance businesses in this corner of financial services. PayPal's de-rating is mostly earned; Global Payments, paid a spread per merchant per transaction, grew adjusted earnings 12% and has been re-rated upward. The same twelve months marked down the button and marked up the rail.

PayPal moved $486.4bn of payment volume in the June quarter and kept almost none of the increase. Total volume grew 9% on a currency-neutral basis; transaction margin dollars — the spread PayPal actually retains after paying the card networks and banks — rose 1%, to $3.9bn.

That gap is the whole business explained in two lines. A payments company is not paid for volume; it is paid a take rate, and PayPal's differs by a large factor depending on where the transaction enters. Branded checkout — the yellow button on a merchant's page, where PayPal owns the consumer relationship — carries a high spread and grew 2% currency-neutral. Unbranded processing through Braintree, where PayPal is a gateway routing other companies' card transactions, grew 13%, at a take rate analysts put near 1.85%. Every point of mix shift toward the cheap channel dilutes what the volume headline implies.

The meter that volume hides

Gross profit tells the same story on a longer clock: growth of 5.9%, 2.5%, 2.5% and 1.5% across the last four quarters, with gross margin down to 44.9% from 46.4%. Active accounts were flat at 439m, though transactions per account excluding the gateway business grew 7% for a second straight quarter. Operating income rose 25.6% on cost cuts while GAAP net income fell 12.5% — the swing sits below the operating line.

What sits there is float. PayPal earns interest on customer balances and on its buy-now-pay-later receivables, a second engine no volume statistic captures, and it is forecast to fall to about $1,003m in 2026 from $1,225m in 2025 on a roughly 75 basis-point lower effective rate. Strip it out and margin dollars grew 3%. The direction is changing: the Federal Reserve raised its target to 3.75%-4.00% on September 16, the first increase since 2023.

The competitive reading is harsher. Apple Pay, Shopify, Affirm, Klarna, Cash App and Zelle have all taken checkout territory, and PayPal is spending $400m this year to defend it. "PayPal has had a lot of trouble evolving from being just a way to pay on your desktop computer," Sanjay Sakhrani of Keefe, Bruyette & Woods told the Associated Press. "I'm encouraged by the progress we made this quarter," Enrique Lores, chief executive since March, said on the July 28 call. "We moved with urgency to sharpen our transformation plan."

The shares are down 23.5% over twelve months in three discrete events, not a grind: a 20.3% fall on February 3 when a fourth-quarter miss coincided with the board's ouster of Alex Chriss, a 17.2% jump on July 15 when Stripe and Advent International bid $60.50 a share, and a 13.1% drop on August 28 when that consortium walked. Trailing earnings multiple: 9.83x, against roughly 13.7x a year ago, on four-quarter GAAP earnings per share that rose to $5.29 from $4.99. Forward is 9.73x — the market underwrites no growth — and the trailing free-cash-flow yield is 14.7%.

The other way to earn a payments dollar

Global Payments is the mirror. It is a merchant acquirer, paid a spread per merchant per transaction on physical terminals and embedded software, and in January it swapped its issuer-processing arm to FIS for Worldpay. Second-quarter adjusted net revenue grew 4% normalised with adjusted earnings per share of $3.46, up 12%, and operating margin of 42.0%. Enterprise and Platforms each grew 7%, small-business 4%. Full-year revenue growth guidance was cut to 4-5% on Middle East travel exposure while the $13.60-$13.80 earnings range held. Leverage sits just under 3.5x; chief executive Cameron Bready said the company feels "very good about getting back to that 3x leverage point" by end-2027, with $1.2bn returned this year. Trailing earnings are unusable — a $1.8bn first-quarter loss on non-deductible goodwill tax from the disposal — so the anchor is 6.23x forward against $13.65 consensus, below Fiserv's roughly 7.5x, and 0.955x book. The shares are roughly flat over twelve months and 27% above their June low.

What the market actually repriced

So the card rail was not marked down wholesale. The spread-per-merchant model was re-rated upward on delivered synergies and falling leverage; the consumer-checkout franchise was marked down a quarter while its earnings rose. PayPal's de-rating is largely earned — a 2% branded meter and flat accounts are what a nine-times multiple describes — and the unexplained residue is narrow: cost discipline and buybacks are producing earnings the market will not capitalize, on the view that mix and float erosion continue.

Both of those assumptions face a date. PayPal reports on October 27, Global Payments on November 3. And the board that called $60.50 inadequate now has shares 15% below it, with the bidder gone and the burden of proof entirely on the button.
