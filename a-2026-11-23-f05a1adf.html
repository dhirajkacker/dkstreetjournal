Celestica's $40bn 2027 Guide Rides on Google TPU Racks; Flex and Jabil Spread Their Risk

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Three contract manufacturers that build artificial-intelligence servers all reported accelerating business this autumn, and all three have been marked down hard. Only one of them has a single buyer behind a quarter of next year's plan.

Celestica guided 2027 to $40bn of revenue, up 90%, after growing September-quarter sales 82.5%. Its third-quarter filing discloses three customers at 35%, 17% and 14% of revenue, and KGI Securities puts Google chip work at 25-30% of 2027 revenue. On 20 November an analyst report of a roughly 20% cut to Google's 2027 Broadcom-designed chip orders took the shares down 16%.

The reported numbers do not explain the de-rating at any of the three. The business case genuinely weakens only at Celestica, where gross margin fell to 10.99% from 13.03% and 2027 is a declared net use of cash. Flex's Cloud and Power margin rose to 10.7% and Jabil guides to about $1.6bn of adjusted free cash flow.

Celestica told investors in late October that 2027 revenue would reach $40bn. Three weeks later the market began asking who would place the orders.

On 20 November, Ming-Chi Kuo of TF International Securities said Google had cut 2027 shipments of the Broadcom-designed TPU 8i chip series by roughly a million units, about 20% of planned volume, while raising orders for MediaTek's competing 8t series. Neither Google nor Broadcom confirmed the figures. Celestica, the Toronto company that integrates those chips into finished server racks, fell 16.0% that session, the steepest drop on a contract-manufacturing shelf where Sanmina fell 9.7%, Benchmark Electronics 9.4% and Plexus 9.0%.

The October quarter had been read as settling the argument about this group: Celestica grew revenue 82.5% to $5.83bn, the third straight quarter of acceleration, and issued its first 2027 outlook at $40bn of revenue and $21.50 of adjusted earnings per share. What the reports of mid-November put in question is not the quarter but the order book, and on that measure the three largest Western builders of artificial-intelligence hardware are not one story.

The buyer behind a quarter of next year

Celestica's third-quarter filing discloses three customers at 35%, 17% and 14% of revenue, 66% combined, all inside the Connectivity and Cloud Solutions segment. KGI Securities estimates Google chip work at 25-30% of Celestica's 2027 revenue, with Celestica the primary system integrator for the rack-level assemblies. Earlier in November the shares also fell on a DigiTimes report that Google was expanding that assembly work to Inventec and other Taiwanese manufacturers. RBC Capital called the reaction an overreaction, noting the report describes Google adding a supplier rather than dropping one, and Kuo tied the chip re-planning to supply tightness and data-center power limits rather than weaker demand.

"Our $40 billion guide took into consideration the risks around the ramps, the component availability, and the operational execution," chief financial officer Todd Ankenmann told investors on the 27 October call. "At 90% year-to-year growth, we feel high confidence that that number is supported."

The company's own lines are less reassuring than its growth rate. Gross margin fell to 10.99% from 13.03% a year earlier, and reported operating income rose 39.4% against revenue up 82.5%. Hardware Platform Solutions, the design-led business carrying Celestica's own intellectual property, grew 70% to $2.3bn, slower than the 102% at the segment containing it, leaving own-design work near half of that segment. Free-cash-flow guidance for 2026 was cut to $460m from $600m, and 2027 was described as a net use of cash on $1.5-2.0bn of capital spending, with positive free cash flow expected in 2028. Celestica trades at 18.7x trailing earnings and roughly 9.4x its own guided 2027 figure, against 46x trailing in May. Comparisons across programs are rough: the OpenAI Jalapeño rack work carries consigned economics, so its major material costs never enter revenue.

Flex is paid more for each AI dollar

Flex, which builds cloud hardware and the switchgear and power distribution feeding it, grew September-quarter revenue 24.8% and gross profit 32.2%. Its Cloud and Power Infrastructure segment grew 54% to $2.5bn at a 10.7% operating margin, up 240 basis points, with the power business near 16%. Full-year guidance rose by about $950m to $34.9-35.9bn. About 10 percentage points of the segment's guided growth comes from the EP2 and EPC Power acquisitions, by the chief financial officer's own accounting, so roughly a third of it is bought.

"You cannot walk away from the fact that hyperscalers are spending the most amount of money," chief executive Revathi Advaithi said on 28 October. "If you want to be able to grow your market share, you have to grow with these hyperscalers." Flex is funding a $1.5-1.6bn capital program at about 40% free-cash-flow conversion, and at an Innovation Day in Austin on 10 November it set out calendar-2027 revenue of $20-21bn for Axiom Solutions International, the cloud and power unit it plans to list separately by 31 March 2027, calling the figures direction rather than guidance. The shares are at 13.2x forward earnings against 23.9x trailing.

Jabil's leverage, not its pricing

Jabil, which assembles cloud infrastructure alongside healthcare and automotive work, guided fiscal 2027 to $44.5bn of revenue, up 24%, with AI-related revenue of about $22.1bn, up 54%, and roughly $1.6bn of adjusted free cash flow. Five customers are each expected to clear $1bn of AI revenue. Its August-quarter gross margin was flat at 9.43% while operating margin widened to 5.67% from 4.08%: the improvement came from scale rather than price. At 12.8x forward earnings and a 5.91% trailing free-cash-flow yield, Jabil is the cheapest of the three on cash.

None of the reported numbers accounts for falls of 19% to 33% over thirty days, or for the 20 November session itself, when the S&P 500 dropped 3.70% and Nvidia about 14% amid reports of a possible accounting probe. What distinguishes the three is who absorbs a change of plan. Flex and Jabil each sell into several buyers and convert cash while they build; their downtrends were already in place a month before the Google reports. Celestica's turn down is new, and it is the name whose marginal dollar earns less than its average, whose 2027 is a declared cash outflow, and whose plan leans on one buyer's chip roadmap. The Google reports are unconfirmed, and if they are wrong the de-rating is a repricing of 2027 AI capital spending across the board. If they are right, one company's guide carries almost all the damage.

Celestica sold 11.1m shares at $310 in August to fund working capital and plant for exactly this ramp. The money is committed; the orders it was raised against are now the subject of someone else's supply-chain note.

Originating hypothesis

category emerging bear ems order book · subject: CLS, FLEX, JBL

The electronics-manufacturing-services shelf — the companies that physically build AI servers, racks and networking gear — has rolled into an emerging bear at gradual intensity (category avg -15.9% over 30 days against a still-positive +8.1% over twelve months), with Celestica carrying the move from mildly bullish to strongly bearish in 30 days and strongly bullish to strongly bearish over the year while Flex and Jabil slide more slowly, and the question is whether this de-rating reflects a real change in the AI hardware order book and in who owns the design (Celestica's own-IP Hardware Platform Solutions and 800G switching versus build-to-print assembly, hyperscalers shifting volume to Asian ODMs, pricing concessions on next-generation racks) or whether bookings, backlog and segment margins are still accelerating and only the multiple has contracted.
