AI Buyers Took the Packaging Lines Microchip Rents, and Its Best Orders Go Unshipped

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The analog shelf was sold through the autumn as a spent restocking trade in cars and factory equipment. The channel data says restocking never happened: Microchip's distributors held 25 days of inventory at the end of September, down from 26 in March, and Analog Devices' channel sits below its own six-to-seven-week target. What changed is supply. Foundries and assembly-and-test houses are steering mature-node and packaging capacity toward data-center customers, and Microchip's lead times have stretched from 4–8 weeks to 6–32.

Automotive is the slowest line at all three companies; industrial and data center carry the growth. Microchip grew revenue 42% year on year in September and guided December down about 1% sequentially because of orders it cannot ship. Its price-to-gross-profit has gone from 20.1x in May to 9.4x; Analog Devices, whose shares agree with its numbers, still trades at 19.9x.

The constraint on Microchip Technology's December quarter is not an order book that stopped growing. It is assembly and test capacity that data-center chipmakers rented out from underneath it.

That matters because the whole analog shelf has been traded since May as an automotive and industrial restocking cycle that had run its course, with the rollover in share prices read as evidence that distributors had finished refilling and orders would follow them down. The disclosures from Microchip, NXP Semiconductors and Analog Devices say restocking never arrived in the first place, and that the binding problem now is on the supply side of mature-node silicon, where little capacity has been added for years.

Lead times quadrupled at the top end

Microchip, the Chandler, Arizona maker of microcontrollers, analog and timing parts for industrial, automotive, computing and aerospace customers, reported 25 days of inventory in its distribution channel at 30 September, down from 26 days in March. Over the same stretch its lead times went from the 4–8 weeks that had prevailed to 6–32 weeks. Its fiscal 2027 risk disclosure states plainly that certain suppliers have notified plans to discontinue legacy manufacturing processes as industry capacity is increasingly directed toward data centers, with no assurance that necessary foundry capacity will be available on acceptable terms.

Chief executive Steve Sanghi told investors on 5 November that the company has "substantial unsupported backlog on high-margin products that our supply chain cannot fulfill", and that capacity at major outsourced assembly and test suppliers had been displaced by AI companies. Customers are not hoarding against it. "We can hardly get them to place orders with a new lead time," Sanghi said on the same call.

NXP, the Eindhoven supplier of microcontrollers, connectivity and processors to carmakers and industrial customers, ended its September quarter with distribution channel inventory at 11 weeks, its stated long-term target, while 80% of its portfolio now carries lead times beyond 16 weeks against 25% a year earlier. Its channel sell-in grew slower than its direct shipments: distributor revenue rose 8.3% sequentially to $2,244m against 8.9% for direct customers. Chief financial officer William Betts said on 27 October that "the book to bill remains well above 1 across all end markets and regions," and chief executive Rafael Sotomayor denied seeing broad-based restocking. Analog Devices' channel weeks ran below its 6-to-7-week target in the August quarter, with book-to-bill above 1 but "not in that sort of unusually high space".

Cars are the slow line, not the engine

The restocking framing also had the wrong end market. Analog Devices' industrial revenue grew 53% year on year to $1.97bn in the August quarter and communications 84%, while automotive grew 16%. NXP's Industrial and Internet of Things line rose 39% and communications infrastructure 54%, against 13% for automotive in its first $2bn automotive quarter. Microchip's industrial sales rose 50.3% and data center 55.7%; automotive, at 19.3%, was its slowest. Vehicle output is not the culprit: S&P Global Mobility's October forecast raised 2027 production for Europe by 68,000 units and Greater China by 258,000.

One order book, three prices

All three businesses are accelerating. Microchip has lifted year-on-year revenue growth for four straight quarters to 42.2%, gross margin by 10.0 points to 65.9% and operating margin from 12.8% to 28.6%. Its December guide of -3% to +1% sequentially, with gross margin stepping to 64–65%, is the cost of parts it cannot get and of $53m of one-time items that will not repeat; management said the March-quarter backlog is better than December's was at the same point.

The shares disagree. Over six months Microchip is down 37.3%, NXP 22.7% and Analog Devices 8.1%, and the week to 20 November took roughly 17% out of the PHLX Semiconductor Index before chip stocks rebounded on 23 November after Nvidia reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive.

Microchip's price-to-gross-profit has fallen from 20.1x in May to 9.4x now while gross profit grew 67.4%, and its 45.4x trailing earnings become 16.8x forward. NXP is cheaper still at 8.2x trailing gross profit, with a 5.7% free-cash-flow yield and a $6.3bn buyback authorization worth about a tenth of its market value; Microchip, at 2.36x net debt to EBITDA, cannot buy its own de-rated stock. Analog Devices is the one whose price tracks its results, and it has quietly got cheaper doing so, at 19.9x trailing gross profit against 25.7x in May.

So the split inside this shelf is about what investors will pay, not what the businesses are earning. What would break the thesis is visible and countable: distributor weeks climbing above target, or book-to-bill slipping under 1. What would confirm it is duller, and is already partly disclosed, which is backlog shifting from December into March as capacity frees up.

Microchip is absorbing the foundry and assembly price increases that start in January rather than passing them on. "We don't want to push the luck," Sanghi said. A company that is holding prices down while turning away high-margin orders is not describing a demand problem.
