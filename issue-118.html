Okta Bundled AI-Agent Sign-On Into Seats It Already Sold; SailPoint Bills Per Identity

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Both companies sell identity software into the same agentic-AI story, and only one has a meter that moves when an agent appears. Okta folded agent single sign-on into the edition enterprises already license, built a consumption cap it has not switched on, and discloses no agent count. SailPoint counts every agent as an identity under management, and its disclosed artificial-intelligence revenue has passed $70m, more than 30% of net new annual recurring revenue on a base of $1.231bn growing 25%.

The income statements run the other way. Okta's revenue growth has slowed for four straight quarters, to 10.6%, while operating margin went from 5.6% to 13.3%; SailPoint still posts a GAAP operating margin of -19.1% as term licenses migrate to ratable subscriptions. Okta's shares have more than doubled in a year on profits and a richer multiple, not demand.

Okta's chief financial officer was asked in August what the company collects when an artificial-intelligence agent authenticates through its identity service, and he said the revenue was not yet worth counting. At SailPoint, the Austin identity-governance vendor that bills by the identity it manages, human or machine, that same agent lands inside a disclosed artificial-intelligence revenue line already past $70m.

The agentic-identity story has been read across identity software as one trade, on the premise that machine identities now outnumber human ones by somewhere between 82 and 109 to one, depending on whose annual landscape survey you take. If that ratio is the demand driver, the billing unit is the whole question.

Okta sells human workforce seats. SailPoint sells identities under management. Only one of those meters turns when an agent is provisioned.

The agent that logs in free

"Still immaterial. Still very small. We're very early innings… for FY '27, we don't think it's going to be material," Brett Tighe said of agent-identity revenue on Okta's earnings call of 26 August 2026. Agent sign-on was folded into the standard edition at the company's Oktane conference in September as an uplift on seats already sold, and the consumption cap built into the product has not been switched on. There is no agent count and no attach rate. The capital is going out all the same: Okta bought Permiso Security for a reported $200m, adding agent-behavior visibility and a sandbox for agent skills.

That pricing choice looks less odd beside what the largest distributor of corporate directories is doing. Microsoft's Entra Agent ID is generally available to all Entra customers, so the agent control plane ships inside licenses enterprises already own. Where Microsoft does charge, it charges per human: Agent 365 at $15 per user per month, one license covering every agent that person owns or manages. Okta's own competitive assessment, updated in July, names Entra ID the single biggest threat to its franchise.

SailPoint's meter counts the agent; its revenue line hides it

SailPoint's annual recurring revenue reached $1.231bn in the July quarter, up 25%, with software-as-a-service ARR of $847m up 36%, net revenue retention of 113% and backlog of $1.9bn, roughly 1.5 times ARR. The artificial-intelligence ARR above $70m is more than 30% of net new ARR, against a $100m target for this year. "Securing and governing all identities, including agentic identities is the exact problem our company was built to solve," chief executive Mark McClain said on the 9 September 2026 call, where he also dated the forcing function: European artificial-intelligence rules on human oversight and audit logging enforceable as early as 2027.

The income statement disagrees with the order book. Reported revenue grew 16.8%, down from 22.7% two quarters earlier, because term licenses recognized up front are migrating to ratable cloud subscriptions, and the GAAP operating margin is still -19.1%. The company has guided third-quarter ARR to $1,288-1,292m, 24% growth, a point slower than the quarter just reported. It publishes no split between machine and employee identities, so the agent share of that ARR rests on management's characterization.

What Okta's double bought

Okta's growth has decelerated four consecutive quarters, to 10.6% on revenue of $805m, and consensus carries 10.0% for the year to January 2028. Profit is what changed: gross margin 79.6% against 76.9%, operating income up 161%. The contracted revenue due within a year reached $2.585bn, up 14%, with management guiding that growth down to 11-12%. Net retention was flat at 107%. Governance, privileged access and threat protection were 30% of second-quarter bookings at roughly 40% average contract-value uplift per deal, the one disclosed line outgrowing revenue, and none of it priced per agent.

So the twelve-month doubling in the shares is margin and rating, not units.

Price-to-gross-profit is the comparison that survives a thirteen-point gross margin gap between the two, 79.6% against 66.5%. Okta stands at 13.33x trailing against 5.97x on 3 May 2026; forward earnings are 48.7x, against roughly 36x in mid-July when the stock was $139.53. SailPoint costs more per dollar of gross profit, at 15.77x trailing, and less per dollar of contracted growth: 25% ARR expansion against Okta's 14% on its forward book. The discount it carries is a supply question, with Thoma Bravo still holding roughly 84-88% of the shares.

The market has begun sorting the two. Okta's advance stalled on 11 November and the stock sits 17.5% below its 9 October high of $232.13, while SailPoint rose 6.3% over the month to 20 November as ServiceNow fell 44.2% and Datadog 38.9%. The sell-off around them has an explicit mechanism: on 17 November Salesforce fell about 9% on fears that agents reduce the number of per-seat licenses enterprises need. A per-seat identity vendor is not obviously exempt from that logic, and Palo Alto Networks, which closed its $21.1bn purchase of CyberArk in February, now sells human, machine and agentic identity as one line.

Okta's own chief executive put a clock on it. Todd McKinnon described a prospective customer that began an evaluation with 50 agents: "And then we came back a few weeks and there was 1,500 Claude agents in the environment." At Oktane he said agent-identity market share "is going to be set probably in three years." Giving the product away inside a seat is one way to be in the room when it is.

Three Ways to Get Paid for AI Rack Power, and Only Vicor's Royalties Face a Court Test

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A federal appeals panel is deciding whether boilerplate in Foxconn purchase orders already licensed the patent behind Vicor's fastest-growing revenue line, and the answer sets the size of a stream the company does not have to manufacture.

Three suppliers of rack power to artificial-intelligence data centers were marked down together in November after all three reported accelerating revenue. They earn money in different ways. Vicor booked $66.6m of royalties inside $197.1m of September-quarter revenue, and its own fourth-quarter guidance implies total revenue falls to roughly $186m. Advanced Energy's semiconductor-equipment half grew 64% year on year to a record, with December-quarter gross margin guided to 42–42.5%. Littelfuse's organic growth accelerated from 9% to 22% across 2026.

Only Vicor has a disclosure-level reason for its de-rating: the court, and a royalty-driven sequential decline. At 30.2x forward earnings it is still roughly double the other two.

A federal appeals panel spent 3 November on the fine print of purchase orders for power converters, and what it decides sets how much revenue Vicor can keep billing without shipping anything. The question in Vicor v. ITC is whether clauses buried in purchase orders from Foxconn affiliates already granted them a licence to the patent that underpins the company's royalty income. The International Trade Commission found a licence had been formed, on the strength of that boilerplate plus one Vicor salesperson's email about shipping dates. Judge Dyk called the clause ridiculously broad at argument and floated a narrower reading limiting it to the items actually sold, an outcome that would shrink the royalty base rather than erase it.

That matters because the power-conversion layer was sold as one trade this autumn and is three businesses. Vicor, which makes brick-format DC-DC converters and licenses its vertical-power-delivery topology; Advanced Energy Industries, which sells plasma power supplies to chipmakers and power shelves to hyperscalers; and Littelfuse, whose Electronics segment ships fuses and circuit protection into data-center racks, all reported accelerating revenue and record orders in the month before the markdown. How each gets paid decides where the markdown has a reason attached.

A royalty line with a court attached

Vicor booked $66.6m of royalty revenue in the September quarter, up 119% sequentially, inside total revenue of $197.1m. That is a third of the top line arriving without a shipment. The hardware behind it is growing too: implied product revenue of about $130.5m was up roughly 16% sequentially, and Advanced Products reached $139m. "So as of yesterday, the backlog is actually closer to $400 million," chief executive Patrizio Vinciarelli told investors on 20 October, updating a $46m quarter-end figure.

The fourth-quarter guide is the awkward part. Product revenue was guided up 10%-plus sequentially against a royalty floor of $43m assuming no new licences, which works out to roughly $186m in total, a sequential decline of 5–6% caused by the licence line rather than by demand. The enforcement machine that produces those licences is active: the Commission instituted Investigation 337-TA-1526 on 9 October, naming 20 respondents including Delta, Infineon, Monolithic Power Systems, Flex and Foxconn, and an earlier case ended in February 2025 with a limited exclusion order barring unlicensed imports of infringing modules. Monolithic Power, which took the Nvidia H100 socket from Vicor, is a respondent in the new case and reported no demand deterioration of its own.

Advanced Energy's cyclical half turned out to be the fast half

The fear about Advanced Energy was that semiconductor equipment, on its own capex clock, would swamp the data-center ramp. It did the opposite: semiconductor revenue grew 64% year on year to a record $322m, outrunning data center's record $256m, inside total revenue of $696m. December-quarter gross margin is guided to 42–42.5%, up from 42.1% non-GAAP in the third quarter. "Our Q4 guidance puts us on a run rate to exceed our 2030 target model, 3 years ahead of our original plan," chief financial officer Paul Oldham said on 2 November.

The company repurchased about $30m of stock in October at roughly $241 a share, some 27% above the 20 November close. Brokers cut targets anyway while citing the strong semiconductor and data-center results, with the shares at a 52-week low. At 15.3x forward earnings against 25.6x trailing, the market is paying for the step-up already in guidance and nothing beyond it.

Littelfuse grows without ever naming the number

Littelfuse is the mildest faller and the hardest to size. Organic growth accelerated from 9% in the first quarter to 14% and then 22%, with the Electronics segment up 27% organically and its adjusted EBITDA margin at 33.1%. "Organic growth contributed 22%. The Basler acquisition contributed approximately 6%. And foreign exchange was neutral," chief financial officer Abhishek Khandelwal said on 28 October, which settles the question of whether the growth was bought. Data-center design wins ran at twice the prior-year level, more of them in higher-voltage systems.

What the company has never disclosed is dollar content per server. Management says the vast majority of data-center revenue still sits in low-voltage systems and that growth comes either way on the voltage outcome, which is a claim about direction and not about magnitude. The trailing earnings multiple is unusable after a 2025 net loss of $71.7m; at 19.4x forward earnings and a 5.8% trailing free-cash-flow yield, it is the only one of the three generating meaningful cash while it grows.

One session did most of it

The falls were wildly unequal: over the 30 days to 20 November, Vicor dropped 39.6%, Advanced Energy 22.4% and Littelfuse 7.8%. The single session of 20 November took Vicor down 11.6%, Advanced Energy 10.4% and Littelfuse 6.8%, the day Nvidia fell 14% and the Nasdaq Composite 5.28%, with no company news at any of the three.

So the reported numbers account for almost none of the move, and the split in what is left has a cause. Vicor's compression, from roughly 68x forward earnings in May 2026 to 30.2x, corrects a price that assumed near-perfect execution, and it is the only one of the three whose next quarter declines and whose largest growth line sits in front of a court. Advanced Energy and Littelfuse carry no equivalent disclosure: both guided margins up, and at 7.5x and 7.7x forward gross profit they are priced at roughly half Vicor's 14.8x. The macro reason for a de-rating is a financing question, not a cancelled order; S&P Global Ratings expects all six largest hyperscalers to post negative free operating cash flow in 2026 and 2027 as cash goes out ahead of revenue.

The architecture everyone is being priced against has not shipped yet. Nvidia's 800-volt direct-current racks enter production with the Kyber platform starting in 2027, and none of these three appears on its published silicon or power-system partner rosters. Vicor begins deploying equipment in its second fab in the second quarter of 2027, funded from $513m of cash. The judges will have answered the licence question first.

Originating hypothesis

category emerging bear power delivery revenue model · subject: VICR, AEIS, LFUS

The power-conversion layer — the components that step hyperscaler rack voltage down to the chip — is the one tech-hardware shelf on this universe snapshot rolling over at gradual rather than violent intensity (Power Conversion & Control averages -14.4% over 30 days against +28.6% over twelve months, a markdown a fraction of the -31% to -35% taken by memory and enterprise servers on the same snapshot), and the question is mechanical rather than directional: establish how each of these businesses actually gets paid as racks move from 48-volt to 800-volt DC architecture, since Vicor increasingly earns licence and royalty income on a patented power-delivery architecture it does not have to manufacture, Advanced Energy books data-center power shelves alongside a semiconductor-equipment business running on a different capex cycle, and Littelfuse sells low-ticket circuit protection whose content per rack rises whichever architecture wins — so one de-rating is being applied to a royalty stream, a capex-cycle business and a content-per-unit business at once.

Fair Isaac Locked Its Credit Score at $10 for 2027 Across Most of the Reseller Channel

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Fair Isaac withheld fiscal 2027 guidance on 4 November, and the withholding was read as the credit-score franchise cracking under a regulator-blessed rival. The November mortgage data says something narrower: lenders pull VantageScore 4.0 widely and price loans with it rarely. In the first ten days of November, 173 agency sellers delivered loans with the rival score pulled; 37 used it to set credit pricing.

What Fair Isaac has done is fix its own ceiling. The wholesale score stays at $10 per bureau per borrower for 2027, now distributed through the reseller Xactus, after an administered price step produced nearly all of fiscal 2026's 27.3% revenue growth. Consensus has fiscal 2027 revenue growing 2.5%, and the shares sit at 19.0x trailing earnings against roughly 65x a year ago.

Fair Isaac said on 20 November that the mortgage-data reseller Xactus, together with its XedaLink and SharperLending units, will make the FICO Preferred Program available to all its mortgage clients from 1 January 2027. The program bundles the classic FICO Score with FICO Score 10T at a wholesale price of $10 per bureau per borrower, explicitly unchanged for next year. A separate direct-license program lets lenders cut per-score fees by up to half.

That is the company putting a ceiling on the one line that carried it. Fair Isaac, which sells credit scores embedded in lender transaction flows and decisioning software to banks, declined to give fiscal 2027 guidance on 4 November, citing unfinished mortgage pricing agreements, elevated rates and weak loan volumes. The withholding was taken as evidence that a rival score authorized for conforming loans is prising lenders away. The measured November data supports a different reading: the threat to next year's earnings is the price Fair Isaac has frozen itself, not share it has lost.

Pulled widely, priced rarely

In the first ten days of November, 173 sellers delivered agency loans carrying a VantageScore 4.0 pull, up from 148 in October, but only 37 of them applied the score to determine credit pricing. Loans priced on VantageScore-based models were roughly 8% of conventional originations in October on Bank of America research, slipping to about 7% in early November. Rocket Mortgage and UWM alone accounted for 95% of agency mortgage bonds priced via VantageScore in October; PennyMac has since completed rollout across its three production channels, joining them.

So adoption is concentrated in a handful of very large originators, and Fair Isaac has signed direct licenses with resellers representing about 73% of mortgage reseller volume.

The price step was the growth

Fiscal 2026 revenue reached $2.535bn, up 27.3%, at a 49.9% operating margin. Scores revenue was $414m in the fourth quarter, up 33%, with business-to-business up 39% on a higher mortgage unit price while origination volumes fell high single digits. The lift came from an administered move in the wholesale score from $4.95 to $10.00. Consensus now has fiscal 2027 revenue at $2.592bn, growth of 2.5%, with net income of $952.5m, down 4.3%.

Chief executive William Lansing told investors on 4 November that the disclosure itself had become the problem. "We were attacked because we're transparent and clear about our score price," he said. "On a go-forward basis, our intention is to not share every detail. We're not going to share every pricing program. We're not going to share what the prices are."

What is left to grow

With price fixed and volumes soft, next year's growth has to come from units and from software. The FICO Platform side reached $453m of annual recurring revenue, up 72% and 53% of the total, on trailing-twelve-month bookings of $140m, up 36%. The 10T adopter program covers 84 lenders representing $684bn of eligible annual originations, and the Federal Housing Administration will accept 10T from 1 January; certification by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac is still pending.

The shares have fallen in a downtrend since early August and are down 59.6% over twelve months, to 19.0x trailing and 15.7x forward earnings on a 7.1% free-cash-flow yield, against roughly 65x trailing a year ago. Reported results do not explain that: earnings per share grew 37% while the multiple compressed about 70%. The forward numbers do, because the frozen price takes the pricing lever off the table for a year while mortgage volume stays rate-bound. Buybacks absorbed $3.028bn in fiscal 2026, about a tenth of the shares, before the company paused them in the fourth quarter to pay down term debt at 3.29x leverage.

Lansing called 10T "far and away the best score available" and said 2027 would be a big year for it. The score Fair Isaac wants lenders to adopt is the one it has agreed not to charge more for.

Warner Music told investors on 19 November that the artificial-intelligence music service it licensed in September contributed immaterially to the quarter just ended. The same report showed fiscal-year operating income up 69.6% to $1,177m, with operating margin moving from 10.3% to 15.8%.

The three-month advance in the shares of the music-rights owners has been read as the moment AI finally started paying for music. What Warner's fiscal fourth quarter shows is a company earning more from the same streams: price pass-through on existing subscriptions, a cost programme below the gross line, and the disposal of a merchandise business. The licence chief executive Robert Kyncl calls a watershed still has no figure attached to it, on the call or in the annual filing.

Fifteen points of paid streaming, and where two came from

Subscription streaming growth accelerated to 15% in the September quarter, and management decomposed it. "First, we had high single-digit subscriber growth of 6% to 7%. Second, we had pricing which contributed about 4.5% of growth. That's up sequentially from prior quarter where we delivered 3.5% of growth," acting chief financial officer Louis Dickler said on the 19 November call. About a point came from market-share gains, a point from the Revelator acquisition and a point from a one-time $7m audit waiver. Two of the fifteen points, then, do not recur.

The pricing contribution is also dated. Warner guided fiscal 2027 to high single-digit comparable revenue growth and said the pricing benefit to paid streaming moderates in its second quarter, when the Spotify price-increase anniversary laps, with smaller benefits thereafter pending new agreements with the streaming services. Reported revenue growth had already decelerated through the year, from 16.7% in the second quarter to 7.1% in the fourth, and consensus has fiscal 2027 revenue rising just 5.9% to $7.78bn.

The gross line barely moved

Gross margin was 46.3% against 45.8% a year earlier, and 47.8% two years ago. Royalty economics at the gross line stood still; everything that improved, improved below it. Warner's 2025 restructuring plan realised $200m of savings in fiscal 2026 and is expected to reach roughly $300m of annualised run-rate by the end of fiscal 2027, while the sale of merchandiser EMP, about $180m of fiscal 2026 revenue with immaterial profit, adds an estimated 50 basis points to fiscal 2027 margin. Adjusted operating income before depreciation and amortisation reached $1,770m, with margin up 2.1 points to 21.7%.

Suno is live and still not a number

"Our artists and songwriters will be getting paid from an AI creative tool for the first time in history," Kyncl said on 19 November. "This is a watershed moment for the industry brought about by our efforts." The Suno model went live halfway through September; Suno has well over two million paying subscribers; Warner guides only to a material revenue contribution in fiscal 2027. Its fiscal 2026 annual report carries AI licensing in the outlook with no revenue figure and no partner terms. Warner has also agreed with Udio to build a licensed creation service and signed Klay and Stability AI. The largest adjacent prize is unsigned: Spotify's AI remix product, reported for early 2027 at roughly $4 to $5 a month, had licences from Universal, Merlin and Believe while Warner and Sony were still negotiating in early November.

Reservoir is paid by the rate curve

Reservoir Media, a 100-employee New York buyer of publishing catalogues and masters, grew September-quarter revenue 9.8% to $49.9m, of which only about four points were organic, and raised fiscal 2027 guidance on 4 November to $188m-$192m of revenue and $77m-$80m of adjusted earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. The royalty growth does not reach earnings: interest expense of $7.0m and amortisation of $8.5m together take most of $22.1m of quarterly adjusted EBITDA, and net debt stood at $433.8m. Consensus prices no inflection, with the forward earnings multiple of 61.3x sitting above the 58.6x trailing. On enterprise value of roughly $1.10bn, the company trades near 14x its own guided EBITDA, inside the 12-18x of net publisher's share at which private catalogue deals are clearing after the risk-free rate moved from under 1% to over 4% and compressed the 18-25x of 2021.

That mechanism is how Reservoir touches this story at all. Its posture toward the AI services is litigation, over unauthorised use: "We will continue to advocate for a framework that supports technological progress while protecting the creative work at the heart of our industry," chief executive Golnar Khosrowshahi said on 4 November. It bids for songs against the majors' own vehicles, including Warner's 50/50 joint venture with Bain Capital, launched with up to $1.2bn of investment capacity, which bought about $650m of catalogues in the first half of calendar 2026. Reservoir's own lever is price discipline. Its 30-day gain is better explained by the contest for the company: a special committee formed in March is still weighing a $10.50-a-share cash proposal from Wesbild and Richmond Hill and an indication of interest at $10.00 to $11.00 from Irenic Capital, and that $10.50 now sits 3.6% above the $10.12 share price, against a 9.3% discount at the start of October.

What the print accounts for

Warner's shares rose 20.6% over three months, and the largest leg was 15-16 October, a 9.8% move on three to seven times normal volume with no company disclosure that day; a broker upgrade is the discoverable event. When the quarter actually beat, with revenue of $2.001bn against $1.91bn expected and adjusted earnings of $0.55 a share against $0.36, brokers raised targets and the stock moved 0.4%. The re-rating preceded the evidence: Warner trades at 17.8x forward earnings against 14.9x in early October, and at 23.0x trailing.

So the fundamentals account for the margin half of the move and none of the AI half. Consensus asks for 23% earnings growth on 5.9% more revenue, which is a bet on cost conversion from a programme already two-thirds realised, arriving as the pricing tailwind laps. Reservoir's cell of the same story resolves differently because its economics are a funding cost: a lower policy rate lifts its asset marks and lowers its interest bill without adding a stream, and a cash bid at $10.50 caps the question the royalties were supposed to answer.

The test is Warner's second fiscal quarter, when the price pass-through thins out and the AI licence has to arrive as a disclosed number.

Digi International, which sells cellular routers and radio modules for machine-to-machine connectivity plus the cloud software that manages them, told investors on 11 November that its $200m recurring-revenue goal would land in fiscal 2027, a full year ahead of the original target. The shares fell 13.9% the next session and kept sliding, closing 20 November at $51.10, down 31.8% over a month.

That markdown, and the matching one at Zebra Technologies, has been read as the leading edge of a capital-spending pause: warehouses, store floors and truck fleets deferring their device refresh. Neither company's results say that. What did break is a cost line, and it broke in the memory market.

Zebra's fastest quarter of the year, and its thinnest margin

Zebra, the largest supplier of rugged mobile computers, barcode scanners and thermal label printers to retail, logistics and healthcare, grew revenue 22.3% to $1.614bn in the quarter ended 3 October, ahead of 20.4% in the second quarter and 14.3% in the first. It raised full-year sales growth guidance to roughly 17% from about 15%. Transportation and logistics was its fastest-growing end market, with what management called a robust multi-year pipeline into a 2027 refresh; North America grew 11% and Europe 13%, and no region shrank.

Gross margin, though, fell to 45.7% from 48.0% a year earlier. The full-year memory headwind rose to $145m from a prior $120m estimate, with the increase attributed to higher mobile-computing volumes rather than worse pricing. Zebra took an October price increase that offset about $25m of the quarter's roughly $50m memory hit. "It generates about a 15% increase on our mobile computing products," chief financial officer Nathan Winters said on the 3 November call.

The obvious suspicion is that the price rise pulled orders forward. Management rejected it on the same call: "we really haven't seen any impact on our revenue associated with pull forward." The $90m sequential inventory build to $851m was advance stocking of memory, not unsold product. Operating income rose 29.5% on revenue up 22.3%.

Digi declined to charge for the shortage

Digi's fiscal 2026 revenue rose 24.4% to $535.2m with operating income up 42.1%, and recurring revenue reached a record $197m, up 30%. Its gross margin was 63.7%, down 24 basis points, essentially unmoved. Part of that is mix: reported revenue is now split roughly half one-time, half recurring, and subscriptions do not buy DRAM. Part is a choice. "We don't want to take advantage of any kind of one-time or transitionary increase in pricing," chief executive Ron Konezny told investors on 11 November, describing both cost and allocation challenges in memory. The proximate trigger for the fall was narrower than the year: first-quarter revenue guidance of $140-144m sat below the $144.7m consensus, while the full-year guide of 10-15% revenue growth was intact.

The name with nothing to ship fell the same amount

Ituran Location and Control, an Israeli telematics firm paid a monthly subscriber fee for stolen-vehicle recovery and fleet tracking, has no order book to soften. Its subscriber base reached 2,711,000 at the end of June, up 163,000 in a year, on revenue up 20.7%.

In the five sessions of 16-20 November, Zebra fell 6.9%, Digi 7.8% and Ituran 7.9%, while the Nasdaq Composite fell 10% on the week. A device refresh business, a subscription-attached hardware business and a pure annuity were marked down within a percentage point of each other.

What the quarters cover and what they do not

The results account for the direction of demand and none of the price action. Zebra now trades at 14.1x forward earnings against 24.8x trailing and a roughly 20-25x five-year history; in mid-August it was near 17x forward and 34.5x trailing, on a lower earnings base. Digi is at 16.6x forward fiscal-2027 earnings against 36.0x trailing, with its price-to-gross-profit ratio down from 9.62x on 18 August to 5.68x. Ituran is the cheapest of the three at 12.4x forward and 7.4x enterprise value to EBITDA.

The genuine unknown is 2027 cost. AI data centers are projected to absorb roughly 70% of high-end DRAM output in 2026, and supply is expected to stay tight through next year. Zebra quantified this year's headwind precisely and declined to quantify next year's; consensus has its revenue growth slowing to 7.2% in 2027. That is a forecastability problem, and it is a real one.

Ituran reports third-quarter results on 24 November. If its subscriber count kept climbing through a quarter in which it lost a fifth of its value, the last argument that this was an edge-demand event goes with it.
