Sysco Bid $29.1bn for Independent Customers US Foods Is Winning One Salesperson at a Time

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The case of chicken a distributor delivers carries its product cost straight through to the restaurant, so revenue cannot say whether the business improved. The mark-up on a delivered case can, and the mix between re-bid chain contracts and independent kitchens decides it.

US Foods lifted adjusted gross profit per case to $8.64 in its June quarter, the fifth consecutive quarter of accelerating independent volume, while chain cases fell. Performance Food Group's foodservice arm grew independent cases nearly 6%. Sysco's local case growth, at 1.7% for fiscal 2026, trailed both, and its answer is a $29.1bn purchase of cash-and-carry warehouses serving independents.

All three shares are lower over three months. Only Sysco's slide has a cause inside its own filings: roughly $21bn of new debt and 91.5m new shares.

On 30 March 2026, Sysco, the Houston distributor that supplies restaurants, hospitals and schools in the US, Canada, the UK and France, agreed to pay about $29.1bn to the owners of Jetro Restaurant Depot, 167 large-format cash-and-carry warehouses in 35 states serving more than 725,000 independent restaurants and foodservice operators. Sysco shares sank 12% that day. What it agreed to buy is the only customer type in this industry where the mark-up on a case is worth having.

Broadline distributors get read through revenue, as though the top line tallied restaurant demand. It mostly tallies product cost, passed through to the operator. US Department of Agriculture forecasters expect beef and veal prices up 9.4% in 2026; those dollars inflate a distributor's sales line and carry no profit with them unless the mark-up rises too. The meters that work are gross profit per delivered case, the operating cost of delivering it, and the split between volume-guaranteed chain contracts and independent kitchens won one account at a time.

Where $8.64 a case comes from

US Foods, which runs roughly 70 broadline depots and 80 CHEF'STORE cash-and-carry sites out of Rosemont, Illinois, discloses the meter directly. In the quarter to 27 June 2026 its adjusted gross profit per case rose 5.0% to $8.64 while adjusted operating expense per case rose 3.7% to $5.93, leaving adjusted EBITDA per case up 8.3% to $2.73. Revenue grew 4.5% to $10.53bn; gross profit grew 8.0%.

The mix did that work. Total cases rose only 1.9%, independent restaurant cases 5.1% and chain cases fell 1.5%. The independent figure was the fifth consecutive quarter of acceleration and the strongest since the fourth quarter of 2023, achieved against falling industry foot traffic; chief executive Dave Flitman attributed it to "healthy new account growth and improved penetration with existing customers." Private-label penetration inside the independent book reached 53%. Not all of the per-case gain is structural: the company credits a $19m favorable LIFO adjustment, vendor-management timing and higher customer fuel surcharges as well.

Sysco's answer to 1.7%

Sysco's equivalent meter is local case volume, up 1.7% for fiscal 2026 and 2.6% in the fourth quarter. "In our USFS segment, we grew local cases 0.5% in the first half of the year, and we grew local cases 2.9% in the second half of the year," chief executive Kevin Hourican told investors on 4 August 2026 — real improvement, and still behind both rivals. Group revenue says nothing useful about it: International sales rose 7.6% but only 4.1% in constant currency, and SYGMA, the chain-logistics arm, turned $2.2bn of sales into $30m of operating income. Full-year revenue rose 3.9% to $84.55bn while net income fell 3.9%.

Restaurant Depot is reported to earn roughly 13% EBITDA margins against Sysco's 5.2% on a format with no truck, no route and no salesperson. The bill is $21.6bn of cash and 91.5m new shares, about 19% of the 480.6m diluted count, with pro forma net debt of $32.3bn, or 4.89x adjusted EBITDA. Moody's cut the senior unsecured rating to Baa2. The Independent Restaurant Coalition has asked the Federal Trade Commission to block the deal on the argument that Restaurant Depot is the one wholesale alternative letting independents avoid minimum orders and delivery fees, and Representatives Jerrold Nadler and Maxwell Frost have demanded an investigation.

The 11.9% that is cigarettes

Performance Food Group has the largest sales line of the three and the least informative. Foodservice was $36.56bn of $67.58bn in segment sales, Convenience $25.97bn; consolidated gross margin of 11.9%, against roughly 18% at both rivals, is the arithmetic of cigarettes and excise taxes crossing the top line. Convenience supplied 38% of revenue and 21% of fourth-quarter segment adjusted EBITDA. The foodservice arm, meanwhile, posted "nearly 6% independent case growth," chief executive Scott McPherson said on the August call — the best of the three — and fiscal 2027 adjusted EBITDA is guided to $2.125–2.225bn, 12.7% growth at the midpoint.

Three declines, two of them unexplained

All three are lower over three months: US Foods 2.1%, Sysco 4.7%, Performance Food Group 15.3%. The sector reason on offer arrived on 29 September, when JPMorgan trimmed its US Foods target to $98 from $108 and adjusted estimates across the group, citing a narrowing gap between restaurant and grocery prices and rising diesel. The gap is real — USDA now forecasts food away from home up 3.5% and groceries up 2.4% in 2026 — but it is a forecast about menu pricing, not evidence about mark-up per case, which rose.

On price against gross profit, the comparable measure when reported margins differ by more than six points, Performance Food Group is the cheapest of the three at 1.84x trailing, Sysco sits at 2.39x and US Foods at 3.05x. Two of those discounts are unexplained by the per-case record; one is not. Sysco's 15.4x forward earnings is computed before the dilution and the debt land, so the apparent cheapness is a measurement artifact rather than a judgment. US Foods' 20.4x forward against 29.5x trailing simply reflects its own guided 18–24% adjusted earnings-per-share growth.

The FTC blocked Sysco's attempt to buy US Foods in 2015. It is now being asked to block Sysco from buying the warehouses independent operators use to get around distributors like Sysco, with management expecting to close in early 2027. Whichever way that goes, the question it settles is narrow and expensive: whether the richest case in American foodservice can be bought in bulk, or has to be sold.

Vicor told investors on 30 September that a single non-exclusive patent licence would carry third-quarter revenue growth past 30% sequentially — the second upgrade to the same quarter in nine days, both attributed to royalties rather than shipped hardware. The audited test of that claim arrives on 20 October.

Royalties were $30.4m of the June quarter's $143.4m of revenue and carry almost no cost of sales. On the same 800-volt supplier list, Navitas has produced no gross profit across four quarters; Bel Fuse, which actually ships data-center magnetics, saw that line grow 55%.

Vicor raised the revenue guidance for a single quarter twice inside nine days, and neither raise came from a module leaving a loading dock. The Andover, Massachusetts maker of brick-format DC-DC converters and custom power systems told investors on 30 September that third-quarter revenue would grow more than 30% sequentially, up from the "more than 20%" it had promised on 21 September, which in turn replaced nearly 10%. Both increases were attributed to higher royalties from one agreement. From the $143.4m June base, more than 30% implies a quarter above roughly $186m.

The market has treated the sequence as proof that Vicor's power-delivery hardware is winning sockets in artificial-intelligence racks. The company's own releases say something narrower: it is being paid for permission. The agreement behind both raises, signed on 16 September, licenses Vertical Power Delivery — the topology that pushes current up into the processor from below — to an unnamed artificial-intelligence equipment maker on a non-exclusive basis, and expressly permits the licensee to buy the covered modules from third-party suppliers Vicor does not supply.

The royalty is scheduled, the leverage is a docket

Royalties were $30.4m, or 21.2%, of June-quarter revenue, the company disclosed on 21 July, of which an earlier agreement contributed $15m, with $5m set for the third quarter and $10m in each of the four quarters after that. That is a payment calendar rather than an order book, and because royalties carry essentially no cost of sales they drop almost whole into operating income. Net income of $49.8m exceeded operating income of $34.9m in the June quarter, so roughly 30% of the bottom line was earned below the operating line.

What creates the calendar is enforcement. In February 2025 the U.S. International Trade Commission upheld two Vicor patents and barred importation of unlicensed computing systems containing infringing power modules, with cease-and-desist orders against Delta Electronics and Quanta Computer entities. "Since the first patent to VPD was only recently asserted, it is still possible to secure a license at an early stage of escalation with a low royalty rate," chief executive Patrizio Vinciarelli said in the 21 September release.

The docket also runs the other way. The same determination found two Foxconn affiliates licensed to one patent through boilerplate in purchase orders for Vicor components, a ruling Vicor is appealing, and a second investigation naming Luxshare, Hon Hai, Delta, Quanta, Monolithic Power, Infineon, Flex and Celestica carries a target completion date of 20 September 2027.

The hardware is not the problem; the price is the question

Product revenue of $112.9m grew 31.8% year on year, book-to-bill ran above one and one-year backlog rose 26% sequentially to $379.7m. Reported gross margin nonetheless fell from 65.3% to 58.0%, consistent with a larger royalty share a year earlier rather than with product-margin decay.

Shares rose 68.0% from $183.91 on 16 September to $308.90 on 2 October, then gave back 13.9% to $266.07 on 9 October alongside the 7 October selloff in artificial-intelligence names and 24-year-high long Treasury yields. At 83.4x trailing earnings the stock is dearer than almost anything in power components, yet cheaper on gross profit than it was in May 2026 at a similar price: 44.9x against 61.8x. Earnings grew into the multiple. A third of the forward growth consensus embeds is contracted royalty income.

The contrast on the same Nvidia 800-volt supplier list is what makes the licence remarkable. Navitas, a fabless designer of gallium-nitride and silicon-carbide power chips, reported $10.5m of revenue for the June quarter, down 27.3%, with cumulative negative gross profit across four quarters and 77.0x trailing sales to carry; its in-rack 800-volt content is dated to 2027 and later. Bel Fuse, which makes magnetics, connectors and power supplies rather than chips, grew its data solutions line 55% to about $58m and sits on 24.8x forward earnings against consensus earnings growth of 45.9%, with no long-term debt — and its shares are down 10.3% over three months.

Vicor is the only one of the three whose third-quarter number is already half-written, and it was written by lawyers. The print lands at 7:00am Eastern on 20 October, and the line investors will read first is not shipments.

Grand Canyon Education Traded $20m of Annual Revenue for a Fee Its University Cannot Cancel

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Education shares have been sold on the theory that the demographic cliff is arriving. The filings of the three largest listed operators describe two unrelated payers, only one of which is a student.

Grand Canyon Education rewrote its master services agreement with Grand Canyon University on 29 July 2026, surrendering roughly $20m of annual service revenue from housing, food and athletics fees in exchange for a term to 30 June 2041 and the removal of the university's right to walk away. The fee percentage, 60% of tuition and academic-related fees, did not move, and GCU has frozen tuition for an 18th year, so the base grows only on headcount: second-quarter revenue rose 6.7% on 7.5% more enrolled students. American Public Education owns its schools instead, drawing 41% of American Public University System's revenue from Pentagon tuition assistance with its 90/10 ratio at 89%. Only its de-rating has a business event under it, and that event is one quarter's phasing.

Grand Canyon Education does not own a university. It sells admissions and financial-aid counselling, marketing, learning technology and back-office finance to higher-education institutions, chiefly Grand Canyon University, the Phoenix nonprofit it sold itself out of in 2018, and it is paid a contractual percentage of what that university bills. On 29 July 2026 the two sides rewrote the contract. The amended and restated agreement re-bases the fee to 60% of GCU's tuition and academic-related fees alone, hands the revenue from student housing, food service, athletic tickets, the arena, hotel, golf course and apparel operations wholly back to the university, runs an initial fifteen-year term to 30 June 2041, and deletes GCU's right to terminate for convenience.

Management put the cost of the re-basing at about $20m of annual service revenue and less than $1m a quarter of operating income, because the ancillary revenue surrendered carried almost all of its own cost. That trade is the whole argument about this corner of the market. The weakness in education shares has been read as the demographic cliff arriving early; what the disclosures describe is a company whose revenue is one counterparty's billed tuition times a percentage it cannot reset, sitting beside a company whose largest payer is the Department of Defense.

A fee that can only grow on bodies

GCU has extended its tuition freeze for an 18th consecutive year. The counterparty that sets the base of the fee has chosen not to raise price, which leaves volume as the only lever. Second-quarter service revenue of $264.0m was up 6.7% on enrollments up 7.5%, to 121,921 students, of whom 113,011 study online. Divide the one by the other and the fee earned per enrolled student was about $2,166 against $2,182 a year earlier — a derived figure, and a falling one.

The margin came from cost discipline. Marketing and communication ran at 22.7% of revenue against 22.6%, counselling services at 33.4% against 33.5%, and operating income rose 12.3% to $58.2m, lifting the operating margin to 22.0% from 20.9%.

The smaller hybrid-campus business, which supports healthcare programmes for university partners, is capped by permits rather than appetite. Chief executive Brian Mueller's team told investors on 30 July that enrollment grew 8.5% across 47 active locations to nearly 6,000 students, that 14 locations are at or near capacity and need further state approvals to expand, and that 22 locations will show no year-on-year growth in new autumn enrollments because eight of them already started the maximum number of students their states authorised a year earlier.

Kaplan is paid the same way

Graham Holdings is filed as a conglomerate, with television stations, car dealerships, burners and lumber, and its education arm is usually treated as a sideline. It is not one: Kaplan's $50.3m of second-quarter operating income was 60% of the group's $83.6m, on education revenue that was only 32% of the top line. And Kaplan collects on the identical architecture — a 30-year support agreement under which it takes a fee of 12.5% of Purdue University Global's revenue, 13% for the years to 30 June 2027. Graham's education revenue fell 4% last quarter while its education operating income rose 9%.

American Public Education's payer is an appropriation

American Public Education owns its institutions outright and therefore keeps every obligation the fee collectors contracted away. In 2025, students using Pentagon tuition assistance as their primary payment source accounted for 41% of American Public University System's revenue, veterans' benefits 26% and Title IV federal student aid 19%. Since the American Rescue Plan Act, military and veterans' money counts on the federal side of the 90/10 rule, and APUS's ratio stood at 89% — one point from the limit, with the company stating it cannot assure future compliance.

The operating news was good. Second-quarter revenue rose 5.5% to $171.7m, gross margin widened to 55.4% from 51.8%, operating income nearly doubled to $13.5m, and full-year guidance was raised to revenue of $690m–$698m and diluted earnings of $2.48–$2.79. On 4 August the company folded Rasmussen University and Hondros College of Nursing into APUS, lifting the last regulatory restriction on Rasmussen's total enrollment. "The foundation is built, the business is simplified, the balance sheet is strong, and now the institutional combination is complete," chief executive Angela Selden told investors on 10 August.

The quarter after it was the problem. Third-quarter revenue was guided to $164.5m–$167.0m against consensus of $169.9m, and earnings to $0.18–$0.29 against $0.56, implying military course registrations up 1.0%–3.0% and nursing enrollment up 2.5%.

What the de-rating got right

Shares of Grand Canyon Education fell 7.0% over the two sessions after the amendment and raised guidance were published, and at $159.80 remain 22.8% below the $207.00 of a year ago. Price against trailing gross profit has compressed from roughly 10.3x then to 6.85x while that gross profit grew 7.6%; the forward multiple of 15.6x earnings sits below the trailing 19.2x. American Public Education is down 17.2% over three months and trades at 2.41x trailing gross profit, back from 3.07x in July, having given back most of a year's re-rating.

Only one of those two moves has a business event under it, and it is the smaller one: a phasing problem inside a year the company raised. The larger move has no deterioration behind it at all. The genuine damage in this industry landed on the owners of Title IV-funded campuses — Lincoln Educational Services fell from $43.20 in early August to $23.49, after reporting that enrollment grew about 9% while student starts rose about 1% "as fewer enrolled students than expected attended the first day of class", with management pointing at the restart of federal loan repayments in May 2026. A fee computed on a tuition invoice does not feel a student-credit shock the way a school that must collect from the student does.

What it feels instead is policy. The Education Department's 2011 bundled-services exception is what permits an online program manager to be paid a share of tuition at all, and the department has been soliciting comment on that exception and on the incentive-compensation prohibition it carves out of. Projections of US high-school graduates have the count peaking in 2025 and falling about 13% through 2041, even as total fall 2025 enrollment came in roughly 1% above the year before. The pipeline is a slope, not a cliff edge, and it runs the length of the new agreement.

So Grand Canyon Education has fixed its share of one university's frozen tuition until 2041, and fixed it over exactly the span in which the supply of eighteen-year-olds is expected to thin. The contract settles who gets paid and for how long. It cannot settle how many people walk through the door.

The October cut named geography, not the checkout page

Wells Fargo cut its price target on Booking Holdings, which collects a commission on hotel rooms, flights and cars it does not own through Booking.com, Agoda, Priceline, KAYAK and OpenTable, to $190 from $220 on 7 October, keeping an Equal Weight rating. The reason the bank gave was not artificial intelligence. It cited prolonged Middle East conflict pressuring long-haul demand, weighing on Booking more than peers given its geographic footprint, and trimmed its third-quarter room-night growth estimate to 5.5%.

The preceding six weeks had been read as the disintermediation trade arriving at last. Meta launched Muse, a personal AI agent able to complete a booking by itself, in the United States on 8 September; Expedia agreed on 22 September to sell hotel rooms inside it while remaining the merchant collecting the payment, and the market read the arrangement as a loss for both names, Expedia closing 5.5% lower that session and Booking 4.2%. Booking's own finance chief had already sized that channel. Referral traffic from large language models is "under 1% of room nights" and "hasn't moved so much recently," Ewout Steenbergen said on the 4 August call.

What Booking's August guidance already contained

The company's framework for the year assumed seven of its twelve months carry Middle East disruption. "We are at a high single-digit level for gross bookings and revenues and at mid-teens level for EPS at the high end," Steenbergen told investors on 4 August. Consensus has since settled where that guidance pointed: revenue up 9.6% for 2026 and earnings per share up 15.0%.

The June quarter is the base. Revenue of $7.352bn grew 8.1% year on year, half the 16.2% of the March quarter, on gross bookings of $51.0bn — a 14.4% commission take. Room nights rose 5% while marketing spend rose 11%, to $2.37bn, which management attributed on the same call to "changes in traffic mix, incremental investments in paid marketing at attractive ROIs, and a shift of merchandising spend to performance marketing." Operating margin still widened, to 34.0% from 33.1%, so the deceleration is a volume and price story rather than a cost one.

The route around it by acquisition is closed. On 9 September the EU General Court upheld the prohibition of Booking's eTraveli deal, the flight-booking purchase that would have put the air ticket next to the room night.

Meanwhile the rooms themselves are firming. CoStar and Tourism Economics raised their 2026 US revenue-per-available-room forecast to 4.4% from 2.8%. It is the flights that thinned: the International Air Transport Association cut 2026 passenger traffic growth to 2.1% from 4.9% and expects Middle East traffic to contract 11.4% in a region that handles more than 40% of global connecting traffic, on jet fuel averaging $152 a barrel against roughly $90 in 2025.

Booking closed at $160.34 on 9 October, down 24.8% from its 25 August close and 26.0% over twelve months. The shares now change hands at 15.3x forward earnings against 17.7x trailing and a ten-year average CNBC puts at 22.3x, with a 7.7% trailing free-cash-flow yield.

Expedia's year went the other way

Expedia Group, which runs Brand Expedia, Hotels.com and Vrbo alongside a wholesale arm selling travel inventory into other companies' checkout pages, has grown revenue faster for four straight quarters, reaching 14.0% in the June quarter, with operating margin at 23.9% against 14.0% a year earlier. Its business-to-business gross bookings rose 21%, to $10.74bn, against 8% in consumer bookings, and the cash it collects before guests travel — deferred merchant bookings — reached $14.4bn at 30 June against $9.3bn at year-end. It trades at 13.1x forward earnings against 16.4x trailing and is up 22.5% over twelve months, the only one of the three still higher. Chief executive Ariane Gorin told the Skift Global Forum on 23 September: "I certainly personally don't believe that a large portion of bookings are going to be an end-to-end conversational experience." A Sherman Act class action filed in Washington federal court on 2 October alleges it imposed restrictive pricing terms on hotels.

The rupee does MakeMyTrip's talking

MakeMyTrip, the Indian platform behind makemytrip.com, goibibo.com and redbus.in, grew June-quarter gross bookings 19.9% in constant currency, to $2,854.7m, with hotels and packages outgrowing air ticketing. Reported revenue grew 6.2%; the rupee touched a record low of 96.775 per dollar on 9 October, down about 9% over twelve months. Net income fell 67.8%, to $8.35m, and the shares still carry 91.4x forward earnings after a 53.0% twelve-month decline.

So the industry label describes an accounting convention, not a shared exposure. Booking's markdown is part earned: growth genuinely halved and the traffic bill is rising faster than the volume it buys, which is a margin claim on every future booking. What the October evidence does not support is the version in which the checkout page was taken away, because the hotel market it distributes was revised up and the measured AI referral share is under a hundredth of room nights. MakeMyTrip's decline is translation plus a collapsed profit line at the group's dearest multiple. Expedia's fall of 18.8% since late August has no deterioration behind it at all.

Booking's last disclosed room-night growth was 5%. Its third-quarter results are due on 3 November and Expedia's on 4 November, and the line that settles the argument is not revenue but the count of nights actually stayed.

Federal energy forecasters told households on 6 October that heating with propane will cost less this winter, about 3% less than last. In the Northeast the saving is larger: the Energy Information Administration, the statistical arm of the US Department of Energy, expects both lower prices and decreasing consumption to pull propane-heating expenditures down 15% against a year earlier. For a distributor whose revenue is gallons multiplied by a price, that is a smaller top line arriving just as the season starts.

It is also close to irrelevant to what Suburban Propane Partners earns. The master limited partnership distributes propane, fuel oil and refined fuels to roughly 1 million residential, commercial, industrial and agricultural customers across 42 states from about 700 locations, and its profit is a unit margin: cents on each retail gallon, the spread between a wholesale cost it pays and a posted retail price it sets. Cheap, well-supplied propane is the friendly end of that arrangement. Inventories stood at 103m barrels in the week to 26 September, about 13m barrels above the previous five-year average, with Gulf Coast stocks some 21% above theirs, and the Mont Belvieu wholesale benchmark was quoted at $0.919 a gallon on 6 October.

The June quarter separated gallons from gross profit

Suburban's fiscal third quarter, ended 27 June, is the cleanest demonstration available. Retail propane gallons came to 70.6 million, down 1.8%, against average temperatures across its service territories 17% warmer than normal. Total gross margin was $160.3m, unchanged from a year earlier, and the company's release attributed the underlying $3.9m decline excluding mark-to-market effects "primarily due to lower propane volumes sold, as propane unit margins remained steady."

The weather did not take the margin; it took the volume, and the volume loss was smaller than the weather implied. Chief executive Michael Stivala said the quarter's volumes "benefited from continued strength and growth in our counter-seasonal customer base," with May and June running ahead of the prior year on agricultural, industrial and national-account business.

What did deteriorate was the earnings line below the margin. Adjusted EBITDA fell to $18.0m from $27.0m on higher operating costs alongside the warmth, and revenue inched up 0.5% to $261.4m.

The socket count is the slow problem

Neither protagonist can buy its way out of attrition. Group guidance of $2.75-$2.90 in adjusted earnings per share for fiscal 2026 implies a down year against $3.09 reported for fiscal 2025, and at 11.5x forward earnings against 11.9x trailing, UGI is the cheapest reading among the listed gas distributors. How much of Suburban's low the business explains

The rest looks like a sector repricing that Suburban happened to sit inside. Nothing in the October outlook tells either of them how cold January will be, and the gallon count remains the number the weather owns. The spread is the half they set themselves, and an overstocked market has handed them the easier half.

Five companies, five different bills

Modine Manufacturing, which builds coils, chillers and air handlers to order for hyperscale data centers and for vehicle makers, nearly doubled sales in its data-center business in the June quarter and lost more than nine percentage points of gross margin doing it. The segment reported $348.6m against $183.7m a year earlier, and its gross margin came in at 20.2%, 960 basis points below the prior year, because shortages of certain key components limited what the plants could produce.

The complex was sold in late July on the premise that hyperscalers were about to stop ordering heat-rejection gear. On the evidence the three largest listed sellers of that gear have published, the ordering never slowed. What slipped is the money earned turning an order into a shipped unit, and that distinction is the whole argument now sitting in front of three reports due in the next month.

The order book is the part that is working

Neil D. Brinker, Modine's president and chief executive, said demand remains robust "as evidenced by three consecutive quarters of record order intake leading to the company's backlog nearly doubling over the past year." Part of the franchise is pre-sold rather than bid project by project: an agreement guarantees Modine's ability to supply more than $4bn of Airedale cooling products between 2027 and 2029, against a $165m upfront payment earmarked for expanding production capacity. Its anchor data-center customers have been reported as Google and Amazon; order visibility and customer concentration are the same disclosure read twice.

AAON's constraint is floor space, and it costs margin

The one that didn't have to de-rate

What the shares did

Modine and AAON are the levered expressions of one end market, converting award-priced backlog while building the capacity to convert it, and that is where a two-point margin miss turns into a 40% drawdown.

Modine reports on 27 October, Trane on 29 October and AAON about 5 November. The question those three prints settle is narrow and answerable: whether the margin on a shipped chiller recovers once the components arrive, or whether building to a price agreed last year is simply what this business now earns.

Zscaler shows how little the meters mattered: the strongest thirty-day gainer of the ten at 41.5%, carrying a price-to-gross-profit of 10.1x in late July — roughly a fifth of CrowdStrike's on the same date — while guiding the slowest growth of the group at 16.6% to 17.4% contracted-revenue growth.

So the order books explain the direction and almost none of the magnitude. Palo Alto's deferral is real, documented and self-inflicted, and its wins land in the obligation line first; CrowdStrike's commitment pool converts at twice the pace revenue shows; Cloudflare's dollar arrives as it is consumed, which is why its gap is one point and its margin is falling. What none of the three supplies is five months of gross-profit growth equal to a doubled multiple. Consensus sits between 10% and 19% above trailing gross profit at each name, derived from the trailing and forward multiples themselves, and the re-rating was several times that.

The free period is the whole strategy and the whole exposure. A customer who commits today may not be billed until a competitor's contract lapses, so the prices set in August rest on revenue nobody will recognize for a year or more. One soft quarter of net new contracted revenue would not disprove the mechanism — it would simply be the only evidence anyone gets to see.
