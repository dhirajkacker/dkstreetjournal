ResMed's Masks Grew 13% Last Year, Faster Than the Machines That Need Them

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

ResMed's advance since the start of October came with no company news attached to it: health care led a defensive rotation out of technology, and the forward earnings multiple expanded from roughly 18.1x to 19.9x on an unchanged fiscal 2027 consensus of $12.10 a share. The quarter the company last reported went the other way.

Mask and accessory revenue reached $2.085bn in the year to 30 June 2026, up 13%, while June-quarter reported gross margin fell to 58.8% from 60.8% and reported operating income declined 1.3%. Growth is coming from the consumable reorder, not from flow generators, life support or the software book being sold.

Inspire Medical, treating the same disease with a one-time implant, is the opposite architecture: revenue fell 7.6% last quarter, and the new surgical billing codes it won do not take effect until 2028.

Mask and accessory sales at ResMed, which sells flow generators, masks and headgear for sleep apnoea through sleep clinics and home-care dealers in roughly 140 countries, reached $2.085bn in the year to 30 June 2026, up 13% from $1.840bn. Group revenue grew 9.9%, which means the machines those masks attach to grew slower than the company did. The dealer reorders cushions and tubing on a resupply schedule; the flow generator sells once.

That annuity is the business now, and it is not what repriced the shares this month. ResMed has gained 10.0% in the nine sessions to 15 October, from $218.89 to $240.77, with nothing company-specific inside the window. Health care led a defensive rotation on 15 October, the sector exchange-traded fund up 2.58% in the session as money left leveraged technology; RBC Capital Markets moved health care to overweight and cut technology to market weight on valuation grounds. On unchanged fiscal 2027 consensus earnings of $12.10, the forward multiple went from about 18.1x to 19.9x in those nine sessions, by derivation from the closing prices: roughly half the distance back to the 24.2x the shares carried a year ago, against 15.1x at the 2 June low of $182.82.

The June quarter moved the other way

Revenue growth decelerated to 8.6% from 10.8% the prior quarter. Reported gross margin fell to 58.8% from 60.8% a year earlier, and reported operating income declined 1.3%. Management's non-GAAP gross margin for the same quarter was 62.3%, up 90 basis points; both are correct on their own basis, and the statutory series is the one showing compression.

Inside the mix, the resupply premium is regional. Americas sleep device revenue grew 8% against Americas masks at 10%; outside the Americas the order reverses, devices 13% against masks 12%. Life support devices fell 45% in the Americas and 38% elsewhere on the Astral halt. The third dollar in the ticker, residential care software, grew 2% in constant currency.

It is also the dollar leaving. ResMed agreed in June to sell its home health and hospice software unit, which includes HEALTHCAREfirst and Citus, to Frazier Healthcare Partners for $490m, with closing expected in the first quarter of fiscal 2027: the quarter reported on 29 October. Roughly $220m of revenue walks out with it, which is why consensus fiscal 2027 revenue growth of 2.9% is portfolio surgery and not a demand reading. The company also announced a $1.5bn repurchase and a 10% dividend raise for the year.

The drug threat shows up as a tailwind

Asked at the Morgan Stanley healthcare conference in September 2026 whether weight-loss drugs cannibalise airway pressure, chief executive Mick Farrell said: "We've got 2.5 million reasons that it's a tailwind. High start rate, high resupply at one year, and even higher than that resupply at three years." The company put therapy start rates 11 percentage points above a control group, with resupply 300 basis points higher at one year. A study of more than 100,000 patients found resupply enrolment halved first-year therapy termination.

Inspire sells the same disease once

Inspire Medical implants a hypoglossal-nerve stimulator per procedure, with no reorder behind it, and the architecture shows. Quarterly revenue went from 12.2% growth to 1.6% to a 7.6% decline, at $200.6m, with first-half operating income at a loss of about $1.6m against $50.95m for all of 2025, on a gross margin of 85.5%. The Senate rejected the resolution to block the Medicare prior-authorisation pilot 46-50 on 16 July, so the paperwork the implant queue runs through stays. Inspire did win new Category I billing codes from the American Medical Association on 1 October, but they take effect on 1 January 2028, with Medicare valuations not final until November 2027. Meanwhile Nyxoah's leadless Genio stimulator, approved in August 2025, is implanting at more than 50 US accounts on $4.5m of US revenue, with both companies suing each other in Delaware.

At 55.7x forward earnings on consensus that has 2026 earnings falling 26.4%, Inspire's shares price a recovery its own reimbursement calendar funds in 2028, well beyond the 3.9% revenue growth consensus pencils for 2027; the forward price-to-sales ratio of 2.34x sits above the trailing 2.22x, which is arithmetic for paying more for a smaller base. The shares are 7.5% lower over thirty days from an 18 September high of $76.78, and still 72% above the May low.

So the two tapes have different authors. Inspire's decline tracks its own disclosures. ResMed's gain does not: the annuity grew, the buyback is real, and the reported margin still fell in the last quarter anyone has seen, which leaves rotation as the likelier explanation for a multiple that re-rated on static estimates.

ResMed's report arrives on 29 October carrying a divestiture, a currency and a mask reorder cycle. What the revenue line will not carry is a count of how many people started therapy.

Falling Power Prices Shrank Air Products' Revenue and Widened Its Margin

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The revenue line of an industrial gas company cannot say whether its business improved, because the electricity and natural gas burned inside a customer's fenceline arrive in sales at zero margin. Air Products' Americas sales rose 5% last quarter with the energy cost rebilled to customers subtracting two points, and that subtraction added roughly 50 basis points to the segment's operating margin.

Linde's own filing splits its growth into six pieces, one of which is the contractual billing of energy cost variances. Where the two part is capital and cost. Air Products took a $2.9bn charge to walk away from Louisiana hydrogen, cut its capital plan, and reported better adjusted margin, return on capital and guidance. Linde's adjusted operating margin fell 60 basis points and its return on capital slipped to 23.5%, even as its signed gas-supply backlog hit a record.

Air Products' Americas business sold more gas in the June quarter and billed its customers less for the power that made it. Segment sales rose 5%, according to the company's quarterly filing: volumes added seven points, price was flat, and the energy cost passed through to customers subtracted two. The margin moved the other way, reaching 29.9% against 29.7% a year earlier, with about 50 basis points of that improvement coming from the same shrinking pass-through.

The top line of an industrial gas company is close to useless, then, as a gauge of whether the business got better. Most of the profit comes from air-separation units built inside a customer's fence under long-term take-or-pay contracts, where power and gas are rebilled at no margin and a fixed facility fee arrives whether the customer runs its plant or not. The meters that answer the question are operating margin, return on capital, and the money each company is committing to new plants. On those, Air Products and Linde have spent this year moving in opposite directions.

Two points of sales that were never profit

Linde, which supplies the same oxygen, nitrogen, argon and hydrogen and also builds the plants that make them, does the arithmetic in public. Its second-quarter 10-Q bridges 9% sales growth, to $9,289m, into six parts: two points of price, two of volume, two of currency, one of acquisitions, one from engineering, and one of "cost pass-through, representing the contractual billing of energy cost variances primarily to onsite customers." Half that growth carries no information about gas sold.

Air Products shows the same line running both ways inside one year. Over the nine months to 30 June, a higher pass-through added two points to Americas sales and cost the segment 50 basis points of margin, which fell to 29.0%.

Air Products got paid for retreating

On 26 June the company exited Casa Grande, Arizona and other small clean-energy distribution projects, citing slower-than-expected market development largely in hydrogen for mobility. Four days later it abandoned the Louisiana Clean Energy Complex, a low-carbon hydrogen and ammonia plant already under construction, on returns grounds. The pre-tax charge runs to $2.9bn, $2.2bn after tax, against cash costs capped at $925m. The NEOM green-hydrogen venture in Saudi Arabia survived; Air Products instead signed a marketing and distribution agreement with Yara for its renewable ammonia.

The shares rose 8.0% on announcement day, from $271.35 to $293.18. Capital spending guidance for fiscal 2026 came down to about $3.5bn from roughly $4.0bn, and the company is steering toward some $1.5bn a year of ordinary industrial-gas plants.

What the trading business did under that charge was improve. Adjusted operating income of $810m rose 9%, adjusted operating margin widened 110 basis points to 25.6%, adjusted earnings per share of $3.47 rose 12%, and return on capital reached 11.7%. Full-year adjusted earnings guidance went up to $13.39–$13.49. "Having taken additional decisions to further optimize our large project portfolio, we have a clear pathway to reduce capital expenditures and drive continued profitable growth through high-quality, traditional industrial gas projects," chief executive Eduardo Menezes said on 30 July.

Linde's margin gave way while its backlog hit a record

Linde's adjusted operating margin narrowed 60 basis points to 29.5% as cost inflation outran price and productivity. Chief executive Sanjiv Lamba, on 31 July, called it "another solid quarter, generating record sales and EPS while maintaining industry-leading profitability with 29.5% operating margin and 23.5% return on capital." That return was below the roughly 24% of the prior quarter. On the call Lamba put the roughly 30 basis points of decline excluding pass-through mainly on the US homecare business, and described the quarter's 2% price attainment as broad-based and generally tracking local inflation.

The order book did not crack. A long-term US electronics supply agreement lifted the contractual sale-of-gas backlog about $1bn to a record $8.1bn, within a total project pipeline near $11bn, and capital spending is guided to $5.5bn–$6.0bn. Linde fell 5.95% the day it reported.

Where the two de-ratings part company

Both stocks peaked on 2 July, Air Products at $314.19 and Linde at $546.64, and sit 6.0% and 9.4% below those marks; Air Products is still up 14.7% over twelve months.

For Air Products the results do not account for the slide. Price to gross profit has eased to 16.3x trailing from 17.5x in early May while margin, return on capital and guidance all improved, and the forward multiple of earnings is 20.4x. Linde's fall from 34.98x trailing earnings in early May to 31.8x rests on compression a reader can see in the filings.

One caveat sits against calling Air Products mispriced: the two now change hands at almost the same trailing enterprise value to cash earnings, 18.9x against Linde's 18.7x, while Air Products earns 11.7% on capital employed and Linde 23.5%. The likelier reading is a market pricing the return gap rather than missing the quarter. In a three-way oligopoly where Linde holds roughly 30% of the global market, Air Liquide about 24% and Air Products some 16%, the asset inside the fence is the moat, and the pressure point is renewal: bulk atmospheric gas contracts in North America and Western Europe get renegotiated as take-or-pay terms expire, which erodes a book without any volume falling.

The answer arrives in a cluster. Linde reports third-quarter results on 29 October and Air Liquide publishes revenue the same morning; Air Products holds its fiscal fourth-quarter call on 5 November. Whichever of them restates backlog or capital spending first will say more about the next decade of fenceline plants than any sales bridge can.

Truckload Rates Rose on a Record Fuel Surcharge; the Rate Carriers Keep Went Backwards

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

September looked like the month the freight recovery arrived: spot and contract rates rose across every trailer type. Strip out the fuel surcharge, which reaches a carrier and leaves again at zero margin, and dry-van pricing fell in both the spot and contract markets.

The second prop softened in the same window. The average retail price of a used Class 8 truck slipped 1.5% from August to $61,000, with retail sales off 7%. That line matters most at Heartland Express, where gains on selling trucks ran at 13.6% of June-quarter revenue and delivered a profit on top of an operating loss.

Werner's dedicated contracts are repricing up 3-5%, and refrigerated rates rose, which separates Marten from the dry-van pair. All three were marked down about a quarter in three months, and the compressed margins behind that are doing rational work.

Two freight data sets published on 13 October took away the arithmetic the truckload trade had been running on. DAT's monthly release reported that spot and contract rates including fuel rose for all three equipment types in September, as the monthly national average on-highway diesel price passed $6 a gallon for the first time. Excluding fuel, van and flatbed rates declined in both the spot and the contract market. Only refrigerated pricing rose.

That distinction is the whole business. An asset-based carrier is paid revenue per mile net of fuel surcharge, multiplied by the miles each tractor runs in a week, multiplied by the number of trucks it can put a driver in. DAT estimates the surcharge reached a record 84 cents a mile for dry van in September, up 14 cents from August, inside an all-in spot rate of $3.01. September's rate print was widely read as the capacity squeeze finally reaching price. What reached price was diesel.

None of those 84 cents pays a driver or a depreciation charge.

The prop under Heartland's profit was the resale lot

The other leg under these shares was used-equipment value. ACT Research's September flash put the same-dealer average retail price of a used Class 8 truck down 1.5% from August to $61,000, softening more than seasonally expected, and retail sales fell 7% from August in a second consecutive monthly decline.

Heartland Express, the North Liberty, Iowa short-to-medium-haul dry-van carrier, is where that line does the heaviest lifting. Gains on the sale of revenue equipment ran at 13.6% of June-quarter revenue against a 2015-25 average of 4.1%, and without them the operating ratio would have been nearer 103% than the 91.0% reported. Net income of $10.6m sat above an operating loss of $8.5m. Heartland holds no quarterly earnings call and discloses no rate or utilisation metrics, so the resale line is the clearest window the company offers. The shares trade at 9.17x enterprise value to trailing earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation and 1.13x book, the cheapest book value of the three, while 75.3x on 2026 consensus earnings falls to roughly 21.8x on 2027 — a 245% per-share increase the price already pays for.

Werner is repricing truck-weeks by the year

Werner Enterprises, the Omaha full-truckload and logistics operator, took the other route out of the spot market. More than 70% of its truckload fleet, about 7,400 trucks, sits under dedicated agreements, and the one-way fleet shrank from nearly 3,300 tractors in 2022 to under 2,400 at the end of 2025. Dedicated revenue per tractor per week rose 5.4% net of fuel surcharge in the June quarter, and the company raised dedicated pricing guidance to up 3-5%. The pace is contractual. Renewals should bring "up low to mid-single digit type increases," senior vice-president of pricing Chris Neal said, and chief executive Derek Leathers added that "you need a couple of years of that to really get dedicated back to its traditional long-term run rate".

Reported earnings flatter and then punish. Revenue grew 24.0% to $933.9m in the June quarter, helped by a dedicated-carrier acquisition completed in January. Operating income fell 74.5% to $16.9m, against a prior-year quarter that carried a $45.7m insurance-and-claims reversal. At 9.93x trailing EV/EBITDA, Werner's forward path runs from 29.2x on 2026 consensus to about 14.8x on 2027, on a 97% jump in net income.

Marten's refrigerated rates helped; its dedicated book did not

Marten Transport hauls temperature-controlled food and consumer goods, and was the one name September's data favored: refrigerated spot rates rose 24 cents from August to $3.62 a mile. Its dedicated segment went the other way, the operating ratio deteriorating to 96.4% from 92.4% a year earlier. Revenue fell 2.8% to $223.5m and operating margin compressed to 2.35% from 4.23%. At 8.31x trailing EV/EBITDA, the cheapest of the three, 46.9x on 2026 consensus falls to roughly 19.7x on 2027.

What the de-rating already accounts for

Revenue trajectories improved at all three: Heartland's year-over-year decline narrowed from 26.1% to 12.5% over three quarters, Marten's from 8.8% to 2.8%. Margins compressed at every one of them. The two lines disagree because what improved was the part of the dollar nobody is paid on, and because the capacity story the shares were bought for has no revenue in it yet. "The truck market has certainly flipped this year, but recent tonnage levels confirm this is due to reduced capacity, not robust demand," American Trucking Associations chief economist Bob Costello said in the September tonnage release, which showed the index down 0.9% to 114.2.

Capacity exit sets a floor under future price; it does not fill a quarter. UBS cut Werner and Heartland to hold from buy and JP Morgan moved both to underweight, arguing a truckload rate recovery would be pushed out. With ex-fuel van pricing lower, used-truck values easing and the weekly national diesel average down from its $6.529 peak in the week of 21 September to $6.199 by 5 October, a markdown from an expensive multiple is doing rational work. The near-identical 8.3x-to-9.9x band across three structurally different fleets says the market repriced a shelf.

Diesel has turned back down from its late-September record, which takes the flattery out of the top line before it takes anything out of cost. Marten prints first, after the close on 22 October, and what it reports will carry less surcharge in it than September's rates did.
