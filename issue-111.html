Celestica's New US Rack Plant Is in Texas, Which Elected a Data-Center Moratorium Governor

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The cost of memory was supposed to be the thing that broke the companies bolting AI racks together. What is attacking them now is permitting. Celestica, Flex and Jabil each beat and raised between 30 September and 28 October — Celestica to $40bn of 2027 revenue at a guided 9.0% adjusted operating margin — and each has since been marked down by a fifth to more than two-fifths, the last leg after midterm elections put data-center moratorium candidates into office in Texas, which holds 27% of planned US capacity.

On the September quarters, only Celestica earns less on its new work than on its old: 8.5 cents of gross profit per added revenue dollar against an 11.0% company average. Flex and Jabil earn more on the marginal dollar than on the average one, which is why the shared bill-of-materials story does not hold across the group.

Celestica raised its 2027 revenue forecast to $40bn in late October, and the shares jumped about 17% in premarket trading. Eight days later they made a twelve-month low, and the cause had nothing to do with memory prices or assembly cost: voters in Texas, where the Toronto contract manufacturer is building its US liquid-cooled rack capacity, elected a governor promising an immediate moratorium on data centers.

The month's markdown across the rack assemblers had been read as the bill of materials finally eating a thin spread. Server DRAM contract prices rose roughly 90–95% in the first quarter of 2026, and memory went from about 18% to roughly 53% of a reference server's bill of materials in eighteen months. For a business that keeps single-digit percentage points of an invoice, that ought to be fatal. The September quarters say it was not — except at one company.

The marginal rack is thin at exactly one of them

Dividing each firm's year-over-year change in gross profit by its change in revenue gives what the new AI work earns against the existing book. The figures are derived, not disclosed. Celestica added $2.64bn of revenue in the September quarter and only $225m of gross profit: 8.5 cents on the marginal dollar against an 11.0% company average, with gross margin down to 10.99% from 13.03%. Management's non-GAAP operating margin was a company record in the same quarter; the reported figure fell to 7.89% from 10.33%. Both are true, and the gap is the adjustment.

Flex, the Singapore-founded design and manufacturing group whose Cloud, Power and Infrastructure unit sells server power supplies, switchgear and busways, ran the other way: 11.7 cents of incremental gross profit against a 9.56% average, on segment revenue up 54% to $2.5bn and segment operating margin up 2.4 percentage points to 10.7%. "Power segment operating margin increased to approximately 16%, up roughly 550-basis points year over year driven by strong volume growth and the benefits of scaling the investments we made last year," chief financial officer Kevin S. Krumm told investors on 28 October. Flex trades at 8.7x trailing gross profit against 21.4x in mid-May.

Jabil, the St Petersburg manufacturing-services firm whose Intelligent Infrastructure segment builds cloud racks and data-center interconnect, grew that segment 56% to $5.8bn and earned 11.2 cents of incremental operating profit against a 5.67% average, on net capital spending of 1.3% of fiscal 2026 revenue. "We are not a contract manufacturer competing with low-cost EMS companies, nor are we a product company competing with our customers," chief executive Michael Dastoor said on 30 September, rejecting the electronics-manufacturing-services label. It is at 7.4x trailing gross profit against 12.8x in May, on a 7.0% free-cash-flow yield. Sanmina and Benchmark Electronics, the diversified assemblers with the least rack content, earned the richest marginal dollars in the group, near 13 cents each.

What the ballots did to the order book

The first week of November did not change a cost. Democratic midterm wins put candidates campaigning on data-center moratoriums into office, and the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.5% in a single session led by buildout names. UBS strategists estimated that roughly 30% of expected 2028 data-center additions could be affected by delays. Texas accounts for 27% of all planned US capacity, and 40 of 41 local ballot measures went against data centers. "This feels like the capitulation phase for the data center capex beneficiary stocks, especially the semiconductors," said Jed Ellerbroek of Argent Capital Management.

One of the three has a fundamental thread for the market to pull.

Celestica's top ten customers were 85% of revenue in the September quarter, with three buyers in one segment at 35%, 17% and 14%. It cut 2026 free-cash-flow guidance to $460m from $600m, told investors 2027 would be a net use of cash on $1.5–2.0bn of capital spending, and raised $3.45bn of equity to fund the ramp. "Our $40 billion guide took into consideration the risks around the ramps, the component availability, and the operational execution, David," chief financial officer Todd Ankenmann said on 27 October. The shares sit at 12.5x trailing gross profit against 26.3x in mid-May, while that gross profit grew 54%; UBS raised its target to $453 against a $206.68 price.

The spread held; the permission did not

Nothing in the three September quarters accounts for a month in which these shares lost between a fifth and more than two-fifths of their value. Guidance went up at all of them. The split in the underlying economics has a cause that survives the selloff: Flex sells power hardware into the rack at a widening margin, Jabil needs almost no capital to add volume, and Celestica is spending borrowed-free equity to build dedicated assembly capacity for two or three buyers. That is the one of the three where a permitting delay lands on fixed cost rather than on someone else's balance sheet.

Celestica's Texas lines are being built for racks whose buyers must first get permission to pour concrete. That approval is the only input in the bill of materials no supplier can expedite.

Rambus Is Paid Per Module Shipped, So Record DRAM Prices Left Its Royalties Falling

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A memory shortage that has sent server DRAM contract prices up double digits has not reached the company whose chips make those modules work — except as a cost. Rambus reported record revenue on 26 October and guided its licensing royalties lower for a third straight quarter, to $68–74m from the $84.2m booked in June.

The upside arrives instead through chipsets: product revenue of $114.3m, a record, guided to $127–133m, at a 60.4% product gross margin management put at the low end of its target band because wafer, substrate and assembly costs are rising. Content per module is the lever, with a multiplexed server module carrying roughly four times the silicon of a standard one.

Rambus shares are down 30% in twelve months; Micron nearly tripled. Microchip's data-centre growth is switch silicon, and Everspin's memory-pooling revenue is a 2029 event.

On 26 October Rambus, which sells the interface chips that sit on server memory modules and licenses the patents behind them, posted its best quarter on record and guided its royalty line down for the third time in a row. Product revenue reached $114.3m, up 22% from a year earlier, while royalties slipped to $77.0m from $84.2m in the June quarter and were guided to $68–74m for December.

Those are the quarters in which server memory went short. TrendForce projects conventional DRAM contract prices rising 10–15% quarter on quarter in the fourth quarter, with server supply tight as makers steer advanced capacity toward high-performance parts. A shortage of that size was taken to pay everyone standing near a memory module. Rambus's own disclosure says what the pass-through is: modules shipped and silicon per module, with a higher bill for making that silicon.

Ten data buffers where there used to be one chip

The content step is specified. A standard registered server module carries a single registering clock driver. Rambus's multiplexed version carries one multiplexed registering clock driver plus ten multiplexed data buffers, an SPD hub and two temperature sensors — roughly four times the silicon on the same socket. Platform memory channels are going from eight to between twelve and sixteen. And "de-specing" — cutting density per module so a server needs more of them — lifts unit counts even as revenue per module is squeezed.

The size of that prize is modest. Rambus put the whole multiplexed-module market at $600m and said the ramp will be shallower than for the standard clock driver, with Qualcomm in pre-production and AMD early. Renesas and Shanghai-listed Montage Technology compete for the same clock-driver content; the memory-pooling controller socket is contested by better-capitalized rivals, Astera Labs, whose Leo controllers went into Microsoft Azure's M-series in late 2025, and Marvell, which bought the switch designer XConn for about $540m in February 2026.

The shortage also sends Rambus a bill

Product gross margin was 60.4%, the bottom of a 60–63% target band, on rising wafer, substrate and packaging-and-test costs. Rambus spent roughly $12m pre-building inventory in the quarter out of $777m of cash, with a similar amount planned, because lead times are stretching. "Demand is strong going into 2027. Supply will remain tight," chief executive Luc Seraphin told investors on 26 October. Group margins still expanded: gross margin of 78.3% and an operating margin of 38.3%, both up from a year earlier.

Most of the share damage arrived in one session. Rambus fell 14.6% on 5 November as the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index dropped 12.2% on post-midterm fears of restrictions on data-centre development, then recovered 7.0% the next day. Over twelve months the stock is down 30%, to $74.53, while Micron rose 194%. It trades at 19.2x consensus FY2027 earnings against 30.3x trailing, and 21.8x trailing EBITDA against roughly 25x recorded in May at a higher price.

The two neighbors on the same shelf

Microchip, the embedded-control supplier whose microcontrollers and interface chips go into cars and factories, does sell memory-pooling controllers — the SMC 2000 and SMC 2100 series — with no revenue disclosed for them. Data centre was 16.7% of September-quarter sales, and the disclosed momentum is switches and retimers: 24 cumulative design wins, double June's count. Revenue grew 42.2% with gross margin up about 1,000 basis points to 65.9%, yet the shares fell about 10% on a December guide 1% lower sequentially at the midpoint. "I have been saying it, that data center is in the bubble," chief executive Steve Sanghi said on 5 November. The stock sits at 16.0x FY2027 consensus earnings against 43.7x trailing.

Everspin, which makes magnetoresistive memory for industrial, aerospace and storage customers, grew 46.9% to a record $20.65m, of which $16.9m was product. Its memory-pooling demonstration is a 2029 revenue event by its own account. What the shortage does pay it is substitution: "we are seeing our distributors actually ask for MRAM parts that would actually replace NOR Flash," chief executive Sanjeev Aggarwal said on 28 October, as flash fabs convert to DRAM. Operating margin went from −11.0% to −38.4%, mostly on a $6m litigation provision; the shares trade at 3.2x consensus FY2027 revenue against 4.1x trailing sales, and much of the 73% fall from the high is that margin.

What the quarters account for

Almost none of the 30-day markdown at Rambus or Everspin is in the reported numbers: both printed records, and both bounced when the macro session reversed. Microchip is the exception, and it falls for a disclosed reason — $53m of one-time full-margin items that do not repeat, gross margin guided to 64–65%, and net debt at 2.36x EBITDA with buybacks deferred to deleveraging. Rambus's growth is accelerating into consensus that still models FY2027 revenue above $1bn.

The real fundamental datum against Rambus is the one the company published itself. Royalties are near-100% margin and contractual; chipsets must be fabricated, packaged and inventoried in a year its chief executive says supply stays tight. A shortage that lifts the price of DRAM raises the cost of the silicon that addresses it and pays Rambus only when another module ships.

Three quarters into the biggest memory squeeze in years, the tollbooth line is shrinking and the factory line is the one growing. That is a lower-quality dollar, and the market has been repricing the quality long before it repriced the growth.

Reddit earned more from a smaller American audience last quarter, and the company has stopped waiting to find out whether that trade can run. On 3 November it began testing advertisements inside its own search results, four days after telling investors that Google's AI answers were absorbing the visitors who used to click through.

The 29 October print was read as the quarter in which Reddit's pricing engine took over from its audience growth. Half of that reading survives contact with the numbers. Advertising revenue of $847m grew 54% year on year, gross margin held at 91.5% for a ninth consecutive quarter, and the adjusted EBITDA margin reached 45%. What does not survive is the assumption underneath it: that the audience being monetized stays put.

The audience fell where the dollars are

Global daily active uniques grew 12% to 130.3m, carried entirely by international users, up 24% to 79.6m. Then the guidance arrived in two halves that point different ways. Fourth-quarter revenue is guided to $1.00bn-$1.03bn, up 38% to 42%, with adjusted EBITDA of $480m-$510m. Price is doing the work, and licensing is not

Average revenue per user rose 36% to $6.87 globally, and in the United States 53% to $13.82. The comparison that explains the mechanism

AppLovin, which runs a mobile ad auction and the measurement layer around it across more than 140,000 apps, has no distribution landlord, and its quarter showed it. Revenue accelerated to a record $2.185bn, up 55.5%, at an 84% adjusted EBITDA margin, with the fourth quarter guided up 43% to 44%. The caveats are real: a securities-fraud class action over its AI claims is unresolved, and its reported return on ad spend is platform self-attribution.

So the question Reddit has set itself is whether a community site can sell its own search box faster than someone else's stops sending people to it.

The tax provision that made American cannabis retailing a structurally unprofitable business stopped applying to a large part of it seven months ago. On 23 April the Department of Justice reclassified state-licensed medical cannabis from Schedule I to Schedule III, effective 28 April, placing medical product outside Section 280E, the rule barring sellers of Schedule I drugs from deducting ordinary business expenses. Green Thumb Industries, which grows cannabis and sells it wholesale and through its own RISE stores, reported income tax expense of $28m for the September quarter against $49m a year earlier, and named the reclassification as the cause.

Adult-use product remains Schedule I, and remains taxed that way. That is the half the market traded in the first week of November, when Drug Enforcement Administration Chief Administrative Law Judge Derek Julius stayed the broader rescheduling proceeding to admit a Government Accountability Office report on scheduling gaps. The AdvisorShares Pure US Cannabis ETF, the sector's usual proxy, fell 8.2% from its 3 November close across the next two sessions, then recovered 2.8% on 6 November. Nothing in the quarters reported those same days got worse. The quarters are the point: the industry's operating economics and its tax position have come apart, and only one of them improved.

The medical half now files like a normal company

Trulieve is the cleanest case. The Florida-concentrated grower and dispensary operator moved to the New York Stock Exchange under the symbol TRLV on 10 June, the first US cannabis company to list there, after both big exchanges rewrote their listing rules in April for Schedule III operators. "From this year forward, we are taxed the same as any U.S. Corporation," chief executive Kim Rivers told investors on 5 November. "This parity is important because it improves profitability and cash flow."

The numbers behind that sentence held up. Gross margin reached 64.3% from 58.9% a year earlier, operating cash flow was $74m in the quarter against full-year guidance of at least $225m, and cash stood at $327m. Its scale in Florida is the moat: 657,000 ounces of flower across 170 stores, 55% more flower per store than the state average, in a market management itself calls saturated with 786 dispensaries. On trailing enterprise value to EBITDA of 9.0x it is also the dearest US operator here, against Cresco Labs at 7.4x and Green Thumb at 5.3x.

The bill from the years when the deduction ban applied

Against that improvement sits a liability the April rule did not touch. The Internal Revenue Service says cannabis operators owe $1.6bn and counting in past-due 280E bills, withheld deliberately to conserve cash, and argued in a 6 March filing that hopes of retroactive relief are unfounded because the law in effect at the time must be applied. Trulieve carried $696.4m of net uncertain tax positions at 31 March, of which $655.6m relates to its own 280E challenge and includes $80.2m of accrued interest. Curaleaf's equivalent balance is $531.5m, Verano's $378.3m and Cresco's $171.4m. Trulieve confirmed on 5 November that the balance includes more than $102m of refunds it has already received.

So the contested money is on the balance sheet, not in the cash tax line, and its resolution sits with Treasury. "Treasury and the IRS have made guidance on 280E one of their priorities for fiscal 2027," Green Thumb founder and chief executive Ben Kovler said on 4 November. "How soon that clarity arrives matters for us and for every operator trying to model its tax position."

The price war is in Germany, not Florida

The other half of the bear case, collapsing realized price, belongs to a different continent. Curaleaf, which exports pharmaceutical-grade cannabis to ten European and Pacific markets alongside its US stores, saw prices "fallen from like $3 a gram to $0.90 a gram in 1 quarter, which is severe," chief executive Boris Jordan said on 3 November, calling October "probably the worst month we've ever seen since the start of the German market." He was explicit about where it hurts: "Our U.S. business is strong... It's really German related." Tilray, the Canadian cannabis-and-beverage group, booked $8.8m of adjusted-EBITDA headwind from the same international compression on a record $257.1m quarter.

The American till moved the other way. Green Thumb's 108 comparable stores posted a 3% same-store gain, its first positive comp since the third quarter of 2024, with "material improvement" in Florida operating economics; reported gross margin of 45.2% against 49.4% was driven primarily by $15.8m of brand licensing fees, and normalized EBITDA was flat at $89m on a 27.6% margin. Industry retail gross margins fell from 52.6% in 2021 to 42.7% in 2025, which puts Green Thumb above the average and Trulieve far above it.

The landlord is diversifying out of the question

Innovative Industrial Properties, the real estate investment trust that owns and leases cultivation buildings to licensed operators, is the one name on this shelf that rose over the past month, up 8.2%. Its third-quarter adjusted funds from operations were $1.96 a share against a $1.90 dividend, but $0.15 of that came from non-recurring past-due rent recoveries, leaving roughly $1.81 of recurring coverage. More than 800,000 sq ft sits vacant, two-thirds of it Parallel's Florida assets, and 4Front Ventures accounts for 7.1% of annualized rent. Its answer is to stop being a cannabis landlord at the margin: about $545m committed to life-science investments with IQHQ, including a $245m mezzanine loan on a Cambridge campus that executive chairman Alan Gold said on 3 November "is expected to generate an average interest rate of ~15.8% over the term of the loan," and roughly 26% of annualized income when fully funded. A 10-year Treasury yield of 4.88%, down 0.41 points over the month, did the rest.

What the quarters account for, and what they do not

Very little of the past month's dispersion follows the tax line. Green Thumb fell 1.8% over 30 days and Curaleaf 4.5%, while Tilray fell 26.3% on international pricing and Cronos Group, the Toronto exporter whose revenue rose 74.6% to $63.5m on Israeli and international volume, gained 9.6%. Cronos trades at 15.9x trailing EV/EBITDA, three times Green Thumb's, so whatever superior cash conversion a non-280E base confers is already paid for.

What the shipped numbers explain is the operating story: margins at the medical operators are flat to better, Florida is improving, and the deflation is German. What they cannot explain is the valuation, and the reason is that the biggest single variable is a tax ruling nobody has. Kovler put the gap in his own terms on 4 November: "If a medical business traded at 15x EBITDA on the New York Stock Exchange, we would be thinking about this very differently." He is looking at 4x to 5x instead, and has bought back about 29.5m shares, roughly 13% of the company, at an average of about $7 since the fourth quarter of 2023.

There is one dated demand event ahead that owes nothing to the Drug Enforcement Administration. Intoxicating hemp products become illegal on 11 December, and Green Thumb's chief executive says the category represents more than a tenth of liquor store sales, with retailers loading the pantry before the ban. Whatever share of those buyers walks into a dispensary instead will arrive in a quarter nobody has priced, at operators who are now, for the medical part of what they sell, ordinary taxpayers.

Applied Optoelectronics reported its sixth consecutive record quarter on 5 November and still earned less on each dollar it shipped than a year earlier, and the reason is printed in the speed-grade split. Revenue of $269.9m was up 127.5% from a year ago, with $6.3m of net income, the first GAAP profit after operating losses in every year on record.

The 800-gigabit-to-1.6-terabit transition has been read all year as a rising tide for anyone holding a socket in an optical module. The quarter says the water level is uneven: the margin accrues to the grade you are actually on and to the silicon inside the package, while the assembler's toll is fixed at whatever it was.

The grade decides the margin

Applied Optoelectronics' incremental data-center volume arrived disproportionately in the older grade. Revenue from 400G nearly doubled sequentially to $93.4m, 60.2% of data-center revenue, while 800G nearly quadrupled to only $47.5m. Non-GAAP gross margin came in at 28.7%, under the 29.0–30.5% it had guided and under 31.0% a year earlier; management's stated objective is to work back toward roughly 40% as mix moves to 1.6T and in-house lasers. It is also not yet a pure AI-optics business: 41% of the quarter was cable-TV hardware, roughly $110m, against 58% from data-center products.

The 1.6T award it has advertised is real and partial. A three-year agreement signed after 30 September obliges a major hyperscale customer, with conditions, to buy 10% of certain 1.6Tb transceiver products from June 2026 to May 2029, worth a potential $1.0–1.5bn. That is a second-source allocation of a defined share. Diluted shares have meanwhile gone from 42.3m in the third quarter of 2024 to 81.6m by mid-2026, so per-share progress has had to outrun issuance.

MaxLinear is paid twice for the same socket

The same transition reprices the chip. MaxLinear's Infrastructure segment reached about $125m last quarter, up 210% and more than half of total revenue, on Keystone, a 5-nanometer 100-gigabit-per-lane signal processor in volume production for 800G builds; its 200-gigabit-per-lane successor is in qualification for 1.6T with first revenue early in 2027. "The ASP on the DSP is more or less doubling, and then we have TIA that we did not add before," chief executive Kishore Seendripu told investors on 22 October, referring to the transimpedance amplifier MaxLinear now attaches at an initial 80% rate. Company revenue of $218.1m grew 72.4% at 58.7% gross margin, with $30.2m of operating income after annual operating losses in 2023, 2024 and 2025. Management puts its optical share at 5–7% today.

Fabrinet's twelve cents are indifferent to speed

Fabrinet, which assembles transceivers, tunable lasers and amplifiers to its customers' designs, is paid for labor and process. Its September quarter revenue of $1.474bn grew 50.7%, the seventh consecutive quarter of accelerating growth, at a gross margin of 11.8% — within a hair of the 12.1% it averaged in fiscal 2025 and 2026. Data-center revenue of $777m, up 78%, is now 53% of the total, and the December guide of $1.60–1.65bn implies about 43% growth. Operating income grew 55%, faster than revenue, because fixed costs are only around 5% of total cost. Its fiscal-2026 annual report lists four customers above 10%: Cisco at 19.9%, NVIDIA 16.3%, Nokia 10.7% and Amazon 10.5%. Capacity goes from roughly $5.8bn of revenue to about $10bn in January 2027 and $16bn with two further buildings; first-quarter capital spending of $159m put free cash flow at minus $137m.

Viavi sells the test; ADTRAN buys the laser

Viavi, which sells network test and assurance instruments, grew revenue 56.9% to $469.3m at 61.2% gross margin against 56.0%, and its network software and convergence segment earned a 25.5% operating margin against 8.2%. "During this quarter, we expect to reach $2 billion revenue run rate, which is ahead of our prior expectations," chief executive Oleg Khaykin said on 4 November. ADTRAN sits on the other side of the invoice: revenue of $281.2m grew 0.6% and came in below consensus, with hyperscaler sales up more than 200% to roughly a tenth of the business. Of the margin pressure, chief executive Tom Stanton said on 3 November: "Generally speaking, I mean, it's 80%-90% supply cost." The inflating inputs are optics, lasers and semiconductors.

Only one margin line was self-inflicted

From 6 October to 5 November, Applied Optoelectronics fell 64.0%, MaxLinear 59.5%, Viavi 41.8% and Fabrinet 40.1%, against 24.1% for NVIDIA. Fabrinet has been in a downtrend since early August, a stretch that brackets two record quarters, and now trades near 14.9x fiscal-2027 consensus earnings against 58.5x trailing in May. Applied Optoelectronics is installing equipment for more than a million 800G and 1.6T units a month by the end of 2027. Whether the halls to plug them into get approved is now a separate question from whether the parts get built.

GE Vernova collected $5.3bn of customer deposits in the September quarter, money wired years before a gas turbine ships, and that single line is why it is the only one of the five companies sold as nuclear reactor builders whose markdown can be argued against an income statement. The deposits and slot reservations were $5.3bn of the quarter's $5.4bn of free cash flow. Its Nuclear Power business, meanwhile, billed $719m of a $12.642bn quarter, under 6% of revenue, and the company discloses no separate nuclear backlog.

So the thing repriced in GE Vernova after the 3 November midterm vote was the gas and grid book, where the revenue and the cash sit, on a political read that state ballot measures and gubernatorial shifts could delay permitting and interconnection for data centers. The belief worth testing across the other four is that a contracted gigawatt of reactor capacity pays its developer something before construction begins. It pays nothing. A firm gigawatt of GE Vernova gas equipment carries roughly $850m of booked revenue; a gigawatt in Oklo's, NuScale's or X-energy's pipeline carries a filing and a schedule.

Twenty percent down before the steel is cut

Gas equipment backlog plus slot reservations reached 122 GW from 116 GW in the quarter, as 13 GW of reservations converted to firm orders and 5 GW shipped: firm backlog rose from 53 GW to 64 GW, reservations fell to 58 GW. Set that against $54.4bn of Power equipment backlog and a firm gigawatt works out at about $850m of equipment revenue, a figure derived from the two disclosures. Services follow it. HA is the company's advanced-class turbine line, and "we expect every gigawatt of HAs to generate $0.5 billion of high-margin services revenue over the first 20 years of its lifetime," chief executive Scott Strazik told investors on 28 October. A reservation earns the label slot reservation agreement only once the customer has put down roughly a fifth of the price in cash, which is both the cash-timing mechanism and the screen against speculative bookings.

That is why the cash guide moved hardest: free-cash-flow guidance went to $16–17bn from $11.5–12.5bn, a raise that tracks the down-payment inflow. Orders rose 96% to $29.3bn and backlog reached about $197bn, split $104bn equipment and $93bn services. Revenue growth has accelerated for three consecutive quarters, to 26.8%, with gross margin 554 basis points wider year on year.

The shares fell through all of it. At $781.24 GE Vernova is 33.5% below its 52-week high, and price-to-sales is 4.69 against 7.47 in May; on the FY2027 consensus estimate of $25.75 a share the multiple is about 30x, against roughly 37x forward in May. The trailing 20.3x is flattered by the gain on Prolec GE and overstates the earnings base. Among the power-equipment makers the markdown was mild: down 21.9% over three months while Powell fell 39.7% and Vertiv 33.3%, and in the week to 3 November GE Vernova lost 11% as Vertiv lost 14%.

What $300m a gigawatt buys without a permit

X-energy, which licenses the Xe-100 reactor and makes TRISO fuel, booked $54.6m of revenue in the June quarter at a gross margin of minus 59.9%, because the revenue is Department of Energy cost-share development work. It holds $1,145.4m of cash and describes $1.9bn of liquidity with no debt, and spent $63.3m of capital in the quarter, mostly on the TX-1 fuel plant at Oak Ridge, about 80% complete and on schedule. Its pipeline is 144 reactors, roughly 11.5 GWe, assuming every customer exercises its contingent rights in full, with Amazon and affiliated projects more than 70% of contracted backlog. On 406.4m shares outstanding the market value is near $4.0bn and on the lower count implied by its May record about $2.7bn, so $233m to $347m per pipeline gigawatt, derived. "We are focused on that $2.3 trillion TAM that we can access with the Xe-100 and our TRISO fuel business," chief executive J. Clay Sell said on the second-quarter call.

Oklo, which builds the 15–75 MWe Aurora powerhouse, billed $1.861m of radioisotopes against an operating loss of $82.4m, and ended September with $3.13bn of liquidity after issuing 32.6m shares for $2.25bn; diluted shares are up 25% in a year. Its 14 GW pipeline is a 12 GW master power agreement with Switch to 2044, up to 1.2 GW at a Meta campus still in interconnection and a 500 MW Equinix letter of intent carrying a $25m prepayment — about $302m a gigawatt against $4.23bn of market value, and 1.21 times book.

NuScale billed $281,000 as RoPower engineering work wound down, down 96.6% year on year, and now carries $1.9bn of liquidity against roughly $1.47bn of Class A market value, 1.02 times book, with quarterly operating expenses near $65m. Its 6 GW framework with the Tennessee Valley Authority and commercialisation partner ENTRA1 is a memorandum: about $244m a framework gigawatt, derived, and the power purchase agreement behind it is unsigned. "We are no longer simply designing and planning, we are manufacturing," chief executive John Hopkins said on 5 November, citing the Doosan Enerbility first-article module agreement.

NANO Nuclear has no gigawatts to divide into. It reported $214,042 of revenue in the June quarter, the first in its history, against a $15.83m operating loss, holds about $580m of cash and short-term investments against $585m of market value, and trades at 0.93 times book, the only one of the five below its own balance sheet. It has one construction permit application accepted for review, with first power around 2030.

Two markdowns, two causes

Nothing in GE Vernova's results explains its markdown: every line improved and the cash guide rose. What is at risk is conversion, 58 GW of reservations whose customers can still walk, and that risk is political rather than operational. For the four developers the results cannot explain anything, because there is almost nothing to bill; what was erased is the premium over cash, while the schedules held and the Nuclear Regulatory Commission issued the first US construction permit for the BWRX-300 at Clinch River on 28 September. Three designs now clear between $244m and $347m a gigawatt of uncommitted capacity, and on 6 November Oklo rose 19% on a narrower loss, NuScale 15% and NANO Nuclear 9% as the policy fear passed, which is roughly what a balance sheet does for a share price when the story stops working.

X-energy reports its September quarter on 12 November. It will be the first look at what a quarter of fuel-plant construction costs now that the equity is worth little more than the money paying for it, and the first developer disclosure since the autumn took the option premium away.

Centrus Energy, which sells enrichment services to nuclear utilities and builds centrifuges under US government contracts, sold a quarter less enrichment in the first nine months of this year than a year earlier at a 30% higher unit cost, and earned almost exactly the same profit doing it. That gap is the whole question hanging over six nuclear-linked equities that all rolled into a six-month downtrend together. They were sold as if the prices underneath the fuel cycle had cracked. Roughly 85% of uranium moves under long-term bilateral contracts of three to ten years, which is why the term indicator, not spot, is the number that reaches a producer's income statement. Three different things went wrong instead, and only one of them is a cost of production.

Mills collected a price increase and paid less for fiber in the same quarter, and the two facts have one cause.

Fastmarkets RISI recognized a $70-a-ton rise in North American linerboard and $100 a ton in medium in September, then nothing further in October, leaving board prices flat for the month. Over the same stretch the recycled mill's main furnish went the other way. US prices for old corrugated containers were cut by up to $45 a ton in October, a cumulative $88 fall in three months, which Fastmarkets attributed to high mill inventories and market-related downtime at every major US paper and board mill, with mills unable to take outside-contract tons at any price and mixed paper being landfilled.

September's recognition was read as proof that supply discipline had restored pricing power, after roughly 3.9m tons of capacity, about a tenth of the US total, was permanently retired from February 2025 onward. The quarters reported between 22 October and 3 November say the spread genuinely widened. They also say the fiber relief is the bill for the machines that stopped running, and that one producer kept it.

The low-cost mills banked the increase

Packaging Corporation of America, which runs containerboard mills and the box plants that convert their output, earned $262m, or $3.04 a share excluding special items, on net sales of $2.6bn against $2.3bn a year earlier. Operating margin ran 11.5%, 13.4% and 16.2% through 2026's three quarters; Packaging-segment earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization (EBITDA) were $578m on $2.4bn of sales, a 23.9% margin against 23.1%.

"We have realized substantially all of the first net $50 increase and about half of the second $50 increase," Thomas A. Hassfurther, PCA's president, told investors on 22 October. Realization of the $70 increase, he said, "will be split between Q4 and Q1 and skewed more towards Q1 in terms of timing" — a third of the year's announced price is still unbanked.

Volume is where the premium is argued. Total corrugated shipments rose 10.6% per day, but legacy PCA shipments fell 0.5% per workday against an industry decline of 1.6% per workday. The growth is bought: the $1.8bn Greif containerboard business, two mills and roughly 800,000 tons, closed in September 2025, and synergies now run at "between $40 million and $50 million," chief financial officer Kent A. Pflederer said the same day, against an original $30m target. Costs still bit: freight cost $0.26 a share and fiber $0.12, with diesel about $6.32 a gallon on 25 October. Fourth-quarter guidance is $2.81 a share, $0.23 below the third, with three mills down for maintenance. The shares carry 30.5x trailing and 23.4x forward earnings, against 21.8x forward in early October.

Smurfit Westrock's price rose and its guidance fell

Smurfit Westrock, the Dublin-headquartered maker of containerboard, corrugated boxes and consumer packaging with 97,000 employees, reported $8,161m of net sales and $1,205m of adjusted EBITDA at a 14.8% margin, and cut full-year guidance to about $4.7bn. North American price rose about 5% sequentially and operating margin recovered to 5.97% from 3.85%, yet operating income fell 19.6% year on year.

The cause is energy. "Energy costs alone expected to represent approximately $700 million year on year headwind in 2026," chief executive Anthony Smurfit said on 28 October, with Dutch TTF gas up about 214% on the year. On the price itself he was blunter: "The first $100 [per ton] is really just basically recovering the cost." The company has permanently removed over 500,000 tons at St. Paul and Forney and a 127,000-ton machine at La Tuque. It trades at 8.7x trailing EV/EBITDA and 1.31x book, or 13.7x 2027 consensus earnings of $3.26 a share.

The name that moved the shelf makes no containerboard

Graphic Packaging converts its own coated recycled and bleached paperboard into folding cartons, cups and food containers for consumer-goods firms and quick-service restaurants. It rose 34% over the 30 days to 5 November, 21.2% of it on 3 November, supplying most of the three-name average gain — after falling 32.1% over the year.

Its quarter was not a board-price quarter. Volumes rose 1% while industry box shipments fell, adjusted EBITDA margin reached 13.2%, up 1.9 percentage points sequentially for a second quarter, and about $90m of full-year structural cost reductions offset $83m of Q3 inflation in chemicals, resins, secondary fiber and logistics. "Margin recovery is especially encouraging to see, given the heightened inflationary environment experienced in 2026," chief executive Robbert Rietbroek said on 3 November. At 8.97x trailing EV/EBITDA it sits near Smurfit Westrock's 8.7x and well below Packaging Corp's 13.4x, on a 12.1% trailing free-cash-flow yield; 4.7x expected year-end leverage on $5.2bn of net debt is the reason it is priced where it is.

What the three quarters account for

The month's gains have three different engines. PKG's inflection is real and its margin moved in the quarter the price did, so the business explains the move, with the caveat that the volume came from an acquisition and the remaining price lands mostly in 2027. Smurfit Westrock's sequential recovery is genuine while its year is not, and the gap is European gas at roughly ten times the US price, which its own chief executive describes as making some mills unaffordable to run. Graphic Packaging re-rated off cost cuts and pricing in paperboard grades, sharing the recovered-fiber basket but none of the containerboard spread.

The shares moved together on 5 November, PKG down 4.6% and the others less, without discoverable company news; the likelier reading is a sector or positioning move. International Paper sat out the year entirely, down 5.7%, having taken a $1.38bn goodwill impairment against its European packaging business.

The awkward part is what cheap fiber means. It is cheap because mills stopped buying it, and the moment box demand returns the furnish bill returns with it, while the announced increases are still arriving in instalments. The spread that looks widest today is the one being paid for by tons nobody is making.

Five makers of power semiconductors reported quarters between 29 October and 4 November, and four of them billed more than they had guided to, on margins that widened. Monolithic Power Systems, which designs the DC-to-DC conversion chips that step voltage down for processors, passed $1bn of quarterly revenue for the first time. By the close on 5 November every one of the five had been marked down by a tenth or more in a single session.

The shelf had been bought as the parts list for Nvidia's 800-volt direct-current rack, which converts utility alternating current straight to 800 volts and removes the 54-volt distribution inside the rack. The quarters just filed say something narrower. The AI money these companies collected came from parts already in production; the 800-volt stages are dated by the people selling them to late 2027 and 2028.

The 800-volt money is dated by its own makers

Monolithic Power's September quarter was $1.202bn, up 63.1% year on year, the fourth straight quarter of accelerating growth. Enterprise Data revenue was $506.7m, 42.1% of the total and up 164.6%. Gross margin was 55.35%, inside half a point of where it sat eight quarters ago, and December quarter guidance of about $1.35bn landed roughly 10% above the consensus analysts carried. "I see this explosive growth period in the next few years," chief executive Michael Hsing said on 29 October. "And we don't see anything -- any signs of the slowdown."

None of that is 800-volt revenue. The company's 800-volt gallium-nitride parts are only now sampling, with revenue expected late 2027 and material ramp in 2028; it intends to ship some 50m units before confirming a sub-one-part-per-million failure rate. Its first 48-volt automotive orders are a 2027 growth driver by management's own description. Against that, the shares trade at 26.7 times trailing gross profit, a multiple that was 49.8x in May.

Navitas Semiconductor sits at the other end of scale: $14.0m of revenue, up 38.4% and its first year-on-year growth since 2024, with gross margin back to 39.3% from minus 8.9%. The operating loss was $34.4m on that revenue and diluted shares rose 22.9% over the year. "Power is now the #1 constraint on AI compute," chief executive Chris Allexandre said on 3 November. The constraint is real; the billing is not yet. December quarter guidance is $16.0m against a breakeven level management frames in the low $60m a quarter, the anchor customer of its $126m Claros acquisition does not ramp until late 2028 to 2029, and the company's own date for in-rack native 800-volt systems has already slipped to early 2028. At 41.9 times forward sales, against onsemi's 3.7x, the de-rating here is a valuation fact.

What the fabs actually did

onsemi, which makes power switching and sensing devices for cars, industrial power and chargers, grew 11.1% to $1.7223bn and expanded non-GAAP gross margin 190 basis points to 41.2%, a fifth consecutive quarter of expansion. AI data-center revenue reached $180m, up 175%. Fab utilization held at 83%, up from 77% in the March quarter, and distributor inventory fell to 9.9 weeks. Automotive at $777m was flat and guided lower. "What you're seeing today is short term in nature and it's supply-driven, not strategically driven," chief executive Hassane El-Khoury said on 2 November. "What we are holding back is their replenishment." The stock is 19.2 times forward earnings against 35.8x trailing, and 10.0 times trailing gross profit against 12.2x in August.

Wolfspeed is the exception the numbers do explain. Revenue of $155.0m fell 21.2%, the fourth straight year-on-year decline, on a minus 21.9% gross margin and a $143.8m net loss. Breakeven needs roughly $200m a quarter. It has a conditional $1.5bn thirty-year Department of War loan commitment, but chief financial officer Gregor van Issum noted on 4 November that drawing the tranches carries "several financial commitments, limited equity raise, and some other de-risking measures." At 1.68 times forward sales there is no earnings or gross-profit multiple to quote.

A passive maker with no power stage fell hardest

On 5 November the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 12.3%, its worst day since 16 March 2020, after a Democratic midterm sweep brought calls from party leaders for data-center restrictions. Monolithic Power fell 10.3%, onsemi 9.9%, Navitas 15.8%, Wolfspeed 10.7% — between a third and a half of each name's entire 30-day decline, inside one session.

Vishay Intertechnology fell 14.8%, harder than onsemi, Monolithic Power or Wolfspeed. It makes discrete semiconductors and passive components — resistors, capacitors, diodes — and owns no 800-volt power stage. It printed record revenue of $978.9m, up 23.8%, on a 1.29 book-to-bill and a $2.2bn backlog equal to 6.6 months, with selling prices up 4% year on year. "AI revenue, last year we were around $100 million," chief executive Joel Smejkal said on 4 November. "This year we keep pushing to be near $200 million is what our push is" — about 5% of the base, at 6.4 times trailing EV/EBITDA.

What the results account for

For four of the five the shipped numbers moved the wrong way for the price: revenue accelerated, margins widened, inventory in the channel thinned, and order books lengthened straight into the decline. The markdown sorted the group by how far each had run rather than by its exposure to the 800-volt socket, which is why the cheapest name on cash earnings fell about as hard as the most expensive one on sales. Only two of the five carry a de-rating their own disclosures support, and for different reasons: Wolfspeed because the revenue is shrinking against a fixed cost base, Navitas because 41.9 times forward sales prices a design-in that its own calendar puts years out.

The prize is not small — power equipment per rack rises from roughly $36,000 to more than $398,000 as racks scale toward 600 kilowatts. What the quarters establish is who is being paid for it now, and the answer is a short list that does not yet include the 800-volt parts themselves.

The next readings land before the voltage does. Monolithic Power's December quarter tests a $1.35bn guide; onsemi's tests a gross margin guided to 41.5-43.5% on $40-50m of capital spending. Both will print long before a single 800-volt gallium-nitride device bills a dollar.
