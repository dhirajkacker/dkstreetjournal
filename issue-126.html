Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A European cable group holding edge data centers and fiber vehicles looks like a landlord to the AI buildout. Liberty Global has been the seller.

It exited EdgeConneX for $726m of proceeds against $177m invested, its AtlasEdge joint venture sold nine data centers and handed Liberty Global a $57.7m share of losses over nine months, and VodafoneZiggo's mobile towers are going for €669m. The one-month advance to $10.36 rests on structure rather than trading: a Ziggo Group spin-off targeted for the second quarter of 2027, £800m of cost cuts at the UK joint venture, and nearly 7% of the shares retired in the second half. Cash plus the $2.9bn venture mark still exceeds the market value.

On 3 November Liberty Global, which sells broadband, television and mobile service in Belgium, Ireland and the Netherlands and owns half of Britain's Virgin Media O2, reported a quarter in which adjusted earnings fell at every operating unit it consolidates or part-owns. Within two sessions the shares rose about 17%, and over the month to 27 November they gained 36.9% to $10.36.

The premise worth testing is the one the asset list invites: a European cable group that holds an edge data-center platform, two off-balance-sheet fiber vehicles and a venture book should be a landlord to Europe's artificial-intelligence buildout. The 2026 record says it has been the seller.

The data centers went out, not in

Liberty Global sold roughly 16% of its EdgeConneX interest in the first quarter for $73.6m and the remainder on 29 June for $604.2m, per its second-quarter filing — $726m of proceeds on $177m invested, about four times money. That exit took the Liberty Growth portfolio's fair value from $3.4bn to $2.9bn.

What remains in digital infrastructure is smaller and loss-making. AtlasEdge, in which Liberty Global holds a 48.8% economic interest, was carried at $365.2m at 30 September against $388.4m at the end of 2025, and contributed a $57.7m share of losses over nine months. In May the venture sold nine data centers to Templus — Madrid, Barcelona, Milan, Zurich, Paris, Amsterdam, London, Leeds and Copenhagen — to concentrate on larger sites, and the €253m funding a 30MW Lisbon campus sits at the joint venture, not the parent. The February Google Cloud agreement commits the parties only to "explore opportunities" to unlock AtlasEdge capacity, with no capacity or revenue disclosed. VodafoneZiggo's towers, meanwhile, are being sold for €669m to cut that unit's debt.

Cheap coax instead of new fiber

The same instinct governs the network. "DOCSIS 4.0 costs 10 times less than building fiber," chief executive Mike Fries told investors on 3 November, describing an at-scale Dutch upgrade reaching 8 million homes. Virgin Media O2 went further on the same day, announcing £800m of annual cost cuts by 2029 — £400m in 2027, roughly 30% of people costs over two years — and a halt to fiber upgrade investment beyond existing partner commitments in favor of a 2Gbps cable upgrade. The reason is that the British retail revenue pool has shrunk by £5bn, or 13%, in five years.

Trading reflects it. Virgin Media O2's third-quarter revenue fell 5.7% to £2.43bn and adjusted earnings 2.6% to £995m, with full-year guidance of service revenue and earnings each down 3-5%. Telenet's adjusted earnings were €157.1m against €169.6m; Virgin Media Ireland's fell 7.1%; VodafoneZiggo's fell 6.9% even as it added about 12,000 broadband subscribers in its best quarter since 2019. Consolidated revenue of $1,920.6m was up 59.1%, but that is the arithmetic of consolidating VodafoneZiggo after paying Vodafone $1.2bn for the other half, and the group still ran a -0.9% operating margin.

What the market paid for

Earnings multiples do not function here: trailing price-to-earnings is -1.5x and consensus earnings per share are negative every year through 2029. Price-to-book of 0.36x is the anchor, and the bridge beneath it is stark. Against a $3.51bn market value sit about $2.0bn of corporate cash, guided to $1.8bn at year-end, and a $2.9bn external mark on 75 venture investments. At $10.36 the equity trades roughly 28% below those two items alone, ascribing nothing to Telenet, Virgin Media Ireland, VodafoneZiggo or the $3.44bn-carried Virgin Media O2 stake; at about $8 a share, Fries put the gap at "near 50%".

So the quarter's results explain none of the advance, and consensus forward price-to-sales of 0.66x against 0.63x trailing says the sell side expects the revenue base to shrink. What changed was structure: December filings and a second-quarter 2027 target for the Ziggo Group spin-off, which Fries said "is happening"; 2.9% of the company retired in the third quarter with another $100m authorized; and a 110,000-share open-market purchase by Fries at an average $9.3652 on 4 November. Charter rose 46% over the same month, so part of the move was cable's, not Liberty Global's.

The discount is also shrinking from both ends. The Virgin Media O2 carrying value fell to $3,442.9m from $3,756.7m in nine months on a $442.3m share of losses — the assets the market pays nothing for are themselves getting smaller, and management's own bull case for the post-spin pieces is about $21 a share, built on €500m of free cash flow capitalized at a 12% yield.

One regulator now sits astride the cash. Britain's Competition and Markets Authority provisionally found that nexfibre's £2bn purchase of Netomnia may substantially lessen wholesale broadband competition, the owners declined to offer remedies on 20 October, and a statutory decision is due 15 December. Until it lands, Virgin Media O2's roughly £200m distribution to its parents stays under review.

Cameco reached a new 52-week low on 25 November, trading as low as $57.01 against a prior close of $59.79. At the 27 November close of $63.97 the whole company is worth $27.86bn, and almost all of that number is accounted for by something Cameco does not operate: its 49% of Westinghouse Electric, which submitted a confidential draft registration statement to the Securities and Exchange Commission on 31 July for a proposed US listing and was reported in September to be targeting a valuation above $50bn. Mark the stake at that target and subtract, and the market is carrying the uranium mines, the refining and conversion plants and the CANDU fuel business for roughly $3.4bn.

The equity was bought over the past two years as the clean way to own nuclear's data-center demand. The arithmetic says something narrower: the operating business has been de-rated against a deteriorating P&L, while the asset holding the reactor new-build optionality sits inside an affiliate whose own earnings contribution just went negative and whose growth outlook has been pulled from guidance.

The mine earned less as the term price hit a record

September-quarter revenue fell 1.9% to $602.9m, but gross margin collapsed from 37.3% to 16.1% and operating income fell 73.0% to $25.1m. The cause was cost-side: a planned maintenance outage at the Key Lake mill, which stripped fixed-cost absorption, plus reagent and input inflation. The average reported long-term uranium price rose to US$96.50 a pound in the quarter while Cameco's own deliveries worked out near US$65.78, because those pounds were committed years earlier; in Canadian dollars, the measure Cameco reports, the average realized price still rose 8% on the market-priced contracts in the book.

The book itself is the constraint. Commitments average about 28m pounds a year through 2030, above that average in 2026-2028 and below it in 2029-2030. Cameco is running some 30% under full licensed capacity, with Rabbit Lake and the US mines in care and maintenance, and it held the annual dividend at CAD 0.24 despite zero net debt.

The part no one has priced

Westinghouse is where the reactor cycle lands. Under the October 2025 partnership with the US Government and Brookfield, at least $80bn of new Westinghouse-technology reactors are to be built in the US, with the Government arranging financing and permitting in exchange for 20% of any Westinghouse cash distributions above US$17.5bn. Cameco and Brookfield bought the company at roughly $8bn of enterprise value in 2023.

It is also the line that broke this year. Cameco's share of Westinghouse adjusted earnings swung from $126m to a $10m loss, and the five-year growth outlook for that business came out of guidance.

What the results justify and what they do not

On its own numbers the stock is not cheap: 99.6x trailing earnings, 56.4x the 2026 consensus, 45.7x trailing enterprise value to EBITDA, with consensus 2026 EBITDA down 13.7%. The forward case rests on a step change — consensus has 2027 EBITDA at $1.55bn, up 60.9% — and the multiple falls to 28.3x only if 2027 earnings roughly double. A third of the six-month decline is the power complex rather than Cameco: over the same window NRG fell 30.0%, Vistra 23.4% and Constellation 17.3%.

"Capital can easily rotate out of a theme in a day or in a week," chief executive Tim Gitzel told investors on 30 October. "It takes much longer to replace a depleted uranium reserve, to build, license, and commission a fuel cycle facility, to establish a Western nuclear supply chain, or to bring a new reactor into service."

The residual value for the mines is a subtraction, not a disclosure, and it moves every day the share price does. A completed Westinghouse listing would replace the estimate with a printed price, and whichever side of $50bn that price lands on, it settles what the other half of Cameco is actually being paid for.

Chilean Inflation Sets Santander Chile's Margin, and the Bank Expects Less Next Year

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Chile's central bank has not moved the policy rate since July, yet Santander Chile's quarterly profit swung from a 40% year-on-year gain in the second quarter to a 4.2% decline in the third. The variable doing the work is the inflation adjustment on a disclosed net gap of CLP 7.9tn in indexed instruments, which the bank enlarged in the second quarter to capture the inflation rebound and then cut.

That makes the margin a function of Chilean consumer prices, and management has told investors to expect less of them: 2027 guidance assumes 3.1% indexed inflation, with return on equity of 22-24% against more than 24% this year. Santander Chile's third-quarter profit fell by well over a third from the second quarter, and Chile's central bank never touched the policy rate. The lender, a Santiago retail, middle-market and corporate bank majority-owned by Spain's Santander, earned CLP 237.1bn in the quarter against CLP 382.6bn in the one before, and the drop was 4.2% measured against a year earlier.

The quarter was read in New York as deterioration in a bank whose returns had been among the best in Latin America. It was something narrower. The policy rate has sat at 4.50% since well before the unanimous October hold, the fifth-plus consecutive meeting without a change. What moved was the inflation adjustment: the variation in Chile's unidad de fomento, the inflation-indexed accounting unit that reprices a large slice of Chilean bank assets, dropped from 2.46% in the second quarter to 0.58% in the third, and net interest and readjustment income fell 18.1% sequentially with it.

The CLP 7.9tn line that moves the quarter

The size of the lever is disclosed. Santander Chile reported a net gap in inflation-indexed instruments of CLP 7,906,527m at the end of September, against an interest-earning asset yield of 7.6%, cost of funds of 3.8% and a year-to-date net interest margin of 4.1%. The bank does not hold that position passively: it widened the gap during the second-quarter inflation rebound to capture the higher print, then reduced it in the third.

Underneath the inflation trade sits the part of the franchise that does not depend on a price index. Year-to-date efficiency was 33%, an improvement of 290 basis points on a year earlier, and deposits grew 9% year to date against loan growth of 3.9%. "I think the first position in the Chilean industry is still safely on our side," Cristian Vicuña, head of strategy and investor relations, told investors on November 4.

Management's own 2027 assumptions are where the mechanism turns. The bank guided to 3.1% indexed inflation, a flat 4.5% policy rate, net interest margin of about 4.0% and return on equity of 22-24%, below the more than 24% it expects for 2026. Consensus still models 2027 earnings growth of 4.3%. The macro backdrop it guides into is thin: "Mining fell to its lowest level since 1996 in August due to lower ore grades, maintenance, and weather," chief economist Andrés Sansone said on the same call, with mining output now expected to contract 6% to 8% this year.

Banco de Chile holds the same lever and a worse consumer book

Banco de Chile, the 1893-founded commercial bank that is the country's other large listed lender, puts a number on it directly: 100 basis points of inflation is worth CLP 91.1bn of net financial income, while 100 basis points of interest rates moves margin by only about 30 basis points, and only after roughly three years of full repricing. Its readjustment income contracted 64.6% in the third quarter; third-quarter net income was 1.8% below a year earlier on revenue up 8.8%. It guides 2026 inflation at 4.7% falling to 3% in 2027, an effect it sizes at roughly 20 basis points of margin.

It also has the weaker credit trend of the two, with credit losses up 21.2% year on year on retail delinquency, even though its 0.96% third-quarter cost of risk remains well inside Santander Chile's 1.4% year to date. Yet Banco de Chile carries the higher valuation on the lower return: 3.37x trailing book against Santander Chile's 3.16x, with full-year return on average capital and reserves guided to 22%. Both sit above Itaú Unibanco at 2.25x and Credicorp at 2.51x.

Twelve months in pesos, twelve months in dollars

In Santiago, nothing has broken. Santander Chile's local shares rose 19.1% over twelve months and Banco de Chile's 10.8%. The American depositary receipts returned 7.1% and 0.3% over the same dates, a gap of roughly ten percentage points that is the peso, which closed at 1,038 per dollar on November 27 after copper fell 21.5% from its September peak. The dollar proxy for the whole market, the iShares MSCI Chile ETF, fell 8.7%.

So the flat dollar year is currency, and the earnings record contradicts it: Santander Chile's year-to-date return on equity was 24.5%. The three-month slide in both receipts, however, has a genuine companion in the accounts, because the third quarter is the first in which the indexed adjustment worked against the margin rather than for it. What has not happened is any de-rating in pesos. Investors are paying the regional set's richest book multiples for returns that both managements expect to be lower next year, for a reason neither bank controls.

Chile's central bank projects headline inflation back near 3% by the second quarter of 2027, which is target achieved for the country and a shrinking revenue line for its two largest listed lenders. Every quarter from here, the number that decides how much these banks earn will be published by the statistics office before either of them reports.

Millicom International Cellular, which sells mobile service, fixed broadband and mobile money under the Tigo brand in ten Latin American markets, halved its third-quarter profit by settling with four men who used to run its Guatemalan business. Profit attributable to shareholders came in at $98m against $195m a year earlier, and earnings of $0.58 a share missed what analysts expected by more than 40%. Nearly every operating line in the same quarter set a record.

The stock has been held as a Central American dollar annuity carrying a risky Colombian acquisition. The filings describe the reverse arrangement. Colombia, bought from Telefónica this year, is now the largest market in the group at $908m of quarterly service revenue, roughly 2.3 times Guatemala's $392m, and only three of the ten markets — Panama, El Salvador and Ecuador — use the dollar at all. Guatemala transacts in quetzales, Honduras in lempiras.

What the $99m bought

The charge sits in other non-operating expenses: $99m for settlement agreements with four former executives of Tigo Guatemala, of which $31m was paid in the quarter and $68m remains payable over seven years. It resolved all pending material litigation and claims involving those parties, which management called the removal of a significant source of financial and regulatory uncertainty. The concentrated country risk in this business is fiscal rather than currency: at the end of September the estimated tax risk exposure of Millicom's subsidiaries stood at $378m, against $60m of provisions recorded.

The Colombian book earned its best margin while being absorbed

Colombian service revenue grew 7.5% organically and adjusted EBITDA of $417m landed at a 44.2% margin, the highest Millicom has recorded there, while the quarter still carried $20m of integration severance. The Tigo brand consolidation in Colombia took effect on 14 October.

"Following the acquisition of Coltel, our teams moved quickly to integrate operations, simplify our business, improve efficiencies and implement our commercial strategy," chief financial officer Bart Vanhaeren told investors on 5 November, describing the result as "bringing up the acquired lower-margin business from Coltel to our highest EBITDA margin in Colombia ever despite still incurring integration costs." Net leverage fell to 2.53x including Colombia, Ecuador and Uruguay, inside the sub-2.5x year-end path, and the $3.00 annual dividend stayed in four equal installments.

Growth comes from re-pricing the prepaid base

What one Tigo customer pays rose about a fifth: group mobile monthly revenue per user reached $7.4 from $6.2. The engine is migration, not new SIM cards. Postpaid net additions of 390,000 took that base to 17.2m, and 73% of postpaid gross sales came from existing prepaid users, a conversion rate of 87% in Guatemala.

Two lines went the other way. Millicom lost 86,000 home customers sequentially, attributed about one-third to the Colombian earthquake and two-thirds to deliberate pricing, and business-to-business revenue of $404m grew only 2.1% organically as large government projects in Medellín and Panama rolled off, with further roll-off flagged for the fourth quarter.

The decline belongs to Millicom alone

The shares have fallen 14.9% over three months and 23.8% from a closing high of $104.31 on 6 August, with the downtrend dating from 5 November, the day results landed. One session, 12 November, took 9.6% on 4.8m shares, roughly six times the prior month's daily average, a week after results and two days after a UBS upgrade. Over the same three months Liberty Latin America rose 14.0%, TIM Brasil 16.5% and América Móvil 6.9%.

So the operating record accounts for almost none of the fall, and the settlement accounts for the earnings line without touching cash generation: equity free cash flow rose 27% to $316m, with roughly $870m banked against about $1.1bn guided for the year. What the price may instead be discounting is share. In the same quarter América Móvil's Claro grew Central American service revenue 11.4% and the region's EBITDA 16.1%, and Colombian postpaid service revenue 12%, ahead of Tigo's 6.5% in Guatemala and 7.5% in Colombia. Nor is Millicom the discounted asset of the group: at 6.87x trailing enterprise value to EBITDA it is dearer than América Móvil at 5.36x, and the case for the fall being an error rests on the one-off charges not recurring — against next year's consensus earnings of $6.80 a share, the stock stands near 11.7x by our arithmetic, versus 21.1x on this year's charge-depressed base.

The largest holder is not waiting for that argument to settle. Klabin and Suzano both grow eucalyptus in Brazil, and both told investors this month what a tonne of it now costs them. The answers moved in opposite directions. Klabin's total cash cost per tonne sold, including scheduled maintenance downtime, rose 12% year on year to R$3,483, of which the company attributed about R$111 to extraordinary weather and geopolitical pressure. Suzano's pulp production cash cost ex-downtime fell 2% sequentially to R$825. The two numbers measure different things — one an integrated pulp, paper and conversion book, the other a pulp mill — so the level does not compare. The slope does.

Until these results the market treated both as hard-currency assets grown in Brazil. On the 26 October session when the Ibovespa fell 13.88%, Suzano's American depositary shares rose 2.1% and Klabin rose 3.2%, both on roughly four times normal volume. What the quarters showed is that only one of them is being paid for fibre on improving terms, and that Klabin's insulation from the pulp cycle works in both directions.

Rain in the plantations, not weakness in the boxes

Klabin's revenue rose 3% to R$5.57bn, yet adjusted EBITDA fell 12% to R$1.856bn and the margin dropped to 33% from 39%. The cause was physical: rainfall tied to El Niño disrupted forestry operations and forced higher wood inventories and purchases of standing timber, with chief executive Cristiano Teixeira saying the extra costs should mostly phase out by the second quarter of 2027.

Where the revenue came from matters more. Klabin's pulp line grew 6% to R$1.51bn on an 18% rise in dollar short-fibre prices, while corrugated-box revenue of R$1.7bn was flat because implemented price increases exactly offset a 2% volume decline to 245 thousand tonnes. More than 70% of the packaging area is contracted with brands including JBS, Nestlé, Unilever and Natura, and Brazilian corrugated shipments are forecast up only 1.6% to 2.0% this year. A contracted book in a 2% market defends price and delivers no growth.

It also carries R$24bn of net debt at 3.3x adjusted EBITDA, financed into a policy rate the central bank held at 13.75% on 4 November after five consecutive cuts. Below-the-line financing costs consumed most of the quarter's operating profit, and management says the end of the investment cycle should support deleveraging from 2027. On 6 August, a quarter before the cost miss, Teixeira told investors: "for the next quarters, you're going to see a significant free cash flow generation in the company." The trailing free-cash-flow yield is 2.5%, and the shares carry 6.09x trailing EV/EBITDA and 2.15x book.

Suzano announced a price and collected it

Suzano's pulp segment earned R$4.8bn of EBITDA at a 50% margin on 3.19m tonnes at $591 a tonne. At the post-election rate near R$5.60 to the dollar that realization is roughly R$3,310 a tonne against R$825 of production cash cost. Two $20-a-tonne Asian increases landed, the second effective 11 November, after consultancies counted more than 900,000 tonnes of bleached chemical pulp pulled from the market mid-quarter.

"We confirm that during October, our price increase announcements were fully implemented with no concessions," pulp vice-president Leonardo Grimaldi said on 6 November, adding that Chinese order intake showed "no decrease at all."

The counterweights are real. Chinese hardwood spot in Shandong averaged 4,613.33 RMB a tonne on 20 November, up 0.51% in five days and still near the lows even as imports rose 23% month on month. Suzano lifted full-year cash cost guidance from R$800 to R$825 a tonne because Brent ran $91 to $98 against a planning assumption of $84. Buying Kimberly-Clark's stake in the Arbex consumer business pushed net debt to R$76.3bn, US$14.7bn, and leverage to 3.7x, while Arbex's 16% margin helped compress consolidated gross margin to 23.7% from 30.4%. Suzano guides net debt below US$14bn by year-end on seasonal fourth-quarter cash flow, and chief executive João Fernandez de Abreu said on 6 November that after Arbex, "nothing else is on our agenda at the moment."

What the split actually rests on

Cerrado is no longer the variable: the 2.55m-tonne mill hit nameplate within months of its 2024 start-up and should run near 2.57m tonnes this year. What moved in Suzano's favor was other producers' downtime and a competitor's Indonesian delay, conditions it does not control, met by a cost line it does. Klabin's position is the mirror image: a price book that worked and a forest that did not.

That accounts for most of the 18-point gap between the two shares over the past month, and it is why the cheaper of the two on every currency-consistent anchor is the one that rose. Suzano trades at 5.39x trailing EV/EBITDA and a 9.1% free-cash-flow yield against Klabin's 6.09x and 2.5%. Forward earnings multiples on both are unusable, dividing dollar or five-share unit prices by reais per-share estimates.

Klabin expects the rain premium out of its cost line by the second quarter of 2027. Later the same year Arauco's Sucuriú mill in Mato Grosso do Sul is guided to start up, carrying more new short-fibre capacity in a single plant than Suzano's Cerrado added, and returning both Brazilian producers to the question neither can settle on its own: what a tonne is worth in China.

Ticket grew, traffic did not

Walmart de México's Mexican same-store sales rose 1.7% in the third quarter, and all of it came from the price on the receipt. Ticket was up 1.6% and customer traffic was flat for the first time in nine quarters. The format that gave way is the one serving the lowest-income shoppers: Bodega Aurrerá, the 2,198-store discount chain, came in below Walmex's internal expectations on lower traffic and smaller shopping baskets, with weakness concentrated in larger stores in central Mexico, while Sam's Club led growth.

Mexican consumer staples are owned as the defensive corner of a market that has been working. The S&P/BMV IPC set an all-time high of 72,937 on 11 November, up about 11.3% on the year. Walmex closed at MXN53.19 on 27 November, down 12.1% over twelve months; of seventeen large Mexican listings, only the tequila maker Becle fell further. What has been marked down is the end of the shelf where the household's income arrives from abroad.

Where the basket's money comes from

Remittances to Mexico came to $5.64bn in October, down 1.7% year on year and the seventh consecutive monthly contraction. The composition matters: the number of transfers fell 5.4% while the average transfer rose 4.0% to $403, so fewer senders are sending more. Analysts in that account tie the decline to a weakening US labor market and political caution among migrants.

The 1% US excise on remittances that took effect on 1 January is the smaller force. It applies only where the sender funds a transfer with cash, a money order or a cashier's check; bank, wire, credit and debit funding are exempt, and 99.1% of January-to-October flows to Mexico arrived electronically.

The currency did the heavier work.

Banco de México's October board minutes record a member warning that the purchasing power of remittances, in pesos and net of inflation, has fallen more than 20% over eighteen months after a 10.2% appreciation, and tying that erosion to Mexican private consumption growth of just 0.8% in the first half.

It shows in the margin. Walmex operating income has fallen year on year for three straight quarters, by 2.2%, 1.4% and 0.8%, and third-quarter net income dropped 6.1% to MXN11.03bn as consolidated EBITDA margin narrowed 30 basis points to 10.2%. Guidance of 3.5% to 4.5% constant-currency sales growth was cut in April. "Consumer spending remains soft, and our performance is not yet where we want it to be," chief executive Cristian Barrientos Pozo said of a soft first quarter. On the 28 October call he described the answer: "We launched a huge campaign that called Precios que no se tocan with more than 4,000 items. We fixed the prices for more than 90 days."

Oxxo bills the flow its own shoppers live on

FEMSA reaches the same households through a different door. Oxxo Mexico same-store sales rose 9.2% last quarter, split 5.5% ticket and 3.5% traffic, and the convenience arm is also part of the country's cash plumbing: Oxxo pays remittances across more than 21,000 stores, and Spin by Oxxo, the largest banking correspondent in Mexico by regulator count, reached 11.7 million active users, up 17.3%, its loyalty program present in 52.1% of Oxxo Mexico transactions against 48.2% a year earlier. The ADR's 35.3% twelve-month gain came with compression, to 23.5x trailing earnings from roughly 46x; forward estimates of 25.5x sit above trailing, consensus saying the trailing figure, flattered by currency gains and discontinued-operations comparisons, comes down from here.

Banorte's funding cost stopped falling

The lender tests the other mechanism, and it failed. Bank net interest margin landed at the high end of guidance at 6.8%, with performing loans up 11% to MXN1.33tn, deposits up 13%, fees up 14% and return on equity of 24.5%. Nine-month profit rose 8.7% and the MXN62-64bn full-year target stands. Deposit margins are not being squeezed by an easing cycle because there barely is one: Banco de México cut to 6.25% on 5 November and signaled an extended hold. The soft spot is credit cost at 2.3% against 1.8% to 2.1% guidance, which management attributes mostly to loan growth. At 9.9x trailing earnings on 2.27x book, against about 8.8x a year ago, a little over half the 20.3% share gain is re-rating.

The month the shares moved without the numbers

Walmex gained 9.9% in the 30 days to 27 November, fifth-best among those seventeen listings, after trading below its moving averages from March to mid-October. No reported figure improved in between. Buen Fin brought more than 76 million transactions and the company's highest sales day on record on 17 November, yet fell short of Walmex's own initial expectations. The likelier reading is the currency: the peso closed at 19.71 to the dollar on 26 November, its weakest in twelve months, against 16.85 in early September, so each transferred dollar now converts into roughly a sixth more pesos than it did then.

The twelve-month de-rating was earned. Trailing earnings per share of MXN2.85 are slightly below the MXN2.91 of a year earlier, taking the trailing multiple to 18.7x from roughly 20.8x, and consensus has 2026 earnings growth at 1.5%. The November leg rests on relief that has not reached a results statement. The other two are different cases, and for a reason: Oxxo collects a fee on the inflow whatever its size, and Banorte's margin is set by a central bank that has stopped cutting.

Ahead of all three sits the wage. Proposals for 2027 were due on 30 November, with unions seeking 15% and employers about 6%; Walmex has guided to roughly 12% plus a workweek cut from 48 hours to 46, FEMSA to 13%.

That ruling lands on both sides of the Walmex ledger at once. It raises what the company pays its 240,779 employees and what the shopper who stopped walking into Bodega Aurrerá brings home, and only one of those two reaches January's income statement.
