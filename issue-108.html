TAL Rebuilt the Fattest Margins in Chinese Tutoring and Is the Only One Down This Month

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The three Chinese tutoring companies climbed together over the summer on the premise that the businesses they rebuilt after academic tutoring was banned in 2021 are compounding. Their filings say the group has already split, and it split on profitability: TAL ran an 18.1% operating margin in its May quarter against 2.5% a year earlier, New Oriental 11.4% for its fiscal year, Gaotu minus 9.0% because selling expense alone eats 54.7% of revenue.

Since 9 October the company with the sharpest margin turn has lost 18.7% of its value with nothing disclosed, sliding into a 29 October print at 7.4x forward earnings. New Oriental's advance is supported by its own accounts; Gaotu's is not. And a Peking University study published 21 October put the share of Chinese students buying academic tutoring at 20.1% in 2025 against 38.1% in 2017, with the top tenth of students funding three-quarters of all spending.

TAL Education, which sells small-group classes for kindergarten-to-grade-12 students under the Xueersi and Izhikang brands plus learning tablets, reports second-quarter fiscal 2027 results before the market opens on Thursday 29 October, having lost 18.7% of its value in nine sessions with no filing or announcement of its own in between. New Oriental, which reports a day earlier, was flat across the same stretch. Gaotu Techedu fell 8.4%, and Alibaba, the readiest proxy for the New York-listed China complex, fell 8.6%.

The summer trade in these three was that the businesses rebuilt after Beijing banned for-profit academic tutoring in 2021 were compounding in unison: over three months to 22 October New Oriental rose 20.5% and Gaotu 51.8%. The accounts say the group has split, and it split on profit. On their latest reported quarters TAL ran an 18.1% operating margin, New Oriental 5.6% in a seasonally weak quarter and 11.4% across its fiscal year, and Gaotu minus 9.0%. The sharpest turn in the group belongs to the one share that has gone backward.

The margin came from centers leased a year earlier

TAL's May quarter put revenue at $758.4m, up 31.9% in dollars, with gross margin at 57.8% and operating income of $137.2m. Roughly seven points of that headline growth was currency translation: in renminbi the increase was about 25%. The company operated more than 600 learning centers across 44 cities as of its 30 July call, and because a center is leased and staffed before its seats are sold, the margin jump is mostly a utilization reading on capacity committed the year before.

The hardware question resolved the same way. Learning content, books, apps and devices reached 35.3% of fiscal 2026 revenue from 31.8%, and group gross margin widened to 55.4% from 53.3%. Weekly active devices passed 2 million units at about an hour of daily use each, and the T6 tablet launched in July 2026. "Our first quarter performance in fiscal year 2027 reflects solid execution across our businesses," president and chief financial officer Alex Peng said on 30 July.

New Oriental's growth sits in the lines the ban created

New Oriental's quarterly revenue growth accelerated through fiscal 2026 to 23.2%, and full-year operating income rose 50.7% on 15.9% more revenue, lifting the operating margin to 11.4% from 8.7%. Its physical network — 75 schools and 1,459 learning centers across 69 cities at 31 May 2026 — is 94% of the center count it held before the ban, carrying a different product mix: non-academic courses booked about 1,072,000 enrollments in the May quarter, while overseas test preparation, the line the ban never touched, grew 6%. "In this fiscal quarter, we executed cautious capacity expansion while carefully balancing revenue growth and operational efficiency," chief executive Chenggang Zhou said on 29 July. The company then guided fiscal 2027 revenue to $6,453.9m-$6,680.3m, growth of 14-18% and a deceleration. Its deferred revenue grew 14.8%, well behind the 23.2% it funds; TAL's book grew 25.9% against 31.9% revenue growth. At all three, prepaid tuition is filling more slowly than the income statement drains it.

Gaotu is the counterexample management itself describes. Gross margin of 66.5% in the June quarter is the best of the three, but selling expenses of RMB913.2m took 54.7% of revenue, about four-fifths of gross profit, and revenue growth has whipsawed from 30.7% to 19.9% over four quarters. "Footprint growth itself is not the main KPI," chief operating officer Bin Luo said on 27 August. Consensus asks for a RMB14.4m net profit in 2026 against a RMB101.2m loss already booked in the first half. Price-to-book of 5.70x is the only usable anchor on a loss-making business, against TAL's 0.47x.

A narrowing pool, and money that stays at the top

The new information about the market all three sell into arrived on 21 October, when Caixin reported a Peking University study finding that the share of Chinese students receiving academic tutoring fell to 20.1% in 2025 from 38.1% in 2017, with non-academic participation down to 16.7% from 22.4%. Spending per student fell only 11.8%, to RMB2,873, and the top 10% of students accounted for 75.8% of it. Enrollment growth now has to be taken from a shrinking, premium-skewed pool. Beijing has not loosened the rule either: in January 2026 regulators fined an unregistered education firm RMB67m explicitly to kill rumors of a reversal.

How much of the price action the businesses explain differs by name. New Oriental's three-month gain is supported by accelerating revenue and expanding margin, and at 13.3x forward earnings against 19.8x trailing it is paid for roughly 26% consensus earnings growth. Gaotu's 51.8% advance has no earnings underneath it. TAL's fall has no company cause: its forward price-to-gross-profit compressed from 3.73x on 9 October to 3.03x with no estimate revision, and the 21-22 October weakness across the complex traced to China's market regulator pressing e-commerce platforms on product quality, with TAL's own slide attributed to Chinese shares retreating on consumer worries ahead of its results. The likelier reading is the complex rather than the company, in a market where MSCI China consumer staples missed profit expectations by 47% last season and consumer sub-indexes sit near decade lows. Averaged over longer windows the price trend for all three still reads as rising, which at this point lags what the shares have actually done.

The split has a cause on each side. New Oriental earns on distribution density, TAL on filling centers and devices it has already paid for, Gaotu on traffic it rents by the quarter. Two of those three get a fresh reading inside 48 hours.

What neither print can settle is the arithmetic underneath all of it: a business can fill every seat it owns and still be selling into a country where four students in five now buy no tutoring at all.

Intapp's Shrinking On-Premise Licenses Hide a Cloud Business Compounding at 29%

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Intapp's reported revenue growth has slowed in each of the last four quarters, and the obvious reading — a vertical software vendor losing ground as artificial intelligence commoditizes what it sells — is not what its own disclosure says. The deceleration is mix. Subscription cloud revenue is compounding while the residual on-premise license and maintenance base shrinks in absolute terms, subtracting roughly 14 points of headline growth.

Cloud annual recurring revenue reached $495.7m at the end of June, and expansion inside the existing client base improved to 123% trailing-twelve-month net revenue retention from 119%. What has not improved is the bottom line: the fiscal 2026 operating loss widened to $40.1m from $27.4m.

The shares have re-rated to 6.3x sales from 4.9x in five weeks, on no new company data at all.

Intapp sells software to private capital firms, investment banks, law firms and accountants: DealCloud for deal and relationship management, client-onboarding and risk-monitoring compliance tools, time capture and billing. Its reported revenue grew 13.0% in the June quarter, the fourth consecutive quarter of slower growth, down from 17.0% three quarters earlier. Read on the top line alone, that is a company losing the argument about whether specialized workflow software survives generative artificial intelligence.

The company's own numbers describe something else. Cloud annual recurring revenue reached $495.7m at 30 June 2026, up 29% and now 84% of total recurring revenue against 79% a year earlier. Total annual recurring revenue rose 22% to $590.5m. The belief being tested across this corner of software — that the 2026 de-rating was a demand event — fails on Intapp's disclosure: demand inside the installed base got better, and the headline got worse, for the same reason.

The 14 points the license book is taking out

Software-as-a-service revenue grew 27% in the June quarter to $115.0m while total revenue grew 13% to $152.5m. The arithmetic of that gap is the legacy book: the on-premise license and maintenance base is in absolute decline, subtracting roughly 14 points of reported growth. Full-year revenue was $577.8m, up 14.6%. Guidance for fiscal 2027 subscription revenue is $528.7m to $532.7m.

The retention number is the one that settles whether this is managed transition or decay. Trailing-twelve-month cloud net revenue retention was 123%, improved from 119% fifteen months earlier. Existing clients are spending more per year, not less.

What a regulated vertical buys you

The reason that matters is where Intapp sells. Conflicts clearance, deal screening, lateral-hire diligence and client onboarding in law, accounting and private capital are workflows with regulatory consequences, which is the hardest ground for a general-purpose model to take. The company made Celeste, which it pitches as an "expert AI coworker," generally available in July 2026 and has since named Ropes & Gray and Forvis Mazars Capital Advisors as customers.

"We had an exceptional year advancing our Firm AI strategy, agentic capabilities with Celeste, and unique competitive position for highly regulated firms," chief executive John Hall said on 4 August 2026.

The cost side has not cooperated. Gross margin improved to 75.8% from 74.0%, but the fiscal 2026 operating loss widened to $40.1m, or -6.9% of revenue, against $27.4m a year earlier. The trailing price-to-earnings ratio of -90.7x is a byproduct of that, not a reading.

A price that moved with nothing behind it

Intapp's last operating disclosure was 4 August. The stock is up 29.7% in the 30 days to 22 October and 79.6% over 90 days, and in the five weeks to 22 October its price-to-sales multiple went to 6.3x from 4.9x, with price-to-gross-profit at 8.3x against 6.4x. Nothing was published in between.

The company is not alone. Twelve of the thirteen members of its vertical-software shelf rose over those 30 days, median 21.7%, and the biggest gainer was an acquisition: PTC, the engineering software vendor, agreed on 5 October to be bought by Schneider Electric at $205 a share in cash, a 42.3% premium. The complex more broadly has been recovering from the first-quarter sell-off dubbed the "SaaSpocalypse," with software stocks reaching 2026 highs in early October as AI-disruption fears faded.

So the business and the move are both real, and they are not the same event. Essentially none of the last month's gain is accounted for by Intapp's results, because there were none; the summer leg of the recovery followed an August print in which the cloud engine and retention both improved. Forward multiples price the resolution of the mix problem — 5.5x forward sales, and a forward price-to-earnings ratio of 29.5x against GAAP losses today — while consensus models 14.6% revenue growth in fiscal 2027, above the 13.0% just reported. That is a bet that the license drag runs out before the cloud growth rate does.

The license base will finish shrinking eventually, and when it does the reported number and the cloud number converge somewhere near each other. Until then every quarter reads worse on the line most people look at than the business underneath it actually is, and the market has already paid for the convergence.

Dutch Bros Pays for Today's Coffee Rally Two to Three Quarters From Now

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Both scaled US coffee retailers climbed about 7% over the past month, and the reading on offer is that the economics of selling a cup of coffee have turned. The cost side says otherwise. Arabica is back near $3.11 a pound with certified exchange stocks at a 27-year low, and Dutch Bros has told investors coffee prices reach its income statement two to three quarters after they move.

The duty everyone watched is settled: Washington carved coffee out of the 25% Brazil tariff. The live cap is the bean itself. Green coffee turned back up through October, and the chain that has spelled out how long beans take to reach its income statement is the same one that has guided its comparable sales lower. Dutch Bros, the drive-thru coffee operator founded in Grants Pass, Oregon, has said that changes in coffee prices typically flow through its profit and loss account with a two-to-three-quarter delay, given inventory turns. Arabica closed near $3.11 a pound on the ICE exchange on 22 October, roughly 14% higher over thirty days.

Shares in both scaled US coffee retailers rose about 7% over that same window, and the bounce has been read as four-wall economics repairing. The disclosures support something narrower. The cost input is re-inflating while the price contribution inside Dutch Bros' comparable sales is scheduled to fade, and the duty question that was supposed to be the shared ceiling on both chains has already been resolved in their favor.

The tariff that never arrived

Green coffee was exempted from the US reciprocal tariff retroactive to 13 November 2025, and the Trade Representative's final notice spared coffee from the 25% Section 301 Brazil duty that took effect on 22 July 2026, because Brazil supplies close to a third of US green coffee imports and there is no quick substitute. The carve-out was estimated to protect $2bn to $2.5bn of Brazilian coffee exports.

What is left is physical scarcity. December arabica settled at $3.157 a pound on 19 October with stocks in ICE warehouses near a 27-year low.

A $2.2m shop against a $1.4m build

Dutch Bros' June quarter was strong on every line that matters to a unit-growth story: revenue up 32.5% to $550.9m, system same-shop sales up 5.8% on transactions up 1.7%, and guidance raised to a 5%-6% comparable range. Average unit volumes reached $2.2m with shop contribution margin near 31%, and the company opened 48 shops at roughly $1.4m of capital expenditure each, funded from $196.9m of first-half operating cash flow. No cash-on-cash return figure was disclosed; those inputs imply something in the high-40s percent on a mature shop, but that is arithmetic, not a company number.

Then the guidance for the September quarter: system same-shop sales of 4%-5%, below the quarter just delivered, on tougher transaction comparisons and a sequential step-down in effective pricing. Gross margin had already eased 145 basis points to 27.4%. Full-year guidance contemplates about 80 basis points of cost-of-goods pressure, front-loaded at roughly 200 basis points in the March quarter.

Chief financial officer Josh Guenser has called high coffee prices "the biggest headwind" the company faces, and the reason it has not reached its 30% shop-margin goal, while arguing costs should normalize. That was said on the February call, before October's rally.

Starbucks is improving four walls and removing 250 of them

Starbucks' June quarter answered the question the market asked of it. US comparable sales rose 7.9% on transactions up 4.2%, carried by traffic rather than price, and North America segment operating margin widened to 13.6% from 13.3%. Reported revenue fell 1.4% on the China deconsolidation. "The shine is back on Starbucks," chairman and chief executive Brian Niccol told CNBC on 10 September.

The estate is shrinking underneath that margin. Starbucks confirmed on 24 September it will close about 250 North America cafes against roughly $300m of restructuring charges, and cut its fiscal-2026 net new store target to about 440, all international.

Why one is expensive and the other halved

Thirty basis points of segment margin improvement, against a shrinking US unit count, is what twelve months of Starbucks' 19% share gain rests on, at 58.6x trailing earnings and 32.3x fiscal-2027 consensus of $3.16 — a figure still 12% below the $3.58 the company earned in fiscal 2023. The growth multiple is being paid for a return to a four-year-old earnings level.

Dutch Bros went the other way with better reported numbers. Revenue growth accelerated from 30.8% to 32.5% across two quarters while the shares fell 49% from their 1 July high, compressing the multiple on fiscal-2026 consensus from 76.5x to 43.6x, and 32.9x on 2027. Nothing in the results deteriorated; the pricing lever did, and the bean cost that lands two to three quarters late has started climbing again.

The October move explains itself without either store model. Starbucks fell 4.9% on 8 October on a report it had discussed a Chipotle takeover with advisers, and recovered after a spokesperson told CNBC on 12 October that "Starbucks is not buying Chipotle." Dutch Bros bounced off a 12 October low of $37.28 and was met by cuts: RBC Capital downgraded it to Sector Perform on 21 October with a $46 target, from $70, and Citigroup trimmed its target to $69 from $86 the same day.

Starbucks reports fourth-quarter and full-year results after the close on 29 October, Dutch Bros its third quarter on 4 November. Neither print will contain October's coffee price. By Dutch Bros' own description of how its inventory works, that bill arrives sometime next year.

Vicor's October quarter answered a question the design-win announcements could not: how much of the 800-volt data-center power transition is billable today. The Andover, Massachusetts maker of modular DC-DC converters and custom power systems reported $197.1m of revenue for its third quarter, and $66.6m of that was royalties on a patent it is asserting against twenty respondents at the US International Trade Commission.

The belief the shares carried into October was that the architecture pays its component suppliers now. NVIDIA's own schedule puts the content later: the 800-volt rack is an optional configuration for Vera Rubin, with broader adoption expected at Rubin Ultra in the second half of 2027, the generation whose Kyber racks draw 600kW. Vicor and Navitas Semiconductor, the two specialists built for that conversion chain, each lost about a third of their market value over the thirty days to 22 October. Each has found a different way to cross the gap to 2027, and only one of them is being paid to wait.

The docket became a revenue line

On 9 October the trade commission instituted an investigation of vertical power delivery modules and computing systems on Vicor's complaint over US Patent No. 10,903,734, naming Delta Electronics, Infineon Technologies, Monolithic Power Systems, Flex, Celestica, Quanta and Hon Hai among the respondents, and seeking exclusion and cease-and-desist orders. The accused products are the systems that power AI accelerators, graphics processors and tensor processing units, which means importers of unlicensed designs need a license from Vicor. Vicor's royalty rates escalate across stages tied to how far that investigation has progressed; it has signed a first vertical-power-delivery license with a leading AI original-equipment manufacturer at stage-two rates, and that customer placed a further significant order at the start of the fourth quarter.

Royalties of $66.6m were up from $30.4m in the prior quarter. Strip them out of both periods and the implied product revenue still rose, from about $113.0m to about $130.5m, a derived increase of roughly 15.5%. Advanced Products reached $139m, up 47.5% sequentially and 70.4% of the total. One-year backlog was $406m at quarter-end, up 7% sequentially with book-to-bill above 1, and gross margin expanded from 58.0% to 68.3%. Licensing added to hardware growth here; it did not replace it.

"So as of yesterday, the backlog is actually closer to $400 million," Patrizio Vinciarelli, Vicor's chief executive, told investors on the 20 October call, marking the figure down slightly from the quarter-end number. The mechanism he described is physical. "Every generation of AI processor is stepping up its current requirements...it is now at the thousand amp level and on its way to thousands of amps," Vinciarelli said on the same call. Vicor's capacity to answer that is dated: equipment deployment at its second fab begins in the second quarter of 2027, funded from $513m of cash without a raise.

The fourth quarter steps down

Guidance is where the composition bites. Product revenue is guided to roughly 10% sequential growth and royalties to a minimum of $43m, which works out to about $186m in total, some 5% below the third quarter. The quarter that beat consensus on both lines, $1.33 against $1.29 of expected earnings per share and $197.1m against $183.7m, is not the quarter being annualized.

The shares rose 7.1% on the print to $204.14. The 13.1% break came two sessions later, on a day the whole AI-power complex fell on data-center financing-cost concerns, with GE Vernova and Bloom Energy each off more than 11% and Vertiv down 8.7%, after Lam Research's chief financial officer Doug Bettinger said supply-chain constraints had held back the December-quarter guide. Over the same thirty days onsemi rose 3.1% and Monolithic Power fell 7.9%, so the de-rating concentrated in the names whose content is dated furthest out.

Navitas has the roadmap and no gross profit

Navitas, a 190-person designer of gallium-nitride and silicon-carbide power devices, is the same timing problem without the licensing bridge. Second-quarter revenue was $10.53m, down 27.3% year on year as it exits mobile and consumer chargers, with negative gross profit in each of the first two quarters of 2026. It guided the third quarter to $13.5m, plus or minus $0.5m, on more than 50% year-on-year growth in high-power markets, and expects AI data centers plus grid and energy infrastructure to exceed one-third of sales by the end of 2026, implying roughly $4m to $5m a quarter. Management describes the 800-volt shift as three staged inflections running from the second half of 2026 through "late 2027 and early 2028". It closed the $232.8m acquisition of Claros on 6 October to reach the final conversion stage inside the processor, and at October's GTC Berlin conference Infineon announced its own gallium-nitride collaboration with NVIDIA for 800-volt racks covering the same 800-volt-to-6-volt sockets.
