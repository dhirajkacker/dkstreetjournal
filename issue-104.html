A January claim the market read as a deletion

The equipment that dumps an AI data center's heat into the outside air looked, for one session in January, like a line item about to be erased. Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang said at CES on 6 January that the company's Vera Rubin systems use warm-water direct liquid cooling at a 45°C supply temperature with "no chillers necessary", and shares in cooling-equipment makers fell the same day. Nvidia's argument was about power: chilled-water plant can account for nearly 40% of a data center's electricity use, and 45°C water is warm enough to reject outdoors.

That belief has been priced repeatedly, and it is testable against what the equipment makers have disclosed. SPX Technologies engineers, builds and services evaporative cooling towers and fluid coolers under the Marley and Recold names, sold through independent manufacturing representatives. In the three months to 13 October its shares fell 22.2% to a 52-week low of $167.28, 32.1% below the year's high, while Johnson Controls, which sells building HVAC and controls, rose 6.5% and the S&P 500 tracker gained 3.7%. The selling was aimed at cooling content, not at industrials.

What the company said while the shares fell

On 30 July SPX raised full-year guidance, to revenue of $2.705-2.765bn and an adjusted earnings midpoint $0.45 higher at $8.40. It also lifted its 2026 data-center revenue expectation from $300m to $430m, and raised estimated annual data-center capacity at full production from about $750m to about $1.1bn. Chief executive Gene Lowe walked the ramp back through its own history on that call: roughly $150m of volume two years ago, $200m last year, a $300m plan entering 2026. "The market is shifting towards our solutions," he said, citing plant throughput running ahead of plan.

The June quarter grew revenue 22.9% to $679m with operating margin up 149 basis points to 17.2%. Gross margin fell 120 basis points to 40.2%, so the margin gain came from operating leverage on fixed costs rather than from price. HVAC backlog ended the quarter at $919m, up 59% organically, on a segment book-to-bill near 1.4 times.

The objection the market actually voiced was balance-sheet, not demand: the shares fell 6.6% after the guidance raise on leverage from the acquisition program, which added Neptronic in July and then FIS Water for $410m in cash on 15 September, a water-control supplier expected to contribute about $105m of 2026 revenue. October added a second channel: a financing scare on the buildout's borrowers that widened credit spreads at the companies renting out the racks. That reaches SPX only through its customers' ability to fund projects.

The de-rating is real and large. SPX trades at 29.8x trailing earnings against 41.9x in May, and 20.1x forward — but consensus 2026 revenue sits at the top of management's own guided range, so the forward figure rests on the company hitting its own upper end.

Dover sells the coupling and cannot show you the revenue

One layer in, the same heat passes through quick-disconnect couplings, and Dover's Colder Products Company is the industry reference for the blind-mate, non-spill, hot-swappable version used at hyperscale volumes; each liquid-cooled rack takes dozens of them plus consumables. That business sits inside the roughly $1.6bn Pump Solutions Group, within one of five segments at a $25.9bn conglomerate. In the June quarter Pumps & Process Solutions grew about 0.4% organically on $553m of revenue, with bookings up 11% and margin up 170 basis points on biopharma mix.

"In data centers, the density of thermal requirements of new chips are driving a shift towards liquid cooling, which directly benefits our connectors," chief executive Rich Tobin told investors on 23 July. The only number attached to any of it is a group aggregate: management expects over $1bn of 2026 revenue from applications tied to artificial intelligence and power generation, which is about 12% of the consensus revenue base by our arithmetic, and lumps cooling in with turbines' end markets.

Dover's own cooling exposure elsewhere makes the point harder. Climate & Sustainability Technologies grew 8% organically to $455m and led group bookings with 46% growth, and heat exchangers had their best quarter ever on liquid-cooling demand. The segment still lost 200 basis points of margin to a food-retail refrigeration footprint consolidation, which Tobin estimated cost one to one and a half points of group organic growth. Group bookings rose 16.1% to $2.3bn across all five segments and guidance went up, to $10.55-10.75 adjusted earnings per share. The shares fell 9.7% that day and 11.1% over three months. Dover is now the cheapest of the three at 18.0x forward earnings against 23.1x trailing.

The name that stayed still

Parker-Hannifin's Quick Coupling Division is the meaningful number two bidding for the same socket, with Stäubli and Walther-Präzision as European challengers. Its June quarter was aerospace: record segment sales of $1.9bn, 13.4% organic growth, orders up 18% and backlog up 15% to $8.5bn. Parker was flat over three months and is the most expensive of the three at 26.9x forward against 33.0x trailing. Its flatness is the cleanest evidence that what sold was cooling content.

So the three-month decline has one documented cause and one undocumented one. Dover's own factory move explains a measurable slice of its quarter, and the company named it. Nothing SPX has published explains a fall of more than a fifth: orders, backlog, guidance and disclosed data-center revenue all moved up, and sell-side analysts argued the January spec change was misread, with TD Cowen noting cooling towers remain essential to heat rejection in liquid-cooled systems. What the price now carries is leverage and customer funding, neither of which shows up in a backlog figure.

The heat still has to go somewhere. SPX's own account of where says $430m of it this year, and the shares have spent three months pricing an answer the company has not given.

ESAB Paid More Interest on Its Inspection Acquisition Than the Acquisition Earned

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A welding company whose gross profit grew faster than its sales has lost nearly half its market value in a year, and the quarter that triggered the slide showed the business improving on every line above interest expense. ESAB's June quarter carried gross margin expansion of 86 basis points to 38.0%, and management lifted full-year core adjusted operating profit guidance to $615m-$625m from $575m-$595m.

In the same August release it cut per-share guidance, to $5.40-$5.50 from $5.70-$5.90. The cause is the $1.45bn Eddyfi purchase: in its first month inside ESAB it added seven cents of adjusted earnings while the debt and equity raised to buy it took sixteen.

The welding end market is not the problem. Lincoln Electric, the closest listed comparison, is up 6.7% over twelve months with consumable volumes still growing. The pressure at ESAB sits in EMEA and APAC, and in a balance sheet that now carries more than $2.4bn of debt.

ESAB, which sells welding wire, electrodes, flux and cutting equipment through independent industrial distributors, raised its full-year operating profit guidance in August and cut its per-share earnings guidance in the same release. The gap between the two is an interest bill.

The company lifted core adjusted EBITDA guidance to $615m-$625m from $575m-$595m, and reduced core adjusted earnings per share guidance to $5.40-$5.50 from $5.70-$5.90 — a midpoint moving from $5.80 to $5.45. The shares have since been handled as though the welding market turned over. It did not: Lincoln Electric, the Cleveland arc-welding and consumables maker founded in 1895 and the closest listed analogue to ESAB's book, is up 6.7% over twelve months and reported Americas consumable volumes growing at a low-double-digit rate in the same quarter, with mid-single-digit growth continuing into the third. Kennametal, which makes tungsten carbide cutting tools for the same metal-fabrication customers, is up 44.5% over twelve months.

Seven cents acquired, sixteen cents financed

ESAB closed the purchase of Eddyfi Technologies, a Québec non-destructive testing and inspection business, on 1 June 2026 for $1.45bn, roughly 14.5 times Eddyfi's expected 2026 EBITDA of about $100m including $20m of anticipated run-rate synergies. It paid with cash, new 5.625% senior notes due 2031 and $318m of equity placed the same day: 175,000 shares of 6.50% mandatory convertible preferred for about $175m, plus 1,254,255 common shares.

One month of ownership added $0.07 to adjusted earnings per share and the financing removed $0.16 — thirteen cents of debt cost and three of share and preferred dilution. Reported adjusted EPS of $1.33 came in below the $1.43 consensus; excluding Eddyfi and its funding it was $1.41. The welding business came within two cents. The miss was the deal.

The debt stack behind it is now $1,700m of senior unsecured notes, $350m of term loans and $360m drawn on the revolver, and interest expense and other, net rose $18.4m in the first six months of 2026, including $4.8m of bridge loan commitment fees. Chief executive Shyam Kambeyanda called the purchase "a defining step that positions ESAB for faster organic growth and higher margins" on the 6 August call.

Where the operating margin actually gave way

Above interest, the quarter reads well. Gross profit rose 15.5% on 12.9% revenue growth and gross margin expanded 86 basis points to 38.04%, helped by consumable list prices resetting against input costs — hot-rolled coil averaged about $1,213 a short ton in September against roughly $800 a year earlier — with the 10-Q attributing $14.9m of customer pricing to tariff-driven materials costs and higher oil prices.

But group adjusted EBITDA margin fell 90 basis points to 19.5% while the Americas segment margin expanded 20 basis points to 20.3% on 5% organic growth. The drag is EMEA and APAC: core sales there grew 14% but only 1% organically, with Middle East revenues down roughly 10-11% and regional logistics costs roughly tripling. Chief financial officer Brent Jones attributed the margin decline to "transitory price/cost neutrality and deliberate targeted commercial investments to accelerate growth".

The equity is cheaper than the enterprise

ESAB hit a 52-week low of $64.47 on 8 October against a 52-week high of $137.42, falling 7.1% on 28 September and 7.7% on 8 October; it closed at $65.06 on 14 October, down 25.4% in three months and 45.1% in twelve. JPMorgan cut its target to $95.00 from $125.00 on 8 October while keeping an overweight rating.

On price to trailing gross profit — the comparison that matters when revenue is a metal index multiplied by pounds somebody else burns — ESAB trades at 3.69x against Lincoln Electric's 8.86x, on near-identical gross margins of 38.0% and 36.8%.

Move to the enterprise and most of that discount disappears: 13.0x trailing EV/EBITDA against Lincoln's 17.1x. The difference is the $2.4bn of debt, which sits inside enterprise value and not inside the share count. ESAB's 23.8x trailing price-to-earnings is on GAAP earnings of roughly $2.73 a share, depressed by deal and financing charges, while the 12.0x forward figure is on consensus adjusted earnings of $5.41 — a definitional gap, not a growth expectation.

The operating results do not account for a 45% decline. A five-percent cut to a per-share midpoint, with gross profit and operating profit guidance both moving up, is not a de-rating of that size. What can account for part of it is the shape of the earnings ahead: consensus has 2026 revenue up 13.2% to $3.057bn while EBITDA sits flat at $619.1m against $619.8m, which is 270 basis points of margin bought with acquired sales, and all of the guidance raise to 11.0%-14.0% core sales growth is acquisition, with organic held at 2.0%-4.0%. The market is being asked to fund a levered earnings pause and trust the $6.37 consensus for 2027.

Third-quarter results come before the open on 4 November, the first period carrying three full months of Eddyfi's profit and of the interest raised to buy it. Until then the cheapest reading of ESAB is also the most literal one: a welding business that worked, attached to a balance sheet decision that has not paid for itself yet.

Salesforce's forward booking meter improved last quarter and its profit engine did not. The software company behind the sales, service and marketing clouds used by large enterprises disclosed current remaining performance obligation of $33.5bn as of 31 July, contracted revenue due within a year, up 14% and up 14% in constant currency, an acceleration from 13% in constant currency a quarter earlier. Reported revenue grew 10.8%, to $11.345bn.

That gap is the whole argument for owning the shares here: bookings outrunning a subscription book recognized ratably means demand arrives in revenue a year late. The August quarter was read as proof that grafting consumption pricing onto seat licenses has restarted growth. It has restarted the order book. What it has not done is produce operating leverage.

The order book moved; the income statement stood still

GAAP operating income was $2.331bn, flat against the year before, so operating margin fell to 20.5% from 22.8%. Net income rose 86.9% to $3.526bn while diluted shares fell 14.7% to 821m — reported earnings per share of $4.29 is the arithmetic of a $25bn accelerated repurchase and items below the operating line, not of a business converting growth into profit. Free cash flow did rise 81%, to $1.1bn.

The consumption lines are real and small. Agentforce annual recurring revenue passed $1.5bn, up more than 240%, with Agentforce and Data 360 together near $3.9bn. Against guided fiscal-2027 revenue of $46.1–46.4bn, the Agentforce line is roughly 3% of the company; Morgan Stanley put it at 2.6%. Subscription revenue of $10.8bn grew 12%, and included $440m from the Informatica acquisition. Attrition, excluding Slack self-service and recent acquisitions, held at about 8%.

The billing change is short-cycle rather than contractual: customers draw down Flex Credits and, as management described it, "when they want more, they raise their hand", while six of the quarter's top ten deals were unlimited enterprise license agreements. "As customer zero, we are putting Agentforce to work across our own business," chair and chief executive Marc Benioff said on the 26 August call.

One session, then a fade, then the upgrade

The re-rating was a single print. Salesforce closed at $205.62 on 26 August and $252.05 the next day, a 22.6% session, peaked at $265.44 on 3 September, and has since given back 10.0% to $238.97. Bank of America's Tal Liani upgraded the stock two notches, Underperform to Buy, raising his target to $285 from $160, on 12 October — after the fade, not before it.

At 14.3x forward earnings against 21.7x trailing, the shares already discount roughly 52% earnings growth this fiscal year. Consensus then has fiscal-2028 earnings per share 4.1% lower, at $16.07: the curve flattens the moment the forward year the multiple is struck on rolls off. A 7.7% trailing free-cash-flow yield is the cushion.

The same bargain, one layer down

Twilio, which sells messaging and voice interfaces billed per message and per minute, is living the consumption model Salesforce is adopting, and it costs margin. Revenue grew 22% in the June quarter while gross margin slipped to 48.4% from 49.1%, because US carrier surcharges are billed on at near-zero margin; 5 of its 116 points of dollar-based net expansion were those fees, and messaging grew 28% reported but 18% excluding them. Its own third-quarter guide steps organic growth to 11–12% from 17%, and at 16.0x trailing gross profit the shares sit above the $265.71 street average target.

Braze, which orchestrates push, in-app and SMS messaging inside brands' own apps, shows the mechanism with the cleanest meters: net retention of 110%, up from 108%, and 112% among the 361 customers above $500,000 of annual recurring revenue, with gross margin down 70 basis points to 66.8% on premium messaging volumes. It trades at 5.86x trailing gross profit, essentially Salesforce's 5.77x, while growing gross profit 24.6% against Salesforce's 8.8%. "For every one example that I have where people are chasing their vibe coding dreams, we've got 10 more for whom Braze is their AI bet," chief executive William Magnuson said on the 8 September call.

What the quarter does and does not pay for

The booking data supports the view that front-office software is being expanded rather than replaced: raised full-year guidance, accelerating contracted revenue, steady attrition. It does not support the view that consumption pricing is yet paying the bill. At Salesforce the agent lines are too small to carry a $46bn book, the margin went backward, and the per-share line was bought. At Twilio and Braze, each incremental unit of metered volume arrives with its own cost of goods attached.

Treating the group as one trade also flatters it. Across the seven front-office names, the twelve-month advance is two stocks — Twilio up 163.6% and Zeta up 83.4% — against HubSpot down 50.2% and Pegasystems down 35.5%. HubSpot, at 4.0x trailing gross profit with revenue up 19.8%, is the cheapest of the group and the one that has been left out.

Twilio reports on 29 October with organic growth guided down by five points or more. That print, not an upgrade letter, is where consumption pricing gets its next invoice.

The Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas published its quarterly survey of energy firms on 30 September, and the oilfield service companies in it described a market with plenty of work and none of the pricing power. Their input-cost index came in at 60.4 against a prices-received index of 16.3. That lands against a year in which activity recovered. The U.S. rig count stood at 603 on 9 October and the frac spread count at 203, both up on the week, with Brent near $102.60 in early October after a Middle East war. Rising rigs were widely read as the setup for a service-pricing recovery. The two listed companies paid to work on wells that are already drilled say otherwise, and both broke uptrends they had held for months on 29 and 30 September.

Ranger added a quarter more rig hours and bought every one of them

Ranger Energy Services, the Houston contractor that sends high-specification workover rigs back to producing wells to pull tubing, plug them or log them, billed 146,800 rig hours in its High Specification Rigs segment in the June quarter, up 25% year-on-year. That is almost precisely the roughly 25% rig count added by American Well Services, bought for about $90.5m in November 2025. Sequentially, hours rose 1%.

The rate line is padded too. Realised pricing of $772 an hour was 5% above a year earlier, an increase Ranger's own filing says "largely" reflects "the pass through of fuel surcharges as well as certain changes in asset and regional revenue mix." A 540-rig fleet is the largest single participant in a market of 1,000 to 1,200 well-servicing rigs where the ten leading providers hold only 50-60%; scale of that kind does not set price.

The lines that carry no pass-through show a decent quarter. Cost of services was 81% of revenue against 82%. Wireline, the completion-linked segment funded out of customer capital budgets, fell 16% year-on-year to $18.6m but swung to $1.0m of operating income from a $2.4m loss in March. Chief executive Stuart Bodden said on the 28 July call the quarter "reinforced the earnings power we believed we could achieve following the AWS acquisition," and guidance of more than $100m of adjusted EBITDA for 2026 was reaffirmed; chief financial officer Melissa Cougle put the softness in rig margins down to "an unusual state sales-tax audit" and make-ready costs for new hybrid-electric rigs. At 5.0x trailing enterprise value to EBITDA, with a forward price-to-earnings of 15.7x against 24.9x trailing, the market is priced for those earnings arriving. The caveat is that the cheap denominator was acquired rather than grown: STEP Energy's U.S. coiled tubing assets closed on 8 October for roughly $27.5m, and first-half free cash flow was minus $1.7m even with $20.0m generated in the June quarter.

The laboratory is paid per cargo, and the cargoes stopped moving

Core Laboratories, which analyses reservoir rock and fluids in laboratories across some 50 countries and separately manufactures perforating charges and completion products, inverted the expected pattern. The asset-light lab was the weak half: Reservoir Description revenue fell 9% year-on-year to $78.7m at a 5% operating margin, while the factory half, Production Enhancement, grew 5% to $45.9m with margins before items of 12% against 5% three months earlier.

The reason is physical. Closure of the Strait of Hormuz cut global crude cargo movements 16% from pre-conflict levels, and an assay laboratory bills per cargo sampled. "Reservoir Description bore the greatest impacts of the Middle East conflict as crude assay work and reservoir characterization projects depend on field operations, active maritime hydrocarbon trade and transportation, timely sample acquisition, and access to client facilities," chairman and chief executive Lawrence Bruno told the second-quarter call. Consensus now has 2026 earnings of $0.48 a share against $0.68 delivered in 2025, which is why the forward price-to-earnings of 21.1x sits above the 18.1x trailing, and why 9.7x enterprise value to EBITDA is close to double Ranger's.

Two squeezes with different escapes

Most of Ranger's 11.6% thirty-day decline was shared with the rest of the sector: Halliburton fell 8.9% over the same stretch, Liberty Energy 11.0% and ProPetro 24.8%. Core Labs' 19.6% is its own, and extends a 41% fall over six months.

The split has a cause. Ranger's price is capped by a fragmented domestic market with spare capacity, but its volume is purchasable, and the one place it holds a premium is technology: Chevron contracted three more hybrid-electric ECHO rigs in July for 2027 Bakken delivery. Core Labs cannot buy sample volume at all; it needs tankers sailing and client sites open, and U.S. exploration budgets guided 5% lower to $59.1bn this year will not supply the difference.

So the late-September break reads as a judgment on price realization rather than on activity. It is only half-earned at Ranger, where margins widened and guidance held, and fully earned at Core Labs, where two consecutive quarters of falling operating income on barely lower revenue show a fixed-cost laboratory network losing leverage.

Ranger's hourly rate can be topped up with a fuel surcharge for as long as diesel cooperates. A sample count has no such padding: somebody has to put the cargo on the water first.

Cipher Digital went back to the market for another $850m on 13 October, in convertible senior notes that carry a coupon of zero. The filed terms earmark the proceeds for capped calls, additional equity in the Barber Lake project and high-performance-computing construction across the near-term pipeline, with a 13-day option on a further $150m; the company simultaneously sought to exchange part of its 1.75% notes due 2030. The shares closed at $11.095, down 14.7%.

The quarter behind that offering looks like a company coming apart: revenue down 43.0% year on year to $24.8m, gross margin of -131.1%, a $267.5m net loss. It is the conversion working. Cipher decommissioned bitcoin mining at Black Pearl to clear the building for Amazon Web Services, so the revenue that vanished is revenue the company chose to switch off. For a converting miner the top line is mute. The meters that speak are contracted megawatts, the date they energize, the capex to get there and who pays it in the meantime.

Hashprice pays by the day, a lease pays by the kilowatt-month

What remains of the mining business is 207 MW at Odessa running about 11.6 exahash per second under a fixed-price power contract with Vistra Luminant near 2.8 cents per kilowatt-hour, which produced roughly 346 bitcoin in the June quarter. That fleet earns hashprice, the dollar revenue per petahash per second per day, which stood at about $40.45 on 3 October after roughly $27.70 in June. Bitcoin's price did that work: network difficulty has fallen about 19% from its November 2025 high of 155.97 trillion as miners exited or moved capital into AI.

The other dollar is fixed and contracted years ahead. Cipher's 15-year lease with Amazon Web Services is worth about $5.5bn on 216 MW of critical IT load at Black Pearl, triple-net: roughly $141 per kilowatt-month averaged across the term, by the arithmetic of the disclosed value, term and load.

No income statement can set those two side by side. One arrives daily at a price nobody at the company sets; the other arrives monthly, years after the ink dries.

Nine months from a signature to the first rent dollar

Cipher's first colocation contract, with Fluidstack at Barber Lake, was signed on 25 September 2025 for 168 MW of critical load. The first rent commenced at Black Pearl in August 2026, two months ahead of schedule after the tenant asked to accelerate delivery. Everything in between has to be built and paid for, and the AWS transaction carries a disclosed maximum development cost of $9.5m per IT megawatt — an order of magnitude above what an interruptible mining shed ever cost per megawatt.

It has been paid for in paper. A $150m convertible funded the first phase at Black Pearl, $1.1bn of notes due 2031 funded Barber Lake construction, and now $850m more. Diluted shares went from 360.5m to 409.4m across five quarters, before this week's issue.

The Alphabet warrant marks against Cipher's own stock

The dominant non-cash item in the June loss has nothing to do with megawatts or coins. Alphabet backstopped $1.73bn of Fluidstack's rent obligations in exchange for warrants over roughly 5.4% of Cipher on a pro forma basis, and the resulting liability is remeasured every quarter against Cipher's share price: a $150.5m loss in June, after a $43.6m gain in March. More than half the quarter's net loss was the stock going up.

This month's fall will book as a gain.

Hut 8 bought the same megawatts with cheaper money

Hut 8 is running the identical conversion through a different balance sheet. Its 245 MW River Bend lease, signed 17 December 2025 and also backstopped by Alphabet, is worth $7.0bn over 15 years with a 3.0% annual escalator, about $159 per kilowatt-month averaged; first data halls are expected in the second quarter of 2027, eighteen months after signature. Its AI landlord business earned $1.3m in the June quarter against 949 MW of contracted capacity and $26.6bn of base-term contract value.

The difference is the funding channel. Hut 8 closed $3.25bn of 6.192% senior secured notes due 2042, non-recourse, fully amortising and matched to the lease term, so the rent repays the debt that built the hall. Its shares still fell 15.6% over three months while revenue grew 81.4% and gross margin widened to 64.1%, leaving price-to-gross-profit at 128.7x against 62.8x in May.

What the results account for, and what they do not

Cipher fell 44.8% over three months, and some of that was shared with every converted miner: lease income arriving in 2027 and 2028 is discounted at a long yield that touched 5.36% on 7 October, and the converted miners sold off together in that session. The rest is specific. Amazon rose 5.4% over the same window, trades at 20.6x trailing earnings and carries $106.3bn of leases not yet commenced in one aggregate line of its March quarterly filing, Cipher's among them, unbroken out. The tenant's budget is not what moved. The landlord's share count is.

No conventional multiple survives the conversion: there are no earnings and gross profit is negative, leaving 24.7x trailing sales on a revenue line being deliberately shut down, against 30.7x in May when the market value was $6.87bn rather than today's $4.72bn. Morgan Stanley cut its target to $51.50 from $54.00 on 12 October and kept an overweight rating, which is to say the sell-side still marks the contracted asset at a level the equity no longer does.

Every converted miner is selling the same scarce thing, an energized megawatt with an interconnection behind it, and the tenants have investment-grade credit and patience. The question these two put differently is who carries the months before the first rent clears: at Hut 8, bondholders with a claim on the lease; at Cipher, whoever owns the shares when the converts turn into more of them.

ONE Gas Raised Guidance on Falling Revenue; the Shares Lost a Fifth in Six Months

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A gas distributor's sales line cannot say whether its business improved, because the molecule inside the pipe is resold at no markup. ONE Gas proved the point this year: first-half revenue fell about 8.5% while the cost of gas it passes through fell 23%, so distribution margin rose 4.2% and the company lifted its 2026 adjusted earnings range in August to $4.89-$4.95 a share from $4.65-$4.77.

The shares went the other way, down roughly 17% over six months, with the trailing earnings multiple compressed to 15.9x from 20.2x in early May. Southwest Gas reaffirmed guidance and raised its Great Basin pipeline build to about $2.3bn. New Jersey Resources is the exception: more than a fifth of guided earnings come from a wholesale gas trading book, and consensus has fiscal 2027 earnings below fiscal 2026.

None of the three reported a worse margin. What changed was the price of money.

ONE Gas bought cheaper gas this year, resold it to 2.2 million households at no markup, and kept more of what was left. Revenue for the first half fell about 8.5% while the cost of that gas fell 23%, so distribution margin rose 4.2% and net income rose with it. In August the company raised its 2026 adjusted earnings expectation to $4.89-$4.95 a share from $4.65-$4.77.

The shares did the opposite, falling about 17% over six months, with the trailing earnings multiple down to 15.9x from 20.2x in early May. A flat year across the eight listed gas distributors has been read as a flat business. The margins say otherwise: at each of the three pure-play distributors examined here the earning line either grew or was reaffirmed, and what moved underneath them was the discount rate applied to a regulated dollar.

The molecule is a pass-through; the pipe is the business

In the June quarter ONE Gas, which operates as Oklahoma Natural Gas, Kansas Gas Service and Texas Gas Service, booked $411.6m of revenue against a $90.3m cost of natural gas, versus $423.7m and $117.9m a year earlier. Revenue fell 2.9%; margin net of gas cost rose about 5.1%. That gas cost is recovered dollar for dollar through periodic adjustment clauses, so a cold winter or a spot spike inflates both lines and leaves the earning line untouched.

What does move it is approved capital recovery. ONE Gas attributed its first-quarter operating income gain "primarily" to "an increase of $27.3 million from new rates". Its 2026 docket is a stack of between-case trackers: a $28.7m Oklahoma performance-based increase recommended by an administrative law judge in June, a $36.9m Texas increase approved in June, a $14.3m Kansas reliability surcharge requested, all against authorized returns on equity of 9.4% to 9.8%. One Texas statute, House Bill 4384, which lets a gas utility defer carrying costs on plant not yet in rates, is worth about $0.42 of 2026 adjusted earnings per share, roughly 9% of guidance. Capital spending runs near $800m against a $6.3bn average rate base, while customer count grew 0.43%.

"This performance gives us the confidence to raise our adjusted earnings expectations for the full year," chief executive Robert S. McAnnally said in the second-quarter results release.

The cash wedge the pass-through famously creates has closed. At 30 June ONE Gas carried $19.8m of under-recovered gas costs against $22.4m over-recovered; the Winter Storm Uri bill was refinanced with ratepayer-backed securitized bonds, including $336m of Kansas bonds in November 2022. Its negative 3.7% trailing free-cash-flow yield is the capex program, not the molecule.

A fifth of New Jersey Resources has no customer bill behind it

New Jersey Resources owns New Jersey Natural Gas, with about 564,000 customers in six counties, and guides 21%-23% of fiscal 2026 net financial earnings to Energy Services, an unregulated wholesale gas book. That segment earned $84.5m in the nine months to June against $39.4m a year earlier, "primarily due to higher natural gas price volatility." The market reads it as a windfall: the forward earnings multiple of 15.1x sits above the trailing 14.3x because consensus has fiscal 2027 earnings per share at $3.44 against $3.58 this year.

Its September filings show who the real counterparty is. The utility cut customer bills 8.9%, about $158 a year, in the same month it asked to recover roughly $950m of system investment across a 7,500-mile network, a 12.7% increase if granted as filed. None of these three competes with the others for a single delivery customer; the rival is not another distributor but the ratepayer advocate arguing the bill is already high enough. ONE Gas describes the same discipline as holding average bills flat while it invests.

Southwest Gas is a pipeline build with a utility attached

Southwest Gas finished separating from Centuri, the utility-infrastructure contractor it owned, for about $1.35bn of net proceeds. The 62% revenue drop in 2025 is that deconsolidation, which also makes its 11.9x trailing multiple unusable. On forward earnings it is 20.0x, down from 28.2x in early May and still about a third richer than ONE Gas for rate-base growth of 9.5%-11.5% a year. Roughly $1.7bn of its $6.3bn five-year plan is the Great Basin 2028 expansion, now about $2.3bn in cost with $270m-$300m of expected incremental annual margin. Its year turns on an Arizona case seeking about $101m, a $5.18 monthly residential increase, plus Nevada and California proceedings. "We're encouraged by the progress our team made this quarter, advancing rate cases in all three states," chief executive Justin Brown said with the second-quarter results.

What repriced was the spread on a regulated dollar

No single session reset this group. First entries into falling trends ran from late May to mid-September, sixteen weeks apart, and the worst of the six sessions when all eight members fell together averaged 2.79%. Over that stretch the ten-year Treasury yield went from 4.639% on 25 August to 5.24% on 9 October and the Federal Reserve raised its target range to 3.75%-4.00% on 16 September, its first increase since 2023. Set 5.24% against ONE Gas's authorized returns of 9.4% to 9.8% and the gap an equity investor is paid for taking regulatory risk narrowed by about 60 basis points in six weeks without any commission writing a word. The selling reached the whole complex: the Utilities Select Sector SPDR fell 6% over the month to early October while the S&P 500 rose less than 1%.

So the likelier reading is a repricing of income, landing on businesses whose delivered margin never deteriorated. It explains the drip without conceding anything about the pipe, and it leaves the twelve-month comfort of the category average looking thin: New Jersey Resources, Northwest Natural and Southwest Gas carry that average, while five of the eight members sit below where they were a year ago.

ONE Gas reports its third quarter on 2 November, and the line to read is the one net of gas cost. Everything these companies earn comes from a commission agreeing that more pipe can go into rate base, and in Oklahoma, New Jersey and Arizona alike the household bill is now the ceiling on how much pipe that is.
