Workday Signed 200 Customers to Paid Agent Credits While 5,500 Use Its Agents Free

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.6

Workday's agents are in wide use and barely billed. The company disclosed roughly $600m of annual recurring revenue from artificial-intelligence products, up more than 200% year over year, and said AI drove more than $100m of new annual contract value in the July quarter — over a quarter of everything it sold. But the credit contracts that actually meter agent work were signed by a small fraction of the customers already running agents, and the chief financial officer put the money in fiscal 2028.

The escape from per-seat pricing is also less clean than it looks: the free credit allowance is banded by the customer's employee count, from 15,000 credits below 3,500 employees to 200,000 at 100,000 and above. Subscription revenue grew 13.9% last quarter and is guided to about 11% next year, while non-GAAP operating margin widened to 31.1% on a headcount that has not grown.

More than 5,500 Workday customers were running its artificial-intelligence agents at the end of July. Two hundred of them had signed a contract that charges for the work those agents do.

Workday, which sells cloud human-resources, payroll, financial-management and planning software to large enterprises and counts more than 65% of the Fortune 500 as customers, has always billed against a customer's employee count. That meter has stopped moving. ADP's same-store measure of employees on client payrolls grew 1% in its last fiscal year and is guided to zero-to-one this year, and American white-collar payrolls have contracted for 31 consecutive months, with the hiring rate at 3.4% in June. Seats are not being deleted so much as failing to arrive. Credits are the intended replacement meter, and they are not yet a replacement revenue line.

What the agents already earn

The monetization that exists is real and disclosed. Annual recurring revenue from AI products reached roughly $600m in the quarter ended July 31, up more than 200% from a year earlier and 20% sequentially. "AI products alone drove more than 100 million of new ACV, which accounted for more than 25% of all new ACV closed in the quarter," chief executive Aneel Bhusri told investors on the August 27 call, adding that it "is starting to lift our core business too, including our win rates." Subscription revenue grew 13.9% to $2.471bn on total revenue of $2.649bn, and about 60% of that growth came from expansion inside existing accounts. Named wins in the quarter included KPMG US, Danske Bank, Caterpillar, AstraZeneca and Novartis; BMO scaled a self-service agent from a 500-person pilot in May to 55,000 employees in June.

The allowance is still a headcount band

Bhusri's answer to the seat-substitution question is that the billing path no longer matters: "because all of it runs through FlexCredits, we monetize every action happening on our rails, regardless of the path." The published rate card charges one credit for an information retrieval, five for an autonomous task completed, six to screen a résumé against a job opening and 750 per requisition to mine existing talent pools — work performed rather than employees employed. But the free annual allotment that sits under that card is banded by employee count: 15,000 credits for a customer below 3,500 employees, rising to 200,000 for one at 100,000 or more. The SKU meant to break the link to headcount begins by measuring it.

That gap between use and payment is deliberate and guided. Chief financial officer Zane Rowe said the difference between 5,500 adopters and 200 credit contracts is already inside current backlog and revenue guidance, with AI becoming a significant part of incremental recurring revenue from fiscal 2028. Sana, the $1.1bn acquisition that closed in November, is being given away for a year through the Lighthouse program before it is sold. Paradox, bought last August, is worth about one point of the roughly 14-point backlog growth and anniversaries this quarter.

Margin from its own payroll

While it waits, Workday is buying profit with its own headcount. Non-GAAP operating margin reached 31.1% of revenue, against 29.0% a year earlier; GAAP operating margin went from 2.5% in fiscal 2024 to 10.7% in fiscal 2026. Employees stood at 20,900, flat, after Workday cut about 400 roles in February — 2% of staff, mostly in non-revenue customer operations — and took $135m of charges. Subscription growth ran 16.4% in fiscal 2025 and 13.1% in fiscal 2026, is guided to about 13% this year and roughly 11% next.

The shares closed at $204.72 on August 28, up 57.5% in three months, though roughly a third of that came in one August 14 session on a report that Silver Lake was in talks to take the company private at $43bn, with no deal announced; Elliott Management has disclosed a stake above $2bn. At 6.62x trailing price-to-gross-profit the stock has re-rated hard from 4.31x at the May trough and still sits well under roughly 9.2x a year ago; forward earnings of 18.5x against 41.6x trailing measure how much of the recovery is expected rather than reported.

So the business earns the profitability half of the advance and the AI order flow behind it; what nothing yet earns is the revenue line the credits are supposed to become, and the guide says as much by stepping growth down while margin steps up. Workday's own risk, as management framed it, is monetization slipping, not seats disappearing. SAP is making the same wager with outcome-based pricing, so the model shift is industry-wide.

The test arrives at Workday Rising in October, when Sana's conversational interface becomes the default screen every user lands on. Free credits will meet a workforce that isn't growing; the second number to watch is not how many companies use the agents, but how many pay past their allowance.

Adobe and Autodesk Both Delayed Charging for AI, Cutting Prices to Buy Usage

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.6

The two largest incumbents in design software were supposed to be the first to meter generative artificial intelligence. Both have instead deferred the bill. Adobe told investors in June it would absorb roughly $500m of second-half recurring revenue to push free tiers of Acrobat, Express and Firefly; its separately disclosed AI-first recurring revenue, a little over $500m, equals about 1.9% of a $27.10bn total. One line down the income statement the two diverge. Adobe's revenue has accelerated for four straight quarters, yet operating margin fell to 33.8% from 35.9% a year earlier. Autodesk grew revenue 16% with operating margin expanding to 29.3% — though a point and a half of that growth is an accounting reclassification management says stops next year. The cheaper of the two is the one whose operating leverage is running backwards.

Adobe told investors in June it would give up roughly $500m of second-half annualized recurring revenue to push free versions of Acrobat, Express and Firefly, and would defer planned price increases to do it. Ten weeks later Autodesk told a different set of investors it had cut the minimum purchase of its Flex consumption tokens from $300 to $99, and that while it remains committed to charging for application programming interfaces and machine-based usage, it has moved the timing out.

Both companies sell the software seats generative AI is most often said to replace — Adobe's Photoshop, Illustrator and Premiere to creative professionals and Acrobat to everyone else; Autodesk's AutoCAD and Revit to architects and builders, Fusion to manufacturers. The expectation was that they would defend those seats by metering AI on top of them. What the disclosure actually shows is two incumbents spending price to buy usage first. Adobe's creative freemium monthly active users went from more than 50m to more than 90m, and total monthly actives passed 850m. Its AI-first recurring revenue tripled year-on-year to surpass $500m — a different $500m from the one being given away, and still under two cents of every ARR dollar.

The mix line went away

Checking price against units at either company just got harder. Effective in the first quarter of fiscal 2026, Adobe combined Digital Media, Digital Experience and Publishing into a single reportable segment, retiring Digital Media ARR in favor of one total — $27.10bn, up 12.5%, of which about $480m arrived with the Semrush acquisition. Autodesk said on August 27 it will stop reporting design and make revenue, substituting construction, Fusion, operations and MaintainX. Each removed the line the question depended on.

Growth without leverage, and leverage without growth

Adobe's revenue rose 12.7% to $6.618bn in its April-quarter, the fourth straight acceleration. Operating income grew 6.1%, and margin fell to 33.8% from 35.9%; diluted earnings per share held at $4.25 mainly because the share count shrank 7.2%. Gross margin was flat at 89.2%, so the cost of generating images is not yet visible in cost of revenue. Meanwhile Canva has reached about $4bn of recurring revenue growing 35%, roughly 15% the size of Adobe's base.

Autodesk is the mirror image. Revenue grew 16.1% to $2.046bn with GAAP operating margin expanding to 29.3% from 25.5% and operating income up 33.1%. But billings grew 10%, slower than revenue; remaining performance obligations of $7.43bn were down 10% against the prior fiscal year end and deferred revenue down 9%, which the company attributes to reducing multi-year discounts. Contract durations are shortening. About 1.5 points of this year's revenue growth is the reseller-incentive reclassification under its new transaction model, and it does not recur next year. Net revenue retention sits slightly above the 100-110% range.

The construction number is the genuine one: growth north of 20% while the Architecture Billings Index stood at 46.6 in July, below the expansion line for nearly three and a half years. That is share taken, not a cycle. "The future of AI won't belong to the company with the best single model," chief executive Andrew Anagnost told investors on the August 27 call. "It will belong to the platform that combines the richest context with the right models to deliver the best outcomes for customers." Chief financial officer Janesh Moorjani said gross margins are flat this year but "will face compression from AI workloads over time."

What the shares priced

On August 27 the whole software complex re-rated on other companies' guidance — Salesforce, Okta and CrowdStrike all surged — and Adobe rose 6.3% and Autodesk 7.2% with no news of their own. That session supplied 61% of Adobe's thirty-day gain and more than all of Autodesk's. The next day Autodesk fell 3.6% after full-year earnings guidance of $12.52-12.60 straddled the $12.60 consensus. Over twelve months Adobe is down 17.6% and Autodesk 9.6%.

Adobe trades at 16.66x trailing and 11.95x forward earnings with a 9.2% free-cash-flow yield, against a five-year median trailing price-to-earnings near 42. Autodesk trades at 33.59x trailing and 20.73x forward — about 1.7x Adobe's forward multiple — on a 5.1% free-cash-flow yield.

The honest split: Autodesk's margin expansion and raised guidance are earned, minus the accounting point and a half; Adobe's move is arithmetic on cash flow from a deeply de-rated base, because nothing in its numbers yet shows AI paying for the seats it is meant to defend. Neither company's August gain was earned by either company.

Adobe's next quarter is the first to carry the freemium giveaway in full, and aggregators list it for September 10, though Adobe has not published the date itself. It will be presented by an interim finance chief: Dan Durn resigned four days after the June print and no permanent successor has been named.

"Scaling companies want real outcomes and predictable pricing when adopting AI, and we are evolving our product, pricing, and go-to-market to meet those needs," chief executive Yamini Rangan said in the results release. The offset is margin, and it is real

Klaviyo's meter is a different meter

Full-year guidance went up, to $1.526–1.534bn. The catch is the near term: the September-quarter guide of $377–381m implies 21.5–22.5% growth against 26.4% just reported. The two companies rarely bid for the same socket — inbound business-to-business versus Shopify-based retail — and their disclosures now say opposite things about expansion.

What the price is paying for

HubSpot is up 31% over three months. But through August 26 it was down 1.3% over the trailing thirty days; the entire 9.5% thirty-day gain arrived in two sessions, after Salesforce beat earnings estimates by 80% and rose 22% in its biggest session since 2020, dragging application software with it. Klaviyo's thirty-day gain was built the same way. HubSpot's price-to-gross-profit went from 4.20x to 4.65x across those two days with nothing company-specific in between; the same measure was 3.79x in late May and about 10.4x a year ago. The business earns part of this. Operating leverage of eight points of GAAP margin and a 3.7% share count reduction in one quarter are worth a higher multiple on a de-rated stock. What nothing in the June quarter earns is the re-rating that arrived on another company's earnings, into a unit meter management had just cut and a per-customer price line that moved 4%. Klaviyo's expansion is doing work; HubSpot's is not, and the two have been repriced as though they were the same instrument.

The honest test is a countable one. HubSpot has published the number it must beat — 5,000 to 6,000 net additions a quarter — and told investors why it will be low. If April's cheaper entry price and free agent trials were an investment rather than a leak, the September quarter is where it shows up.

DoubleVerify, the New York company that checks whether digital advertisements were seen by real people in safe places and bills its customers per impression measured, told investors on August 6 that its largest business had shrunk. Activation revenue — the pre-bid targeting layer that runs inside demand-side platforms — fell 1% year on year to $107.7m, while Measurement rose 6% to $66.8m and the supply-side unit rose 13% to $19.3m. The same morning it agreed to sell itself. Nielsen is paying $13.60 a share in cash, an enterprise value of about $2.15bn and a 30% premium to the prior sixty trading days' volume-weighted average, with closing expected by the first quarter of 2027. Volume grew, price fell

DoubleVerify itself supplied the arithmetic. In the first quarter, advertiser revenue rose 9% on 12% growth in measured media transactions, offset by a 4% decline in the fee per transaction. More impressions, less money each. A verifier's meter and a demand-side platform's percentage-of-spend meter both run on the price of the underlying impression, and connected-television inventory has been getting cheaper as supply expands.

That is why the other side of the trade offers no relief. The Trade Desk, whose platform buys media for agencies and is the biggest single home for DoubleVerify's Activation product, watched revenue growth decay from 17.7% to 14.3%, 11.8% and then 3.0% across four quarters, and has guided the September quarter to at least $650m — roughly 12% below a year earlier. "These numbers are not a reflection of our company or the long-term opportunity in front of us," chief executive Jeff Green told investors on the August 6 call. Management's position is that the take rate has been flat for a decade. The squeeze is arriving through what impressions cost: Amazon's demand-side platform charges nothing on its own inventory and has gone from under 10% to just under 20% of global programmatic share in about fifteen months, while connected-TV advertising overall grew 13% in 2025 to $26.6bn.

What the profit line was doing

DoubleVerify's earnings improved as its revenue stalled. Second-quarter operating income rose 70% to $23.0m and the operating margin nearly doubled to 11.9%; adjusted earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization reached $65.3m at a 34% margin. That is cost control. Full-year 2025 revenue of $748.3m grew 13.9% while operating income fell 3.9% — the second consecutive annual decline in operating profit before the revenue stall arrived.

Where growth exists, it is inside the walled gardens rather than the open web DoubleVerify was built to police: first-quarter social measurement revenue rose 23% and reached 49% of measurement revenue, and social activation rose 92% — off a Meta product with 87 advertisers and roughly $12m of annualized run rate.

What the two meters are worth

At $13.38 on August 28, DoubleVerify sits 1.6% under the cash consideration and has traded between $13.245 and $13.380 for fifteen sessions; the 37.2m shares that changed hands on August 7, against a normal one to three million, were the repricing. One of those two readings is wrong.

"Joining forces with DoubleVerify will extend our capabilities deeper into the digital media industry," Nielsen chief executive Karthik Rao said on August 6; DoubleVerify's Mark Zagorski called it "an exciting milestone".

When the vote closes, public investors lose their last direct read on what an advertising impression is actually worth per unit — leaving one listed meter on open-internet ad pricing, and it is guiding down.
