Moody's Leans on Bond-Deal Fees Twice as Heavily as S&P Global, and Those Fees Fell 5%

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Both rating agencies collect two kinds of money: a one-time fee set by someone else's deal size, and surveillance and subscription revenue that bills whether or not a bond prices. In the September quarter the one-time half shrank at Moody's, to $714m from $753m, while S&P Global held its equivalent roughly flat.

The difference is proportion. That transaction line is about 35% of Moody's group revenue and only 18% of S&P Global's, which also owns a $554m-a-quarter index business growing 20% on record exchange-traded fund assets that Moody's has no equivalent of.

Moody's reported 0.8% revenue growth, missed on adjusted earnings and only reaffirmed its outlook, yet trades at 30.2x forward earnings against S&P Global's 26.2x after two guidance raises. And the growth left in the fee meter comes from the AI borrowers investors spent October selling.

Both of the world's dominant rating agencies collected less for the average new bond deal this autumn than a year earlier, and the September quarter's real disclosure is how unequally that matters to each company. Moody's booked $714m of transaction revenue at Moody's Investors Service, which rates corporate, government and structured debt, down 5% from $753m as rated issuance fell 2%. S&P Global's comparable Ratings line was $677m, up 1% even though billed issuance rose 8%.

The month's lift in these two shares has been read as a bet on a refinancing wave arriving with cheaper money. That reading does not survive the calendar: the Federal Open Market Committee held its target at 3.75-4.00% on 28 October, with two members dissenting in favour of a hike. Nor was the move confined to ratings. Over the same 30 sessions the data and exchange shelf re-rated together — Morningstar up 20.0%, Nasdaq 16.6%, MSCI 15.9%, S&P Global 13.4% and Moody's last at 8.0% — while an S&P 500 index fund fell 2.1%, as money left technology for defensive quality.

The fee that shrank, and how much of each company it is

Transaction fees are a meter on other people's deals. Moody's meter fell faster than volume because of mix: investment-grade revenue rose 18% on 16% issuance growth in a record third quarter, while high yield fell 40% and bank loans fell 27%. The other 65% of Moody's bills regardless. Moody's Analytics, the research, scores and know-your-customer software arm, carries annualized recurring revenue of $3.7bn, up 8%, with 95% retention. "Adjusted operating margin was 35.3%, and that is up 100 basis points year-over-year," chief financial officer Noémie Heuland told investors on 21 October.

S&P Global's recurring half is a different animal. S&P Dow Jones Indices produced $554m of revenue, up 20% on record exchange-traded fund assets of $6.5tn, with asset-linked fees up 24% and a 71.9% operating margin. Moody's recurring book is subscription; S&P Global's is partly a claim on asset values, and an extended Cboe index licence running to 2051 is expected to add two to three points of index growth in 2027.

The marginal borrower is now a data center

"AI-related issuance accounted for a little over half of total issuance growth," chief executive Rob Fauber said on Moody's 21 October call, putting rated AI capital spending at about $800bn for 2026 and north of $1tn next year. S&P Global disclosed roughly $240bn of hyperscaler issuance year to date and calls itself the agency of choice for it. Defensive buyers, in other words, have been bidding up a toll booth whose growth increasingly depends on the capital expenditure cycle they were fleeing.

Beneath that, the older business is leaking. S&P Global's own researchers report that private credit has out-lent the syndicated loan market to borrowers rated B- and below for four consecutive years, reaching nearly $146bn in 2025 — loans that pay no issue fee. PitchBook expects US private credit issuance to fall about 15% to $120bn in 2026, so the leak is wide rather than widening, and Moody's is selling into it, rating business development companies and pairing its credit models with private-credit data through a partnership with Allvue.

One quarter earned its re-rating

S&P Global's results account for its move. Organic revenue grew 7%, operating margin reached 53.2% against 43.1%, and management raised full-year guidance for a second time, to adjusted earnings of $17.70-$17.95 a share, lifting the Ratings outlook to 6.5-8% growth. It also opened a strategic review of Capital IQ and associated data businesses. At 26.2x forward earnings the shares sit mid-range against 21.8x at February's trough and 30.5x at January's peak.

Moody's results do not. Revenue grew 0.8%, operating margin slipped to 44.8% from 45.7%, adjusted earnings of $4.14 a share missed the $4.25 expected, and guidance of $16.50-$17.00 was reaffirmed rather than raised. The shares nonetheless went from 28.0x forward earnings a month ago to 30.2x, a four-turn premium to the faster-growing company. What Moody's bought was 2.2 turns of multiple on an unchanged outlook, plus first-time rating mandates up 40%, which Fauber called the highest quarterly total in five years.

Both companies then told investors the meter slows from here: Moody's guided fourth-quarter issuance to a mid-teens percentage decline, S&P Global to a slight fall in transaction revenue. The next year of growth in the oldest fee in finance is underwritten by the spending plans of a handful of companies building data centers, and those plans are revised quarterly.

Chipmakers are handing a larger share of their equipment budgets to the companies that verify the parts work, and the September quarter is where that shift turned into revenue. "The portion of semi CapEx associated with test is increasing as well," Teradyne chief executive Gregory S. Smith told investors on October 28. "From 6.4% in 2025 to 8.1% for the first 8 months of 2026."

That is the mechanism the back-end shelf has been priced on all year, and it was read this autumn as a capex-timing trade: when the duration of the hyperscaler build came into question, the test names' order books were supposed to pause with it. What the September filings disclose instead is more content per tested die, more consumable revenue, and in one case a supplier that has run out of factory. Teradyne fell 28.5% over the 30 sessions to November 12, FormFactor 26.9% and Aehr 19.0%, and the dates of those declines sit outside the three companies' own disclosures.

Where the extra capex points landed

Teradyne, which sells the automated testers that verify chips at wafer and packaged stages, did $1,305.95m of revenue in the quarter ended September 27, up 69.8%, with operating income up 176.6% to $402.0m. Semiconductor Test was $1,072m of that, or 82% of the company, so the group number is a test read; the Universal Robots cobot business contributed $96m, grew 28% and turned profitable, and is too small to move either way. Memory test set a record at $301m, a fourth consecutive quarter above $200m on high-bandwidth memory and DRAM, with a NAND resurgence behind it.

The one soft line is the fourth quarter: $1.25bn to $1.35bn of revenue, flat sequentially at the midpoint, with gross margin guided to 57.5–58.5% against 59.1% delivered. Secondary coverage ties the post-print fall to that margin guide rather than to the revenue line, which came in above consensus.

A probe card is bought by the wafer, and FormFactor has no floor space left

FormFactor sells probe cards, the consumable interface that touches the wafer at test, and its quarter was its fourth straight record: $280.5m of revenue, up 38.4%, with gross profit up 90.7% and GAAP gross margin at 54.7% against 39.7% a year earlier. Its 10-Q attributes roughly 75% of the year-over-year increase in DRAM product revenue to high-bandwidth memory designs, even though mix within the quarter tilted toward DDR5 as customers chased a DDR5 contract price that went from $8.70 a 16Gb chip in October 2025 to about $48 in September 2026.

"We delivered again record DRAM revenue and at the same time, delivered record or near record company gross margins," chief executive Mike Slessor said on October 28. "That's something you would have not seen from the FormFactor of the past." The fourth-quarter guide of $285m plus or minus $10m is about 1.6% sequential growth, which management puts down to existing capacity being at its limit. Chief financial officer Aric McKinnis set the release date on that constraint: "Exiting 2027, we expect Farmers Branch will have the capability to produce about $400 million in annual probe card revenue."

Teradyne has bought into the bottleneck. It agreed to invest $300m of equity in FormFactor alongside a multi-year collaboration on probe and interface technology for high-performance compute, advanced packaging, high-bandwidth memory and silicon photonics, expected to close in the fourth quarter. FormFactor frames it as an open-ecosystem effort and keeps its existing partnership with Advantest.

Aehr's blades outgrew its razors

Aehr sells wafer-level and package-level burn-in systems with WaferPak contactors attached. Revenue was a record $33.0m, up 200.9%, against quarters of $11.0m, $9.9m, $10.3m and $19.8m through fiscal 2026. Consumables were $13.5m, 41% of the quarter and more than five times the year-ago level, and are guided to about 35% of fiscal 2027 against 29–30% last year. Bookings of $46.7m were 1.4x revenue; effective backlog reached $109.3m as of November 1 from $80.6m at fiscal year-end, and the company raised its full-year outlook to $150m. Chief executive Gayn Erickson gave the per-die arithmetic on November 2: "For every million units shipped in a calendar year... you would need somewhere between 5 and 10 of our systems per million units per year... It's $35 million to $70 million for each million units." NAND burn-in, he said, is moving more slowly on limited new fab capacity.

Aehr's trend turned down on October 21, six sessions before it reported. The shares rose 26.5% the day after the print, to $65.11, then gave all of it back by November 5.

What the books account for, and what they do not

None of it. The markdown is uniform across the back-end: Camtek down 26.9%, Advantest 21.5%, ASM Pacific 33.1%, Cohu 29.2%, Lam Research 22.1% over the same 30 days. Advantest, Teradyne's direct rival in both SoC and memory test, reported record September results and raised full-year guidance and fell with the rest. Two dated sessions carry most of it: October 22, when Lam's supply-constrained December guide took the PHLX Semiconductor Index down more than 6%, and November 5, when semiconductor shares plunged on fears about the AI trade and all three protagonists fell about 15% in one day. What is being repriced is the duration of the data-center build, not this quarter's wafer starts.

The repricing does not land evenly. Teradyne at 13.9x trailing gross profit, against 25.2x in May 2026, and FormFactor at 14.9x against 34.4x, have each had roughly half their multiple removed while gross profit grew 72% and 91%. Aehr at 67.6x trailing gross profit has fallen 57.9% from its peak and still carries the dearest valuation of the three; measured against its own raised $150m guide, the $2.0bn market capitalization is nearer 13x sales. The caveat on Aehr belongs with it: 138 employees, a lumpy book, and a $22m follow-on order from one lead AI-processor customer doing much of the work.

Smith was blunt about why the old framework no longer reads this business. "The patterns that we saw up through 2024 are over," he said on October 28. "There has been a seismic shift in the semiconductor test market that started in 2024 and really took hold in 2025." Teradyne can now see more than two quarters out, against a historical thirteen weeks of visibility. The market spent this month deciding it would rather not look that far.

Mobileye Global, which sells the EyeQ vision chips and higher-content SuperVision and Chauffeur driving stacks that let a car see, shipped 9.5 million of those chips in the September quarter and was paid about a dollar less for each of them than a year earlier. A dollar off a fifty-dollar chip

Unit volumes are not the problem. Management put the full-2026 decline at about $2 and expects 2027 pricing to be "more flat." Per-unit deflation is the perception layer's condition this year, not Mobileye's alone. Hesai shipped 65.3% more lidars in its own September quarter for 34.3% more revenue, which works out to about 19% less revenue per unit.

The content ladder is real and it is small

Nextpower, the Fremont company formerly called Nextracker that sells the steel, actuators and control software pointing utility-scale solar panels at the sun, reported record revenue of $1.11bn in the September quarter and a thinner gross margin than a year earlier. The reason those two facts sit together is an accounting line: the federal advanced-manufacturing credit for US-made components, which both big American tracker makers subtract from cost of sales, where a tax subsidy becomes indistinguishable from a better price.

The five-session leap in the tracker shares in early November was read as the cycle turning. The September-quarter filings say something narrower. Order books are growing in dollars at both companies, the price of a tracker per watt is still falling, and the margin line that would normally settle the argument is partly a function of who collected a credit and who wrote one off.

$192.3m that never came from a price

Nextpower recognized roughly $192.3m from the Section 45X credit as a reduction to cost of sales in the first half of fiscal 2027, worth 9.4 points of the half's 33.3% gross margin and about 28% of its $681.2m of first-half gross profit, according to its results filing. Strip it out and the half runs near 24%. The quarter-level disclosure is coarser still: about $99m of credits, vendor rebates and tariffs, net, with the subsidy and the tariff drag inside one number.

Array booked the same credit backwards

Array Technologies, the Albuquerque maker of single-axis trackers and SmarTrack control software, took the mirror image. Fair Isaac closed its fiscal year on 30 September with what its chief executive called the most successful twelve months in the company's history, and then declined to tell investors what the next twelve would look like. On the 4 November call the company withheld fiscal-2027 revenue and earnings guidance altogether, citing mortgage pricing programs still being finalized with lenders, elevated rates suppressing volumes, and a fiscal year that begins in October while mortgage score pricing resets in January.

Fair Isaac licenses the FICO credit score, which lenders buy through the three bureaus and their resellers, and sells the FICO Platform decisioning software to banks. The market had treated the first business as a toll whose price the company sets at will. The year just filed shows that the toll was the entire growth story — and that its price is now fixed.

Seventy-two percent more mortgage revenue on fewer pulls

The fourth-quarter release states the mechanism in the company's own words: business-to-business Scores revenue rose 39% "primarily attributable to a higher mortgage origination scores unit price, partially offset by a decline in mortgage originations volume." Mortgage origination revenue grew 72% in the quarter while score volumes fell high single digits. Auto originations added 11% and card and personal loan 12%. Scores revenue of $1.652bn for the year was 65% of the $2.535bn group total, up from roughly 59% a year earlier.

The price behind that did roughly double, from $4.95 a mortgage score to $10. Under the FICO Preferred Program launched in October, it holds flat at $10 for 2027 — available to lenders and brokers buying tri-merge reports through resellers that commit to pulling FICO on 100% of applications. That is pull-through insurance bought with price certainty. Mizuho's Sean Kennedy cut his target to $986 from $1,063 on flat mortgage score pricing in 2027.

The separate Mortgage Direct License Program routes around the bureaus, letting resellers calculate and distribute scores themselves; FICO has signed resellers representing about 73% of mortgage reseller volume, including Xactus. Its Performance Model — a $4.95 royalty plus a $33 fee per borrower when the loan closes — remains pending certification by the government-sponsored enterprises, so the half of the economics tied to closings is not usable in the conforming market.

Someone else sets the units

The Federal Housing Finance Agency directed Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac to price loans on a single adjustment grid whether scored with Classic FICO or VantageScore 4.0, removing the 20-point penalty previously applied to the rival. TransUnion extended $0.99 VantageScore pricing through 2028 and counts more than 1,100 mortgage lenders using it. And the Mortgage Bankers Association expects 5.0 million loans in 2027, down 5.6%.

Chief executive William Lansing's answer is the newer model. "10T is the best score available," he said on 4 November. "All the trend and momentum is in the direction of 10T adoption, and I think 2027 will be a big year for 10T." Eighty-four lenders representing $684bn of eligible annual originations have joined its adopter program, and the Federal Housing Administration will accept it from 1 January 2027. Conforming certification is still outstanding.

The software book is compounding underneath

Software annual recurring revenue reached $851m, up 14%, with platform ARR of $453m up 72%. "Our dollar-based net retention rate in the quarter was 112%," chief financial officer Steven Weber told investors. "Platform NRR was 156%." The non-platform book retained 82%, shrinking at an 18% annual rate, and segment operating margin reached 61% from 55%.

The shares are down 59.7% over twelve months. The two worst sessions were regulatory, not operational: down 29.2% on 29 September on the FHFA grid decision and TransUnion's pricing, and 13.2% on 26 October when the flat $10 was announced. The 4 November print itself moved them 3.2%.

What the compression is actually paying for

Fiscal 2026 contradicts the markdown on reported numbers — revenue up 27.3%, gross margin from 82.2% to 86.1%, free cash flow of $1.034bn — and supports it on forward ones. Consensus has fiscal-2027 revenue growing 2.3% and net income falling 3.7%, with earnings per share still up 4.0% because the diluted count fell 13.2% in two years on $3.028bn of buybacks, now paused for term-loan repayment at 3.29x leverage. At the 12 December 2025 peak of $1,844.98 the shares carried roughly 69x trailing earnings; today's $717.38 is 19.7x, and 7.0x trailing gross profit against about 27x then. The forward 16.2x rests on an estimate cut from $49.65 in mid-October to $44.35 — a denominator the company itself will not stand behind.

Lansing was explicit about why. "We were attacked because we're transparent and clear about our score price," he said on 4 November. "We're not going to share what the prices are." The toll becomes a private matter between Fair Isaac and the resellers, and the only public read on it will be a revenue line whose two drivers are now pulling in opposite directions.

BWX Technologies builds the reactors, nuclear fuel and missile launch tubes that the US Navy's propulsion program buys from it as sole source, and in the September quarter it widened operating margin to 13.86% from 10.64% a year earlier while total backlog reached $8,556m, up from $8,398m three months before. The shares closed at $124.87 on 12 November, down 39.6% from six months earlier and 47.6% below their April peak.

That decline arrived in step with Oklo, down 66.1% over the same six months, and NuScale, down 57.7%. The market has treated listed nuclear as a single expression of the AI-power trade, and the late-October trigger was financial rather than nuclear: Meta's raised capital-expenditure forecast reignited concern about data-center borrowing, sending Centrus, Oklo, NuScale and BWX Technologies to 52-week lows together. What separates them is whether anyone has signed anything.

The backlog rose through every month of the decline

BWXT's Government Operations segment grew revenue 4% to $639m in the quarter with operating income up 26% to $122.6m; Commercial Operations revenue rose 25%, 15% of it organic. A $189m annual naval fuel contract was booked. At the 2 November report the company raised the low end of 2026 free-cash-flow guidance to $350-360m and tightened non-GAAP earnings guidance to $4.72-$4.77 a share.

The de-rating is in the multiple alone. BWXT trades at 26.2x forward earnings against roughly 46x forward in May 2026, when the trailing multiple was 54.97x against 30.4x now, and consensus has 2026 earnings growing 24.4% before decelerating to about 10% in 2027.

Oklo's customer book is letters of intent, and its regulator is the Energy Department

Oklo's 10-Q for the nine months to 30 September records total revenue of $3.07m and describes non-binding letters of intent with Equinix and other customers that it is still working to convert into binding power purchase agreements. The 1.2 GW Ohio campus agreement with Meta, 900 MW of it under grid study, is not binding either. The company has not tendered a combined licence application to the Nuclear Regulatory Commission; its 2020 filing was dismissed without prejudice in January 2022, and it is building under Energy Department authorisation instead. First power at Aurora-Idaho slipped to 2028-2029 from 2027-2028.

Meanwhile operating cash burn rose 127% year on year to $110.9m for the nine months, and diluted shares rose 25.4%. "We have everything we need, and that's an incredible position to be in and something that hasn't been very present in the United States for decades," chief executive Jake DeWitte told investors on 6 November. He is right about the cash: $3.1bn, or $16.44 per diluted share against a $24.94 close, roughly five years at guided burn and capital spend.

NuScale has the approved design and no buyer of record

NuScale's quarterly revenue fell 96.6% to $0.281m and it has still sold no power module. Its commercial route runs through ENTRA1, which holds global exclusive rights to deploy the plants and whose September 2025 Tennessee Valley Authority agreement for up to 6 GW is non-binding; shareholder class actions followed a research report characterising ENTRA1 as thinly staffed. Management's own sequence puts the power purchase agreement first and module revenue last. "We are no longer simply designing and planning, we are manufacturing," chief executive John Hopkins said on 5 November of first-article work with Doosan Enerbility, which sits on the balance sheet as inventory recoverable only through a future equipment contract. Diluted shares rose 39.0%; liquidity is $1.9bn against about $65m of quarterly operating expense, and the stock trades at 0.99x book.

For the two developers the results account for the move. Nominal revenue, heavy dilution and a start-up date pushed a year later are the repricing of announcements into deliveries, and neither is a solvency story. For BWXT nothing in the disclosures moved that way; the likelier reading is rotation out of defence and nuclear multiples that had reached levels earnings could not hold.

One event would separate the three again. "I said at the beginning of this year that I expected us to have new orders for U.S. reactor plant builds in -- by the end of this year," chief executive Rex Geveden said on 2 November. "And I still think that's a possibility for us, but certainly no later than about the first half of the next year." A signed build order lands on the one company here already paid for work it has done.

The money reaching federal technology contractors this autumn is arriving on contracts they already hold. Leidos, which integrates national-security systems for the Pentagon, runs air-traffic systems for the Federal Aviation Administration and manages federal health programs, booked $5.5bn of net orders in its September quarter. Its funded backlog — the slice of contract value for which Congress has actually released cash — rose 22%, to $11.1bn. The total backlog those orders sit inside grew 4%.

That gap between funded and total is the autumn's real finding, and it has been read as something else. Shares in the group rose over the past 30 days: SAIC 12.7%, Leidos 8.9%, CACI 5.7%. A single session on 9 October carried much of it, and it was confined to the services names — Leidos up 6.7%, SAIC 7.2%, Booz Allen 7.0% — while the hardware primes General Dynamics and Northrop Grumman moved 1.4% and 0.2%. No company-specific catalyst for that day was discoverable; the likelier reading is rotation into the cheapest contractor valuations.

Seventy-two days of money that starts nothing

Agencies have been funded since 1 October by a continuing resolution running to 11 December at largely prior-year levels, which holds the Pentagon at roughly $838.5bn against the $1.5 trillion it requested for fiscal 2027. A stopgap increments programs already running. It does not start new ones. What the funded-backlog growth records, then, is incumbency money rather than an awards recovery — and CACI's management said as much, calling the award pace sluggish and naming the December deadline as a headwind inside guidance.

CACI's margin arrived with an acquisition attached

CACI, which builds signals-intelligence, cyber and electronic-warfare systems for defense and intelligence agencies, had the loudest quarter. "We won $4.8 billion in awards, producing a 2x book to bill for the quarter," chief executive John Mengucci told investors on 22 October. Chief financial officer Jeffrey MacLauchlan added that "funded backlog increased by 15% year over year… the seventh time in the last 8 quarters we have delivered double digit year over year growth in funded backlog." Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization reached 13.3% of revenue against 11.7%, and firm-fixed-price work — where a productivity gain stays with the vendor — rose to 33% of revenue from 27%, about half of that from the ARKA acquisition. Revenue grew 8.2%; organic growth was 2.1%. The shares carry 16.65x trailing EV/EBITDA, the dearest of the three, on 3.5x pro-forma leverage, and a trailing price/earnings ratio of 26.2x that is unchanged from a year ago.

Leidos is cheap for a reason with a date on it

Leidos raised full-year adjusted earnings guidance to $12.50-$12.70 from $12.20-$12.50, the third consecutive quarter of increases, and its shares are still down 32% over twelve months while trailing earnings grew. The overhang is one contract: the Health segment shrank 12%, to $1.07bn, on volumes tied to the Veterans Benefits Administration disability-examination program, now in recompete. "We believe it's highly likely our existing contracts will be extended through the first half of 2027, in line with the customers' planned new contract start date of July 1, 2027," chief executive Thomas Bell said on 3 November; a customer decision is expected around April or May. At 11.6x trailing and 10.4x forward earnings, against roughly 18x trailing a year ago, that single recompete is most of the de-rating.

SAIC's order book never joined the recovery

SAIC sells engineering, information-technology modernization and systems sustainment almost entirely to US government customers, and it is the one name where the awards story does not hold. Net bookings of about $1.2bn in the July quarter produced a 0.6x book-to-bill, with 0.8x over the trailing twelve months, against Leidos's 1.1x. It lost the RITS enterprise-information-technology recompete, worth about 350 basis points of second-half revenue, and the $1.4bn CASTLE-NET order went to Accenture Federal Services. "Our recompete win rate of over 90% this quarter creates an easier path to on-contract growth," chief executive James Reagan told investors on 31 August — a defense of retention, which is a different thing from winning new sockets. Reported revenue did inflect, up 6.3% in the quarter, but net income fell 19.7%.

The funding went one way and the valuations went another

The quarters account for the direction of the group and almost none of its ranking. Where Congress released cash — Leidos and CACI — the business improved measurably, and where the order book stayed under 1.0x, it did not. The market inverted that. SAIC's shares have gained 65% over twelve months, nearly all of it a move from about 11.6x trailing earnings to 17.3x, while consensus models fiscal 2027 revenue of $7.30bn against $7.26bn, growth of 0.6%. CACI, improving fastest in absolute terms, is priced for it: its 19.1x forward multiple needs roughly 40% earnings growth off a 2.1% organic base. Leidos, with the best conversion of funded dollars into revenue in the group, is the cheapest, because its problem has a hearing date rather than an open-ended one.

SAIC reports its third quarter around 3 December, a date the company has not confirmed. Whatever that print says about bookings, it lands eight days before the money underneath the whole group stops.

Chairman and chief executive Brett Cope said the quarter "elevated our backlog to over $2 billion for the first time in our 79-year history." Gross margin was 30.57%, versus 30.70% a year earlier. That is the mechanism in one line: a record order book and no incremental margin, because the margin was set at signing. The $2.4bn is roughly 2.1 years of revenue at the trailing rate. Powell trades at 20.8x fiscal 2027 consensus earnings and 27.2x trailing, against the 35x forward and 58x trailing flagged in May.

Vintage, not volume, at GE Vernova

GE Vernova's third-quarter orders rose 96% organically to $29bn and backlog reached $197bn, with Electrification equipment backlog at $42bn, up 60%, and segment margin up 470 basis points to 19.1%. The company attributes the improvement to "favorable price and disciplined underwriting" as older work rolls off. Yet its Electrification orders themselves fell 9% organically to $5.4bn against a prior-year synchronous-condenser comparison, with book-to-bill near 1.4x, and chief executive Scott Strazik told investors on 28 October that gas turbine orders and slot reservations have slowed while the company takes reservations for 2032 delivery. Revenue growth accelerated across the year to 27%; the shares fell 18% in 30 days. On 2027 consensus earnings the multiple is about 31.9x, against the roughly 50x Rajiv Jain called unsustainable in July.

All three peaked on 3 November and gave up most of the decline in the two sessions of 4-5 November. Over twelve months GE Vernova is still up 43% and Powell 21%, while nVent has given back its year at up 1.9%. Eaton fell 14.8% over the same 30 days and Hubbell 5.3%, so the three carried the group's move.

The quarters account for one of the three declines. nVent's own disclosures show the deceleration its risk list anticipated, and its short book means a 2028 slowdown reaches it fast. For Powell and GE Vernova the next several quarters of revenue are already contracted at prices set before any of this, and the physical queue has not loosened: large power transformers still run 30 to 36 months and 38kV switchgear 78 to 104 weeks, with roughly $1.8bn of announced North American transformer capacity not operating until 2028. The exposure the ballot measures created and the cash SoftBank wrote off belong to the same window, the one past the contracted book.

Powell's September-quarter numbers do not exist yet. The company said on 3 November it would report fiscal fourth-quarter results after the close on 16 November, which makes it the first of the three to describe an order book assembled before the vote to a market that has already decided what the vote meant.

Building AI Racks Pays Sanmina Better Than Designing Them Pays Celestica

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Assembling racks for the AI buildout is supposed to be thin work: the customer's silicon, memory and power gear book as the builder's own revenue at little markup, so the top line multiplies while gross profit barely moves. On the latest quarters that describes one of these companies and not the other two.

Sanmina, which paid Advanced Micro Devices $2.55bn for ZT Systems' factories, lifted gross margin to 11.3% of revenue from 9.1% as rack volume scaled, and guided fiscal 2027 revenue to $17.5-19.5bn. Celestica, which designs what it builds, saw gross margin fall to 11.0% from 13.0% on 82.5% revenue growth, and discloses three customers at 66% of revenue combined. Jabil's gross line was flat.

The contract manufacturer that bought Advanced Micro Devices' server-assembly plants reported on 9 November that its heaviest quarter of rack output was also its richest. Sanmina, a San Jose builder of electronics for industrial, medical, defense, telecom and cloud customers, converted 11.3% of revenue into gross profit in the quarter ended 3 October, against 9.1% a year earlier, while revenue grew 84%.

That is not how assembly work for the AI buildout is supposed to behave. A manufacturing contract books the customer's bought-in processors, memory and power equipment as the vendor's own revenue at thin or no markup, so revenue per rack can multiply while gross profit per rack barely stirs. Memory makes the inflation vivid: conventional DRAM contract prices rose roughly 90-95% in the first quarter of 2026 as makers shifted wafers to high-bandwidth memory for accelerators. Each rack shipped now carries a far larger bill of materials. The mechanism is real; it simply does not describe every company doing the work.

Sanmina bought the build and left the design with AMD

Sanmina paid AMD $2.55bn in cash for ZT Systems' manufacturing arm while the chipmaker kept the design business, so it competes for build volume rather than design content. Those plants contributed $884m of the $3.08bn Integrated Manufacturing Solutions segment last quarter, while the legacy factories in that segment grew 13.8% to $2.2bn. Derived from the reported lines, each incremental revenue dollar carried about 13.9 cents of gross profit, above the company's own 11.3% average, and still above it after stripping a disclosed one-time $30m tariff recovery.

"Non-GAAP operating margin expanded from 5.7% operating margin to 7.4% operating margin year-over-year," chairman and chief executive Jure Sola told investors on 9 November, on fiscal 2026 revenue of $14.53bn. Management then guided fiscal 2027 revenue to $17.5-19.5bn with operating margin of 7.0-7.5%, flat at the midpoint against what it just delivered. The gross line is rising; the line below it is guided sideways.

Celestica designs the racks and keeps less of each new dollar

Celestica, the Toronto company that designs and builds switches, optical interconnect, servers and storage for hyperscalers, grew revenue 82.5% to $5.83bn in its September quarter and saw gross margin fall to 11.0% from 13.0%. The incremental dollar carried 8.5 cents of gross profit, well under the average dollar. Hardware Platform Solutions, the design-led line, reached $2.3bn and grew 70%, slower than the 102% growth of the segment containing it, so design content is shrinking as a share of mix even while it expands.

Management guides 2027 gross margins "in the low 11s" with operating margin of 9.0% reached through operating leverage, which concedes that the new revenue arrives at roughly today's gross margin. Celestica's 10-Q for the quarter discloses three customers at 35%, 17% and 14% of revenue. It cut 2026 free-cash-flow guidance to $460m from $600m, set 2027 capital spending at $1.5-2.0bn, and told investors 2027 would be a net use of cash.

The split has an arithmetic cause. Rack work landing near 11% of revenue dilutes a book that was earning 13% and lifts one that troughed at 7.6% in the December 2025 quarter.

Jabil moved the line below the gross line

Jabil's August quarter held gross margin at 9.4% against 9.5%, while operating margin expanded to 5.7% from 4.1%. It guides fiscal 2027 revenue of $44.5bn with AI-related revenue of $22.1bn, up from $14.4bn, and roughly $18.9bn of the group, about 42%, sitting in automotive, healthcare, energy and devices. "Most of our AI business supports everyday AI usage rather than frontier model training," Matt Crowley, president of its Intelligent Infrastructure segment, said on 30 September. Inventory days ran at 64 against a 55-60 target, the one disclosed figure in the group supporting the pre-bought-material story.

The markdown did not read any of this

Over the 30 days to 12 November the six-name group fell between 15.5% and 28.1%, and the deepest fall belonged to Flex, whose own 10-Q reported gross margin up 60 basis points to 9.6%. The 5 November session was the biggest technology selloff since the Covid crash, two days after voters backed candidates and measures curbing US data-center construction; Celestica fell 12.66% that day with no company announcement, its most recent update having beaten and raised. All three have rebounded since, Sanmina 22.9% and Celestica 22.8% in five sessions.

So the quarters account for almost none of the move. On the same forward year, Sanmina trades at 9.5 times consensus earnings, Celestica at 11.6 and Jabil at 14.6, and each dollar of Sanmina's trailing gross profit costs $6.15 against $15.25 at Celestica, down from roughly $26 in May. The only disclosed deterioration anywhere in the group is Celestica's gross margin and its cash guidance, and that company is the one the market had re-rated hardest first. Where the business actually improved, the repricing was indiscriminate.

Sanmina's flagship program is barely under way. "We've now begun to ship the Helios product, albeit in small quantities," chief financial officer Jonathan Faust said on 9 November, of the AMD rack platform the whole acquisition was built around. The margin it reported last quarter was earned before that volume arrived.
