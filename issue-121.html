So the two share a mechanism and split on direction: Dell guides group operating margin up about 350 basis points; HPE guides its server segment down.

Dell's October quarter landed after the close on 24 November, a day behind Hewlett Packard Enterprise's, and between them the two prints answer the question the enterprise server shelf has been marked down on: who pays for memory. Dell's Infrastructure Solutions Group, which sells its rack, blade and hyperscale servers alongside storage, earned an operating margin of 17% of segment revenue on $42bn of revenue, up more than four percentage points from a year earlier, disclosed in the 8-K filed that day.

Going in, the quarter was read as the reverse story. Dell, HPE, Lenovo and Super Micro book multi-quarter artificial-intelligence server orders at prices agreed before enterprise memory costs went vertical, and the shares have been priced as though that increase lands on the vendor. What the two American vendors disclosed this week is that it is largely landing on the buyer. Only one of them expects that to get harder next year.

Memory is now most of the box

Memory has passed half of a server's bill of materials, with cumulative memory cost inflation above 400% since early 2024. DigiTimes reported on 23 November that Dell, HPE, Lenovo and Supermicro are demanding 25% to 45% more at delivery than the prices written into signed contracts. The repricing is contested rather than contractual: XTX Markets sued Dell in Ireland in June over $62-70m of charges sought above an agreed $549m server order.

The supply reason is that the capacity which would make server memory modules is making stacks for accelerators instead. TrendForce puts high-bandwidth memory at 23% of all DRAM wafer capacity in 2026 and close to 30% in 2027, with conventional DRAM contract prices projected up 10-15% in the fourth quarter.

Dell repriced faster than its inputs rose

Dell's revenue doubled to $54.9bn and gross margin came in at 21.04%, a touch above the 20.71% of a year earlier and the recovery leg of a curve that bottomed at 17.75% in the April quarter. Operating income rose 251% on revenue up 103%. Backlog closed at a record $132.3bn, which chief financial officer David Kennedy told investors on 24 November "has been fully vetted through our rigorous due diligence process."

"Infrastructure demand continues to outpace supply," chief executive Jeffrey Clarke said on the same call. "As we look into next year, the gap between component supply and end-user demand continues to widen." Dell raised fiscal 2027 guidance to $202bn of revenue and $29.68 of non-GAAP earnings per share, with operating margin guided up roughly 350 basis points on an AI server mix three times the prior year's.

The 14% that moved HPE

HPE's fourth quarter, reported on 23 November, lifted gross margin to 38.0% from 30.4% and produced an 18.4% operating margin in Cloud & AI, the segment housing its ProLiant servers, on segment revenue up 69%. For fiscal 2027 management guided that segment to about 14%, citing this year's steep price increases moderating, a more dilutive AI mix weighted to the back half, and component costs still rising. Chief financial officer Marie Myers was reported as telling the call that costs were rising faster than pricing.

This is not the Juniper integration problem. Networking segment margin was guided to expand into the mid-to-high 20s, and the acquisition's annualized cost synergy target was raised to $800m from $450m.

Subscription revenue is not large enough to carry the mix either: annual recurring revenue reached $3.5bn, up 12%, against company revenue up 50% in the quarter. The warning is about parts, with memory and NAND shortages expected to restrict fulfilment until at least the second half of 2028 and lead times of 28 to 40-plus weeks.

What the prints explain, and what they don't

HPE fell 15.6% on 24 November. Dell fell about 12% during that session, before its own results, then recovered roughly 4.8% in after-hours trading once the backlog was out. Over thirty days Dell is down 35% and HPE 28% against 5% for the S&P 500 tracker, with Super Micro down 32% over the same stretch; both Dell and HPE remain well above where they traded a year ago.

One guided number does real work here, and it is HPE's. Four points of margin coming out of the segment that produces most of its revenue is a reason to pay less, and consensus still models group operating margin near 17.6% for fiscal 2027, above what management guided for Cloud & AI. Dell's de-rating has no such anchor in its own disclosure: every reported margin line expanded, guidance went up, and the board added $25bn to the repurchase authorization. Dell trades at 11.4x consensus fiscal 2027 earnings of $26.00 against 12.2x trailing, where trailing was 39x in August; HPE sits at 8.1x forward against the 10-13x it fetched in May. Antonio Neri, HPE's chief executive, put it plainly on 23 November: "I will argue that based on that profile we should deserve a higher multiple because I don't see us at a multiple that is 8 to 9x, makes no sense whatsoever."

What neither vendor controls is allocation. The wafers that would build server memory are building stacks for the accelerators that created the demand, and HPE expects the shortage to run into the second half of 2028 — the same window in which Dell has to deliver a record backlog at the margin it just printed.

Navitas Semiconductor, which sells gallium nitride and silicon carbide power chips that step high-voltage electricity down to what a processor can use, booked twice as many orders as it shipped last quarter. Its backlog now reaches into mid-2027, and that date carries the whole argument.

Revenue was $14.0m in the September quarter, up 38% from a year earlier and 33% sequentially, the company's first year-over-year growth since 2024. Gross margin recovered to 39.3% after minus 9.5% in the June quarter. December-quarter guidance is $16.0m, give or take $0.5m. What this tests is a specific belief: at a Citi conference on 8 September, Navitas had pushed its in-rack native 800-volt content out to early 2028, six weeks after dating the same content to mid-to-late 2027 on its July call. The schedule had been sliding. It stopped sliding.

The order book now has dates and purchase orders behind it

Management set out four steps on the 3 November call. Silicon carbide rated at 1.2 kilovolts, replacing silicon inside the alternating-to-direct-current power supplies that feed a rack, ramps this quarter and into the first half of 2027, where Navitas says it typically holds 30% to 40% of the power-supply content. Rack-level 400- and 800-volt silicon carbide designs have "large orders in place" for a first-half 2027 ramp. Native 800-volt parts are sampling across more than a dozen programs with production expected before the end of 2027. Solid-state transformers for the grid arrive from 2028. One customer has already ordered one million gallium nitride devices for an 800-volt application.

That sequence is not the company's invention. Nvidia's own published calendar puts the first 800-volt direct-current power racks in production in the second half of 2026, row-level power centers in 2027 and solid-state transformer blocks in 2028, with megawatt racks supported starting in 2027.

"Power is now the #1 constraint on AI compute," chief executive Chris Allexandre told investors on 3 November. "Power efficiency is becoming capacity."

The wait costs $34m a quarter

Navitas has finished leaving the business that used to pay its bills: mobile and low-end consumer fell below 5% of revenue last quarter and management expects it to be insignificant by the December quarter, which leaves high-power markets at essentially the entire company. Artificial-intelligence infrastructure should exceed 40% of December revenue and triple in calendar 2027, on management's estimate.

The cost of standing in that position is visible. The operating loss widened to $34.4m on $14.0m of revenue, worse than the $27.2m of the prior quarter, so the revenue turn has not reached the loss line. Pro forma cash is about $405m with no debt, which funds roughly three years of losses at the current rate. On 6 October Navitas completed its purchase of Claros for about $232.8m in cash and shares, buying integrated voltage regulator technology for the last inches of delivery to the processor.

That purchase walked it into Vicor's room. Vicor, which builds modular direct-current converters for computing, defense and test equipment, used its own October call to argue the opposite case: "Vicor proprietary dual FPA enables 10% to 15% reduction in AI processor power loss with over 40x the current gain of IVRs," sales head Philip Davies said on 20 October, meaning integrated regulators of the kind Navitas just bought cost power to insert.

Two suppliers are already billing the same transition

Vicor's September quarter was $197.1m, of which $66.6m was royalty income including a catch-up payment from a new licensee, leaving about $130.5m of shipped product. Backlog was $406m at 30 September, up 166% in a year. Its December guide of 10%-plus product growth and a $43m royalty minimum implies total revenue falling to roughly $186m, because the catch-up does not repeat. Bel Fuse, which makes power supplies and magnetics, grew data solutions revenue 61% year over year and took $12.4m from one artificial-intelligence customer against $3.2m a year earlier.

So the transition is paying somebody now, on shipments, at 68.3% gross margin in Vicor's case and 40.9% in Bel Fuse's.

What the markdown accounts for

Navitas closed at $8.56 on the day of its results, up 7.94%, then $6.595 three weeks later on 24 November, 23% below the post-earnings close and 47% below late August, with no adverse company disclosure in between. Nvidia itself fell 28.6% over the thirty days to 24 November, and the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.28% on 20 November alone on doubts about returns on AI capital. Vicor fell 27.7% over the same thirty days while its revenue grew 78.5% year over year.

The results account for none of the decline at either name, and the group's split has a cause rather than a mood. Vicor and Bel Fuse own revenue recognized on things leaving a loading dock; Navitas owns purchase orders that convert on a rack architecture dated to the first half of 2027. A price-to-earnings multiple does not exist for Navitas, where consensus sees no positive net income until 2029; on sales it trades at 35.7x forward against roughly 100x enterprise value to sales in May. Vicor sits at 30.3x forward earnings against 31.9x trailing, down from about 68x forward in May, with the caveat that the royalty line is 34% of revenue and under Federal Circuit review over whether a salesperson's email licensed Foxconn for free.

Navitas has 190 employees and consensus revenue of $82.4m next year. Everything it has sold for 2027 is now a promise with a purchase order number on it, which is a better asset than a design win and a worse one than a shipment.

Half the company is now light

Silicon photonics alone was about $240m of the $526m third quarter, roughly 46% of revenue, and Tower guided its annualized silicon-photonics run rate above $1.1bn on fourth-quarter group revenue of about $600m. Its 200mm fabs ran at 85–90% utilization and the 300mm fab ran above its 85% model. Management raised the long-term annual model to $3.6bn of revenue at a 45% gross margin, $760m higher than the February 2026 version.

"This annual net profit of $1.2 billion target is $450 million higher than the pre February 2026 model, representing 59% incremental net profit derived from the incremental $760 million revenue, reflecting our enhanced product mix," chief financial officer Oren Shirazi told investors on 2 November.

The prepayments are the mechanism behind that confidence. "Customers will never sign up to being the end of '26 now, not '27, not just '28, but 2029, unless they have faith that, number one, you can ship wafers on time at the highest quality," chief executive Russell Ellwanger said on the same call. Tower's $920m capacity program was 65% paid through the third quarter, self-funded, with every long-term agreement securing that capacity signed. On 3 November it announced a collaboration with Innolight on near-package optics for AI infrastructure.

The fall was the rating, not the estimates

Shares fell 9.6% on 20 November, rose 18.4% on 23 November and fell 8.7% again on 24 November, leaving them 46.8% below the 24 September high. That is the signature of index-level repricing: Nvidia dropped 14% on 20 November after Reuters reported an SEC review of its accounting, and futures markets put roughly 91% odds on a Federal Reserve rate increase on 9 December.

Tower now trades at 30.4x consensus earnings for this year, against about 53x at its 24 September close on the same estimate, and 17.3x 2027 consensus. Consensus revenue growth for 2027 is 39.8%. Nothing in the estimates moved down.

The same gap sits at the other two. GlobalFoundries, a specialty foundry in Malta, New York, posted its first year-on-year blended wafer price increase since 2023, up 4.8%, and chief executive Tim Breen said on 4 November the company is "absolutely booked out through 2027"; its forward multiple went from roughly 23.6x in late September to 18.3x. UMC, the Hsinchu contract foundry, lifted utilization from 85% to 92% on record shipments of 1.2m 12-inch equivalents and guides a 10% blended price rise for 2027; it trades at 7.1x trailing enterprise value to earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization.

What breaks in 2028

The credible risk is not mature-node commoditization. It is that everyone builds the same photonics capacity at once: Tower's Japanese fabs, GlobalFoundries Malta, STMicroelectronics and UMC's Singapore shells land together, with Samsung Foundry arriving from 2027, against demand that TSMC expects to take silicon photonics past half the optical transceiver market by 2027. Ellwanger himself flagged discounting by entrants without technical differentiation as a near-term risk. Tower has the thinnest valuation cushion of the three and the richest growth assumption inside it.

Until that capacity arrives, the distinguishing fact is whose customers pay early. A buyer who wires cash in 2026 to hold a 2029 wafer slot has made a statement that a price chart cannot revise.

Grand Theft Auto VI went on sale in the week of 17 November, and the figure its publisher put out the day after is the largest ever announced for an entertainment launch: $3.79bn of day-one retail sales, on 32.5m units sold ahead of release. The launch was read as the year's earnings arriving. What it actually sets in motion is a recognition process that runs for quarters, and the timing is the whole question for a publisher whose revenue is governed by one release calendar while its closest listed comparison, NetEase, is paid every day.

Who gets paid before the publisher does

The gap between consumer spend and publisher revenue is wide enough that the third-party estimates do not even measure the same thing. Sensor Tower put first-day digital sales at 24.58m units and about $2.36bn, after an initial, lower figure. Company retail spend and vendor digital estimates are separate quantities and should be carried as such.

What the shipment does change immediately is amortization. Take-Two's capitalized software development costs and licences stood at $2,577.3m at 30 September, against roughly $2,270.7m of internally developed costs at 31 March, and that pool only starts running against revenue now. The quarter into which the launch lands was already guided hard: net bookings of $3.35bn to $3.5bn against $1.76bn a year earlier, with operating expenses up roughly 21%, almost all of it launch marketing. NetEase's deferral book stopped building

NetEase, the Hangzhou internet group that develops its own games and publishes Blizzard's catalogue in China, holds the other model. Its contract liabilities, the in-game money collected and not yet recognized, were RMB20.21bn at 30 September against RMB20.51bn at the end of December: roughly 90% of a quarter's games revenue by our calculation, and flat rather than growing. Online games revenue was RMB22.4bn, down from RMB24.5bn in the June quarter, and group revenue fell 1.8% to RMB27.85bn, 8% to 9% below consensus on Bank of America's read.

The part that worked was the licensed shelf, where World of Warcraft: Forever launched on 5 November to record annual-pass purchases; the sequential fall came from self-developed titles.

Lower spend per payer needs global scale to compensate.

One year is paid for, the next is not

The recognition machinery is real and it explains the accounting on both sides. It does not explain the prices. Take-Two's reported September quarter showed revenue down 15.6% to $1,497m and a GAAP operating loss of $117.5m; the shares are up 8.2% over 30 days because the market is trading a quarter that has not been reported, encouraged by target raises to $295 at Wedbush and higher elsewhere. Withheld guidance does account for some of it, and on 24 November the shares moved with the China listings rather than the gaming shelf, Alibaba falling 3.4% the same session.

One publisher now has to convert a day's till receipts into years of recognized revenue; the other has to persuade players to pay for cosmetics in a market where short dramas and short video absorbed more than the entire net increase in mobile screen time. January settles the second question before the first quarter of amortization settles the first.

The assembly fee that did not compress

The shares fell 5.5% in New York to $48.75 the following session.

Neither company accepts the signal. At 7.6x forward earnings the stock looks cheap until the denominator is dated: consensus already models 2027 earnings of $7.97 a share against $10.69 this year, which puts it nearer 10x next year's number. Book value of 0.90x is the one anchor arguing the other way. Set against that, 2.3m mandatory convertible preferred shares convert automatically on 1 March 2027 into as many as 9.14 common shares each, roughly 21m shares or 17.7% of the third quarter's diluted count at the maximum ratio.

He named no date, which is the whole trade: the companies are arguing cost-curve logic against a futures curve that only has to be right for two quarters.

Accenture, the Dublin-headquartered firm selling strategy, consulting, cloud, data and multi-year managed operations to large enterprises, closed its fiscal year on 31 August with record new bookings, a wider operating margin and roughly 35,000 more people on the payroll than a year earlier. That last fact is the problem with the story the sector has been sold.

The case against the big integrators, stated plainly in the note that reinstated coverage of Cognizant at Neutral in mid-November, is that artificial intelligence is a structural rather than cyclical headwind, because it erodes billable hours and pushes delivery risk onto the vendor. The case for them is that the same tools let a firm bill the same work with fewer people. Both arguments resolve to one measurable quantity: revenue per employee. Derived from each company's reported revenue and its own disclosed headcount, it moved about 2% at each over the past year.

Accenture's margin gain was made below the delivery line

Revenue of $74.18bn was up 6.5% in fiscal 2026, operating income up 11.5%, and the operating margin reached 15.4% against 14.7%. That is real operating leverage, and it did not come from the price of the work: gross margin was flat at 32.1%. Headcount reached approximately 814,000 at 31 August against approximately 779,000 a year earlier, a 4.5% rise set against revenue growth of 6.5%, leaving output per head at roughly $91,100 from $89,400. Voluntary attrition rose to 14% from 13%, so nobody is leaving faster either. The firm booked $923m of restructuring charges in the year under a programme that reskills some people and exits others where reskilling is not viable.

The order book is the strongest part of the record: new bookings of $84.54bn for the year, a record, up 5% in dollars and 3% in local currency, with a trailing four-quarter managed-services book-to-bill of 1.2 and 141 client bookings above $100m in the fourth quarter. Managed services supplied $12.77bn of that quarter's $22.17bn, so most of what was signed is contracted run-work priced before the hours are worked.

What is no longer in the record is the AI line. Accenture retired its separate advanced-AI bookings disclosure after the first quarter of fiscal 2026, the final print being $2.2bn of bookings and about $1.1bn of revenue, on the grounds that advanced AI is now embedded in nearly everything it does. A client count replaced it: more than 400 clients initiated advanced-AI work during the year. "AI is the breakthrough innovation. Only reinvention can unlock the value," chief executive Julie Sweet said at the 14 October investor day, where the firm also said its AI and data workforce had grown from 40,000 to nearly 110,000 professionals over four years, additive to the 814,000 rather than a substitution for it.

Cognizant's 4.6% was mostly bought and resold

Cognizant, the Teaneck firm selling consulting, technology and outsourcing to financial services, healthcare, products and communications clients, grew third-quarter revenue 4.6% to $5.664bn. Acquisitions contributed roughly 2.1 percentage points of that and resale of third-party products roughly 1.9, leaving organic services growth under 1%. Headcount of 359,900 was up 2.9% year on year, putting revenue per employee near $60,800 from $59,600.

The adjusted operating margin of 16.3% was the highest in four years and a seventh consecutive quarter of year-on-year expansion. The reported margin went the other way, 15.4% against 16.0%. Guidance for constant-currency growth was cut to 4.0-4.4% from 4.0-5.5% on discretionary pressure in healthcare and consumer-facing sectors, and bookings signed in the quarter fell 7% even as the trailing-twelve-month total rose 4% to $28.7bn on a 1.3x book-to-bill. "Even at midpoint of 4.2%, we would be both on total growth basis as well as on the organic basis would be delivering a top-tier performance vis-à-vis rest of the players," chief financial officer Jatin Dalal told investors on 29 October. Deutsche Bank cut the stock to Hold on 13 November, target $66 from $75, saying its growth thesis had not played out.

One mechanism here points the right way. Fixed-price and transaction-based work reached 58% of revenue, up 1.4 points year on year and 5 points since the third quarter of 2022, the contract form under which a productivity gain stays with the vendor. "The pivot is switching from productivity to growth imperatives using AI. And the rebaselining of productivity has already happened to a large extent," chief executive Ravi Kumar said on the same call. At 10.6x forward earnings, the shares carry about a third less than the 15.3x on which the equivalent forward estimate was priced two years ago.

What the per-head numbers settle

Neither income statement carries the deflation yet, in either direction.

Six months of share gains, Accenture up 31.8% and Cognizant up 18.7%, came with no acceleration underneath them. Accenture's quarterly revenue growth ran between 5.6% and 8.3% through the year and its fiscal 2027 guidance is 3% to 6% in local currency; Cognizant decelerated from 5.8% to 4.6% across three quarters. Both then handed much of the gain back in a market-wide correction driven by a December Federal Reserve rate hike moving to roughly 95% probability, with no company disclosure behind it.

Accenture is being paid more per client without getting more out of each employee, and part of the next leg is bought: the investor-day framework has acquisitions contributing 2% to 2.5% of fiscal 2027 revenue against roughly 1.5% in fiscal 2026. Its 17.1x trailing earnings against 15.9x forward prices about 7% earnings growth, well under the 27.8x forward the shares carried two years ago. Cheap because slow, in other words, and the slowness is in the guidance.

The threat neither filing captures arrived from above. Anthropic, whose Claude models roughly 30,000 Accenture professionals are trained to deploy, launched its own consulting and professional-services division on 3 November, and OpenAI has made a similar move. The model vendors now bid for the implementation work, and the integrators' answer, for the moment, is to hire.
