Symbotic's Gross Profit Grew Twice as Fast as Revenue, Then Walmart Committed $6bn More

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The warehouse-automation maker whose shares have halved in a year is getting more out of each system it installs, and its largest customer just enlarged the order book by about a third. Neither fact has done anything for the stock.

Symbotic's June quarter put gross margin at 22.3% against 18.2% a year earlier on revenue of $720.8m, with 11 new deployments started and 56 of 77 systems now operational; software and operation-services revenue grew 57% and 49%. On 29 October Walmart signed an agreement committing roughly $6bn across 25 US regional distribution centers.

The business does not explain the fall. Within the same robotics shelf, Teradyne's drop is a semiconductor-test event wearing a robotics label, and Rockwell Automation, improving and flat on the month, never declined at all.

Walmart signed an Enterprise Master Automation Agreement with Symbotic on 29 October, committing roughly $6bn of spend over several years across 25 US regional distribution centers and selected Americas markets, plus $125m for next-generation racking and inventory technology. Symbotic, which sells an end-to-end automated warehousing system of robots and software to wholesalers and retailers, expects the commitments to add up to $6.92bn to its future backlog — about 31% on top of the $22.5bn it carried at the end of June. The shares rose 3.7% premarket on 30 October, then closed at $37.93, below the $38.97 close of the last session before the agreement was signed.

The month's selling across robotics names has been read as a verdict on robot demand. What a deployed Symbotic system actually earns its maker says the opposite.

Fifty-six systems running, and each one earns more

In the quarter ended 27 June, revenue reached $720.8m, up 21.7%, while gross profit grew 49.5% to $160.9m — more than twice the pace of sales. Gross margin went to 22.3% from 18.2%, and the company swung to a $32.9m operating profit from a $20.2m loss. It started 11 new system deployments, brought four more into operation to reach 56 operational of 77 in deployment, and recorded adjusted EBITDA of $95m against $45m a year earlier.

The layer that compounds behind the hardware is growing faster than the hardware. Systems revenue was $671m, up 20%; software revenue was $13m, up 57%; operation services $37m, up 49%. Founder and chief executive Rick Cohen told investors the margin move was not a structural step: "it just has, once again, to do with the mix of deployments," he said on the third-quarter call, "it really then comes down, when you're tracking it, just really comes down to the 11 deployments we are putting in the quarter, just what those pricing adjustments were." The multi-year line is steadier than that caveat implies — full-year gross margin was 18.8% in fiscal 2025 on $2.247bn of revenue, against 15.6% on $1.788bn the year before.

The shares fell from $77.26 a year ago to $37.93, a decline of 50.9%, with 9.0% of that in the past month, and the trend has been bearish since early August rather than breaking in one session. On trailing earnings the stock is at 396x, which measures nothing at a 1.6% net margin. Against gross profit, the measure that fits a hardware deployer, it is 29.6x forward against 40.2x trailing — still 92x forward earnings.

Teradyne's 31% fall is about chip testers

Teradyne carries the robotics label on this shelf and supplied roughly two-thirds of its monthly drawdown, falling 31.2% in October. Its Robotics segment — Universal Robots collaborative arms and MiR autonomous mobile robots — did $96m in the September quarter, up 28% and profitable for the first time, with the Gen7 cobot launched in the period. That is 7.3% of the $1.306bn the company billed. Semiconductor Test passed $1bn, up 77%, and total operating margin went to 30.8% from 18.9%.

The shares fell after a beat: adjusted earnings of $2.29 against $2.06 expected, spoiled by a December-quarter guide whose $1.30bn midpoint sits fractionally below the quarter just reported and gross margin guided down to 57.5–58.5%. Then came 30 October, when the semiconductor index slid 7.2% and Intel fell nearly 29%. Chief executive Gregory Smith framed robots as the next thing, not the current thing: "with the growth of physical AI, the capabilities of physical AI, we think that that is going to unlock significant growth in the robotics group." Management puts broad physical-AI deployment at 2028 and later. At 28.9x this year's consensus earnings and 22.2x next year's, against the 30–35x the stock carried in May, the de-rating arrived while estimates rose.

The one that never fell

Rockwell Automation, which sells control hardware, industrial software and lifecycle services to factory operators, is flat over the 30-day window and up 19.3% over the year. Its June quarter had organic sales up 10% and Software & Control revenue of $751m, up 19%, at a 34.8% segment margin against 31.6%, with full-year guidance raised. Software & Control book-to-bill came in at 0.97, the one number pointing the other way, and on the call management said "the book-to-bill is in good shape." No deferral of discrete-automation spending was flagged. At 40.8x trailing and 29.3x forward earnings, Rockwell is below the 45.9x it carried in early May.

So the drawdown is a repricing, and most of it is not about robots at all. Teradyne's fall traces to a flat sequential guide inside a sector that lost a fifth of its value, and its robot line is too small to move the share price either way. Rockwell has no drawdown to explain. That leaves Symbotic, where the gap is real and one-directional: deployments started, systems brought live, margin per system and recurring revenue all moved up through fiscal 2026 while the equity lost half its value, and the single largest customer has now underwritten the next several years of installs.

What the agreement does not fix is the thing that makes the stock fragile. Walmart is getting a board nomination recommendation and non-voting observers in the boardroom of the supplier it has just committed $6bn to, and Symbotic accepted certain restrictions on whom it sells to. The customer concentration investors call Symbotic's chief risk is now also the reason its order book is growing.

Getty Images Was Wiped Out by $2.1bn of Debt, Not by a Shrinking Picture Business

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Two stock-image libraries are being destroyed by different things, and only one of them is generative software. Getty Images filed a prepackaged Chapter 11 on 21 October whose plan cancels every listed share for no recovery, yet the business under it earned $32.4m of operating income last quarter on revenue down 2.5%, with gross margin improving almost five points to 70.2%.

Shutterstock is where substitution shows in the per-unit record: subscribers down 11.4% to 951,000, paid downloads down 12.3%, and the data-licensing line sold to model developers falling faster than the catalogue it was meant to offset. Its founder returned as chief executive on 28 October and the 5 November earnings call was cancelled for a strategic review.

Getty's equity died of roughly $2.1bn of debt. Shutterstock's revenue is dying of the technology.

Getty Images filed a prepackaged Chapter 11 petition on 21 October to put into force a recapitalisation its lenders had already signed, and the plan it implements cancels all of the company's Class A common stock with no recovery for current holders. What is odd about the filing is the business sitting underneath it. In the quarter ended 30 June, Getty's revenue fell 2.5% to $229.1m, its gross margin improved almost five percentage points to 70.2%, and the company earned $32.4m of operating income.

The visual-content libraries have been priced for two years as the clearest casualties of image generators, and the late-October record splits them on exactly that question. Getty's accounts describe a licensing operation that still turns pictures into profit, carrying about $2.1bn of debt that consumed every dollar of it. Shutterstock's accounts describe the substitution itself.

What killed Getty sat below the operating line

For the full year 2025 Getty posted $231.5m of operating income, a 23.6% margin and up 28% on the prior year, and still reported a $206.1m net loss. The June quarter repeated the shape: positive operating income, a net loss of $86.3m, roughly $119m of distance between the two.

The restructuring support agreement is dated 12 October, backed by holders of all revolving loans and more than two-thirds of the term loans and notes. The Getty family and Koch Equity Development committed up to $533m of new equity and will own 100% of a privately held reorganised company; unsecured bondholders agreed to retire $270m of notes. "Today's filing is a procedural step to implement the financial restructuring previously announced," chief executive Craig Peters said on 21 October. "This process addresses our capital structure and certain non-operating liabilities."

The listing had already ended. The New York Stock Exchange notified Getty on 29 September that it would begin delisting proceedings for an abnormally low selling price and suspended trading immediately; the shares moved to the over-the-counter Pink market the next day. On 30 October the stub fell 36.7% to $0.019, leaving $8.3m of equity value against $981.3m of 2025 revenue. No Getty disclosure carries that date, and the likelier reading is continued liquidation of an interest the plan has already written to zero.

Getty's catalogue is weakening, but in customers rather than usage: 240,000 active annual subscribers at 30 June against 321,000 a year earlier, while paid downloads over the same twelve months fell only 2.8% to 90m and subscriber revenue retention slipped to 88.4% from 93.4%. "Our second quarter results reflected continued pressure in Agency and iStock e-commerce, while the larger parts of our business serving enterprise customers continued to demonstrate resilience and growth," Peters said on 10 August.

Charging model developers through the courts is not the alternative. On 4 November 2025 the UK High Court rejected Getty's secondary copyright claim against Stability AI, holding that model weights are not a copy of the training images because the model does not store them.

Shutterstock's downloads are the substitution case

Shutterstock's June-quarter revenue fell 16.9% to $221.8m, the third consecutive double-digit decline, and gross margin fell to 57.7%. The per-unit record is unambiguous: subscribers of 951,000 against 1,073,000 and paid downloads of 98.7m against 112.6m, with average revenue per customer rising to $292 from $266. A smaller base, each remaining account spending more.

The hedge is shrinking faster than the thing it hedges. Data, Distribution and Services revenue, 25% of the total, fell 16% to $56.1m, dragged by the data offering, down 11% in the June quarter after a 63% drop in the March quarter. The company also took a $173.7m goodwill impairment after its fair value fell on the collapse of the Getty merger, and attributed lower content revenue chiefly to weak new-customer acquisition.

At $4.14 the equity is worth $153m, below a single quarter's revenue, and forward price-to-sales of 0.195x sits above the trailing 0.170x. That inversion is the market marking the denominator down, with consensus revenue falling 23% this year and a further 8% next.

Founder Jon Oringer, chief executive until 2020, returned to the job effective 28 October. "AI is fundamentally reshaping the creative industry, and the pace of change is accelerating," he said in the 29 October announcement. The next day the company said it would not hold its 5 November earnings call while it "continues to evaluate its strategic direction and priorities"; the shares fell 6.1% on the heaviest volume of the month.

Two collapses, two causes

This corner of software looks like one story in October and is two. Getty's 78% monthly loss is a documented capital-structure event with filed terms, and its operating margin went the right way throughout. Shutterstock's much smaller monthly decline sits on top of a year in which the equity lost 83%, and there the income statement and the download count agree: the catalogue is being replaced, and the fees model developers pay for data are falling faster than the licensing revenue they displace. The software vendors, Adobe and Figma, both finished the month higher, with revenue growing 12.9% and 48.2% respectively and gross margins down roughly one and five percentage points as inference costs landed in cost of revenue.

Shutterstock will publish third-quarter figures on 5 November with nobody on the phone to explain them.

Smithfield Guided Its Branded Meats to Out-Earn the Whole Company This Year

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Smithfield told investors on 27 October that its branded Packaged Meats arm should earn more this fiscal year than Smithfield entire, because the hog farms and slaughter plants that supply it are losing money. Packaged Meats widened its margin to 11.6% on average selling prices down 5.3%; Fresh Pork lost $89m as the processing spread fell 35%.

That mechanism splits the shelf the market has been selling as one. Smithfield and Hormel own the brand that captures a cheap pound of meat, and Hormel's adjusted operating margin reached 9.0% as it raised fiscal 2026 earnings guidance. Pilgrim's Pride sells the commodity itself, and with the jumbo cutout down 27% its US margin halved to 8.7%; there the share price has followed the earnings down.

Smithfield Foods, which raises hogs and sells Smithfield bacon, Nathan's Famous hot dogs and Eckrich deli meat, told investors on 27 October that its branded Packaged Meats segment should earn adjusted operating income of $1,075m–$1,150m this fiscal year. The company as a whole is guided to $900m–$1.0bn. The branded arm is expected to out-earn the business that contains it, because the farms and slaughter plants feeding it are losing money.

Four of the five big US meat and poultry names have been marked down between about 7% and 29% over the past year, Tyson Foods excepted, on a premise that cheap grain and cheap hogs would widen processing spreads. The quarters reported in June and September show something narrower. Inside a vertically integrated protein company the cheap raw material is a transfer, not a windfall, and it settles wherever the brand sits.

Smithfield's margin came out of its own barns

Packaged Meats earned about $230m in the September quarter, with margin widening to 11.6% from 10.8%, on sales down 5.2% to $2bn. Average selling price fell 5.3% and volume rose 0.2%: the segment made more money per pound because hog and pork cost fell faster than the price it charged. Upstream, Fresh Pork swung to a loss of $89m from a $10m profit as the processing spread fell 35% and volume dropped 6.3%, while Hog Production earned $34m against $89m. Group adjusted operating profit fell 55% to $145m.

The share gains downstream rest on distribution and brand. "Across our top 25 categories, industry volume declined 3.3%, while we grew volume by nearly 1%. We were the only company in our core competitive set to grow volume in this quarter, driving 0.4 points increase in volume share," Shane Smith, president and chief executive, told investors on 27 October. Smithfield gained share in all five of its largest categories, led by 2.3 points in smoked ham, and its Nathan's Famous purchase eliminates $33m of annual licence costs on rights previously held only through 2032. A new supply agreement with a joint-venture partner cuts internally produced hogs by roughly 900,000.

The shares closed 27 October at $18.33, a few cents above a 52-week low, at 1.03x book with a 12.8% trailing free-cash-flow yield and net debt at 0.5x EBITDA. The forward earnings multiple of 9.3x sits above 8.0x trailing, so consensus is still falling.

Pilgrim's had nothing downstream to catch it

Pilgrim's Pride, the Greeley, Colorado chicken and pork processor controlled by JBS, is the same geometry with the brand missing. US net revenues slipped to $2.65bn from $2.82bn in the June quarter while US adjusted EBITDA fell to $231.5m from $482.7m, a margin of 8.7% against 17.1%, which the company attributed primarily to a 27% drop in the jumbo cutout value. Consolidated operating margin has run 10.4%, 4.5%, 3.6% and 1.4% over the last four quarters.

Feed, the premise the widening-spread case rested on, moved against the processor. Corn ran roughly $4.25–$4.50 a bushel for the year but averaged $5.18 in the first week of August, and October soybean meal traded at $350–$360 a short ton in mid-September against a first-half range of $275–$325. Corn and soymeal are 60–70% of a broiler's diet.

The company is also adding pounds into the falling cutout: about 2.5% US supply growth in the second half. "What we want is to grow our branded business in the U.S. That will compensate the volatility in the big bird commodity market. Once again, we don't want to reduce our commodity operation," chief executive Fabio Sandri said on the 30 July call. Consensus has fiscal 2026 earnings at $2.27 a share against $5.26 for 2025; at $27.09 the forward multiple of 11.9x is wider than the roughly 8.4x the stock carried last October on $4.54 of delivered earnings.

Hormel divested the turkeys and kept the margin

Hormel Foods, the SPAM, Skippy and Jennie-O owner, is the counter-case. Gross margin moved 15 hundredths of a percentage point in the July quarter, against a 7.7-point loss at Pilgrim's; adjusted operating margin expanded to 9.0% and the company raised fiscal 2026 adjusted earnings guidance to $1.45–$1.51. Roughly half its segment sales decline came from divesting the whole-bird turkey business and exiting private-label snack nuts. "With our strong year-to-date performance and continued opportunities ahead, we are raising and narrowing our adjusted earnings outlook for fiscal 2026," interim chief executive Jeff Ettinger said on 27 August. The shares still fell 19.4% over three months, and at 13.3x forward earnings Hormel is marginally cheaper than Tyson's 14.1x — Tyson being the name whose beef unit is guided to a fiscal 2026 operating loss of $625m–$775m while its chicken segment is guided to $1.85bn–$1.95bn.

Arithmetic at Pilgrim's, an open question at Smithfield

Three companies, one cheap pound of meat, three outcomes decided by who owns the label on the package.

For Pilgrim's the market has done the arithmetic: a 28.5% decline over the year against consensus earnings more than halved, and a forward multiple that widened as the price fell. Smithfield is the awkward one, sitting at a 52-week low on the day it reaffirmed the segment guided to carry all of group profit; management tied the duration of the upstream loss to the fourth-quarter holiday season and spring 2027. Hormel's de-rating tests something else entirely: organic sales growth guided to 1–2%.

Pilgrim's Pride reports its third quarter after today's close, with consensus at 48 cents a share. Smithfield has shown where the money in a cheap pound of meat ends up; nobody can yet price when the cutout stops falling for the company that only sells pounds.
