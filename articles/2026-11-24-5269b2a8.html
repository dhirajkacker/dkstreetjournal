Veeva Sells Its Life-Sciences Software on One-Year Orders With Almost No Backlog

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A life-sciences software company that renews most of its revenue every twelve months has nothing in its filings fixing future years in place. Veeva's July-quarter 10-Q states that the transaction price allocated to remaining performance obligations on noncancellable subscription contracts longer than one year was not significant, and that the majority of customer orders run one-year terms.

The business itself is improving. Revenue rose 17.6% in the quarter, operating margin reached 29.6% against 24.8% a year earlier, and the company raised full-year subscription guidance to about $3.08bn in August. What holds the revenue in place is regulatory and dated: support for legacy Veeva CRM running on Salesforce ends 31 December 2029, and more than 180 customers are already live on the replacement.

The shares fell 16.6% in the month to 23 November with no issuer event disclosed, leaving the 3 December print as the test.

Veeva Systems sells cloud software to one industry — drug and device makers — and it sells almost all of it twelve months at a time. The Vault platform that holds clinical-trial documents, regulatory submissions, quality records and safety data, and the commercial suite that runs pharma sales and medical teams, are both bought on annual orders. Its 10-Q for the quarter ended 31 July 2026 says the transaction price allocated to remaining performance obligations on noncancellable subscription contracts longer than one year was not significant, and that the majority of customers' subscription orders have one-year terms.

That matters because the markdown running through enterprise software this autumn has been sorted by contract structure: a committed multi-year book reads as protection from the possibility that artificial intelligence shrinks what buyers need. Veeva has no such book, and the market has not spared it. The durability here is regulatory switching cost rather than contract duration, which is a harder thing to observe and a harder thing to date.

The twelve-month order book

The filing shows where the money sits. Veeva recognized $992m of revenue out of beginning deferred-revenue balances in the six months to 31 July. Unbilled receivables of $45m and contract assets of $24m are both tied to professional services. There is no contracted floor extending into 2028 to point at.

What exists instead is a sunset with a date on it. Legacy Veeva CRM, which runs on Salesforce, reaches end of support on 31 December 2029, forcing life-sciences customers to move to Veeva's own Vault CRM or to a rival inside a roughly four-year window. As of 26 August, more than 180 customers were live on Vault CRM, including five of the top 20 biopharmas, with 12 of the top 20 committed; that is up from 140 live at the end of fiscal 2026.

The migration is contested. Salesforce's Life Sciences Cloud, co-developed with IQVIA, won Novartis and Pfizer over the stretch in which Veeva added Merck, Roche and Novo Nordisk commitments.

The business the filings describe

Nothing in the numbers has deteriorated. July-quarter revenue of $928.0m was up 18%, with subscription revenue up 16%, and Veeva lifted full-year subscription guidance to about $3.08bn. Reported growth has edged up across four quarters, from 16.0% to 17.6%. Operating income grew 40.4% on 17.6% more revenue, taking operating margin to 29.6% from 24.8%.

"Q2 was another strong quarter, delivering results ahead of our guidance," chief executive Peter Gassner said in prepared remarks on 26 August. "It was our best CRM quarter ever. We also are accelerating rapidly in AI overall, and especially with Veeva Falcon."

The encroachment is real and recent. On 20 October, OpenAI acquired Orchestra Bio, whose agents let biopharma teams delegate study monitoring and the tracking of development timelines, folding it into its Rosalind life-sciences suite — the clinical-operations work Vault's R&D applications serve.

Eight sessions with nothing behind them

Veeva fell 5.2% on 19 November, the eighth consecutive down session, a stretch worth about $7.1bn of market value. It closed at $269.47 on 23 November, 16.6% below its 23 October level and still 9.2% higher over three months. No issuer disclosure accompanies the fall; the sector lost roughly $2 trillion of market value over twelve months as investors repriced per-seat software.

So the drawdown is a repricing of the premium, not a verdict on the quarter. At 43.3x trailing and 29.1x forward earnings, Veeva has barely compressed from roughly 42x trailing in August, because earnings grew into the fall faster than the shares gave back. Consensus has revenue growth easing to 12.3% in fiscal 2028, which is what the forward number is paying for, and the annual renewal cycle leaves that estimate resting on how customers behave at each order date.

A company with no backlog is re-underwritten by its customers every renewal cycle. The quarter Veeva reports on 3 December is the first in which those customers had reason to ask whether an agent could do the monitoring instead.
