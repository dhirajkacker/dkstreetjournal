Nvidia Signed Its Next Packaging Capacity With ASE's Siliconware, Not Amkor

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Two outsourced chip assemblers were marked down together this month as one bet on advanced packaging being the bottleneck in artificial-intelligence hardware. The week of 17 November split them on the thing that pays: a signed capacity commitment.

ASE Technology's subsidiary Siliconware Precision Industries agreed with Nvidia to triple Nvidia-dedicated turnkey packaging lines in Taiwan by mid-2029. Amkor, by a 23 November Digitimes report, is a third-choice supplier of 2.5-dimensional packaging still in early-stage collaboration, with meaningful volume not expected in 2027.

The segment numbers say the same. ASE's assembly, test and materials business earned a 30.6% gross margin last quarter and delivered 91% of group operating profit; Amkor earns a blended 18.26% and discloses no segment split, with revenue flat year over year after 25.6% growth in the June quarter.

Siliconware Precision Industries, the packaging subsidiary of ASE Technology Holding, signed a multibillion-dollar, multi-year agreement with Nvidia on 17-18 November covering turnkey advanced packaging lines in Taiwan — bumping, interposer wafer fabrication, assembly and test — intended to triple Nvidia-dedicated capacity by mid-2029. Amkor Technology, the Tempe assembler that counted Apple as 29.8% of 2025 revenue and Qualcomm as 11.1%, has been brought in as a third supplier of 2.5-dimensional packaging and remains in early-stage collaboration, with engineering runs and pilot production but no meaningful volume expected in 2027, Digitimes reported on 23 November.

That reorders a hierarchy the market had taken for granted. Earlier this year the assumption was that TSMC's chip-on-wafer-on-substrate capacity was the binding constraint on accelerator output and that the independent assemblers were its overflow valve, with Amkor first in line as the non-Taiwanese second source. Both names have since been sold as one trade. The disclosures separate them on the only measure that matters: what a finished package pays.

One leading-edge package pays ASE three times what the electronics business does

ASE's assembly, test and materials segment turned over TWD 151.5bn in the September quarter, up 51% year over year, on a 30.6% gross margin and a 20% operating margin. That segment is 62% of consolidated revenue and 91% of group operating profit. The electronics manufacturing arm, whose volumes still grew 35%, ran an 8.3% gross margin after component pass-through costs, down from 8.9% in the June quarter. Consolidated revenue overstates the franchise; the segment table corrects it completely.

"The so-called structural margin ceiling has now become the base for us," chief financial officer Joseph Tung told investors on 29 October. Leading-edge advanced packaging revenue was guided above $4bn for 2026, up from $3.7bn mid-call, and Tung said ASE expects "to double our LEAP revenue" in 2027. Full-process CoWoS goes from $300m this year to above $1bn next; co-packaged optics is guided above $0.5bn in 2027. Utilization is at or near 100%, with physical space rather than demand named as the constraint, and capital spending was raised to roughly $11.5bn against net debt-to-equity of 66%.

Amkor's mix turned over while its revenue stood still

Amkor's September quarter was a profit record on a flat top line: revenue up 0.18% year over year after 25.6% growth in the June quarter, with gross profit up 27.8% and operating income up 41.4% as gross margin reached 18.26% from 14.32%. Communications fell to 37% of revenue from 51%, Computing rose 25% sequentially to a record on a high-density fan-out processor program, and automotive and industrial grew 37%. Advanced products were $1.632bn of $1.991bn in net sales, about 82%. The handset weakness is an artifact of memory: DRAM wafers diverted to high-bandwidth stacks starved the system-in-package line of components.

Amkor is spending into the gap. It committed $1.3bn to Korea on 21 October, taking multi-year commitments with Nvidia to roughly $3bn, and has taken about $3bn of advance payments from a dozen customers against future capacity, enough to fund capex without new debt through 2027. Arizona Phase 1 completes late 2027. "Customers are not only planning further ahead, but are also making long-term commitments," chief executive Kevin Engel said on 26 October.

What reaches either company is the cheaper end. TrendForce reported in August that CoWoS-R and CoWoS-S are the processes being released first, and HBM stacking stays inside the memory makers, from through-silicon-via fill to final test.

Two sessions, both market-wide, did the damage

Over the month to 23 November Amkor fell 11.4% and ASE 17.6%. Compound 5 November and 20 November alone and the declines are 24.3% and 31.1% — more than the full-month moves. Those were the two worst sessions in the market this month, the first on the post-midterm turn against artificial-intelligence capital spending plus Qualcomm's 26% drop in handset chip revenue, the second on a reported SEC probe of Nvidia. Neither day carried a disclosure from either assembler. On 23 November, with Jensen Huang reinstated, Amkor rose 15.1% and ASE 21.4% against a market mean of 3.3%.

So the month's selling says nothing about packaging economics; the week's contracts say a great deal. Amkor trades at 13.9x trailing earnings and 12.6x FY2027 consensus EPS of $2.76, against 44.4x in May on a market value that has gone from $19.1bn to $8.6bn, with nothing worse than flat revenue behind it. ASE sits at 30.2x trailing against 52.6x in May, but its market value has fallen only about 6% since then while trailing earnings nearly doubled: the de-rating is earnings arriving at a price that stood still.

Amkor's Arizona plant finishes its first phase in late 2027 — the same year the wafers it has not yet qualified to run start shipping from Taiwan.
