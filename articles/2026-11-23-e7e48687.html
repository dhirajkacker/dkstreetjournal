Viasat Said Airline Switches to Starlink Will Hit Up to a Fifth of Its Aviation Revenue

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Three satellite operators were marked down together in the five sessions to 20 November, and the disclosures say only one of them is losing customers. Viasat's US home broadband base is down to roughly 100,000 subscribers from 143,000 a year earlier at an essentially unchanged price, and its finance chief told investors that recent airline announcements will ultimately affect 15% to 20% of aviation revenue.

The same company's defense arm grew 20% to $365m on a backlog of $4.2bn, which makes its markdown a split story rather than a demand story. Telesat's is a debt wall: its geostationary lenders agreed only to hold off enforcement until 18 January 2027. Iridium's is arithmetic from somebody else's share price, with Rocket Lab trading below the floor of the collar in the takeover the two agreed in June.

Viasat's finance chief gave investors a size for the business its airline customers are taking to SpaceX. "These announcements will ultimately affect 15% to 20% of our aviation revenue," chief financial officer Gary Chase said on the 5 November earnings call, and the company guided aviation revenue to decline in fiscal 2028 from fiscal 2027 levels, with installed aircraft slipping slightly each sequential quarter. The announcements he meant have names: American Airlines is bringing Starlink to its entire mainline fleet and Southwest is pivoting fleet-wide, while United has passed 600 Starlink-equipped aircraft and Delta has committed to Amazon's Leo service, which does not begin rolling out until 2028.

Viasat, Telesat and Iridium were all marked down through November, and the markdown has been read as a single story about Starlink and direct-to-device connectivity taking the satellite industry's customers. One of the three sets of disclosures supports that reading. The other two describe a loan maturity and a takeover collar.

What is actually shrinking at Viasat, and what is not

Viasat, a Carlsbad multi-orbit operator selling home and business broadband, in-flight connectivity, maritime service and government communications, ended the September quarter with about 100,000 US fixed broadband subscribers at $111 average revenue per user, against 143,000 at $112 a year earlier. Price held; roughly 43,000 customers left. Maritime vessels in service fell 8%. Communication Services revenue was $815m, down 3%.

The other half of the company went the other way. Defense and Advanced Technologies revenue grew 20% to $365m, close to a third of the $1,180m consolidated total, with segment earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization up 57% to $76m and total backlog of $4.2bn. "In the second quarter, DAT earned about $420 million of firm awards and our current fiscal year '27 plan anticipates about $2 billion of awards compared to $1.6 billion last year," chairman and chief executive Mark Dankberg said on the same call. In November Viasat was awarded a Phase 1 contract under NASA's NEXUS space-relay initiative worth up to $460.9m, beating out larger awards to nobody: SES Space & Defense took up to $340.4m and Astranis up to $264.3m.

The financing story improved too. Net debt to adjusted earnings fell to 3.2x from 3.5x, free cash flow is guided above $225m, capex is $850m to $900m including $75m of residual ViaSat-3 spend, and Dankberg told investors that "we believe that capital-intensive ViaSat-3 phase is behind us." What the mix is doing to profitability is the catch: revenue rose 3.4% last quarter while gross profit fell 1.5% to $378.9m, and price-to-gross-profit has widened to 5.6x from 5.1x in early May because the denominator is shrinking. On trailing cash the shares look cheap for an operator with that backlog, at 7.3x enterprise value to earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization and a 7.5% free-cash-flow yield. A board strategic review committee formed in May under a cooperation agreement with Carronade Capital Management, which argues the defense unit is worth separating, was still running as of November.

Telesat's December maturity became a January deadline

Telesat, the Ottawa operator leasing geostationary capacity to broadcasters, carriers and governments while it builds the Lightspeed low-orbit constellation, reported September-quarter revenue of C$81m, down 20%, and a net loss of C$157m including a C$90m charge from revaluing Lightspeed financing warrants. Its legacy revenue has now fallen by a fifth or more for three straight quarters. Lightspeed contracted backlog held at about C$5.6bn, but full-year Lightspeed spending guidance was cut to $1.1bn-$1.3bn from $1.3bn-$1.5bn on a revised schedule, and chief executive Dan Goldberg said: "We now plan to have full global service around the end of Q2 2028, as opposed to the end of Q1 2028, which was our previous target."

None of that is what moved the equity. Telesat's geostationary term loan and secured and unsecured notes left roughly $2.3bn requiring repayment or refinancing at the end of 2026, and in mid-November an ad-hoc committee of those holders agreed not to take enforcement action through 18 January 2027 while interest keeps being paid and a broader restructuring is negotiated. No completed refinancing has been announced. On trailing figures there is no multiple to quote: enterprise value to earnings is negative and the free-cash-flow yield is minus 21.9%.

Iridium trades off Rocket Lab's share price

Iridium, which sells voice, broadband and machine-to-machine connectivity over a 66-satellite low-orbit network, has been under an agreed acquisition by Rocket Lab since 28 June: a headline $54 a share, $27 in cash plus Rocket Lab stock at a ratio that floats inside a collar running from $67.50 to $112.50, closing expected mid-2027. Rocket Lab closed at $45.42 on 20 November, well under the floor. Pin the ratio at the floor and the stock leg is worth about $18 at that price, putting total consideration near $45 against Iridium's $42.08 close, a spread of roughly 7% on our arithmetic rather than a contractual number.

The underlying business is neither confirming nor denying the Starlink thesis. Billable subscribers rose 6% to 2,696,000, led by commercial machine-to-machine connections, with no visible substitution; service revenue grew 13% but primarily because of the Aireon acquisition, government service revenue on the US Enhanced Mobile Satellite Services contract rose only 2% to $27.6m, and operational EBITDA was $135.6m against $136.6m a year earlier. Full-year guidance of flat to 2% service-revenue growth was reaffirmed. The buyback was terminated on signing the merger and the board plans to lift the quarterly dividend to $0.16, with net leverage at 4.2x. Forward price-to-earnings of 29.1x sits above a trailing 27.5x, the shape of flat earnings, and for the duration of the deal the acquirer's share price is the valuation.

How much of November the businesses explain

Viasat's own numbers account for a real de-rating of one leg of the company: the consumer and aviation franchises are losing volume to named competitors, with capacity already in orbit and consumption on ViaSat-3 measured at double to triple prior levels but the subscriber base not yet turned. They do not account for a markdown of the whole enterprise, because the government business is growing at 20% with a backlog that rose 7% and leverage falling.

Telesat's decline is the equity sitting behind a debt stack whose lenders have granted six weeks, and its Lightspeed revenue begins in 2028 whatever happens in January. Iridium's is a collar, not a customer. And the timing argues for sector beta over fresh company information: all three sets of disclosures predate the week by two to four weeks, and the broader space complex fell with them after a failed Vulcan launch destroyed 40 Amazon satellites, with AST SpaceMobile down 22% and Rocket Lab 17% in the same five sessions. Viasat fell 16.9%, Telesat 18.1% and Iridium 8.3%, each roughly twice its decline over the prior month, which is the pattern a macro-led risk-off leaves rather than three simultaneous revenue shocks.

One of these three will answer the question on a date already set. Telesat's lenders stand still until 18 January, and nothing in Lightspeed's schedule arrives to help before then.
