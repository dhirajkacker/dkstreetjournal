Liberty Latin America Retired a Tenth of Its Stock; Its New Preferred Yields 11.7%

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The cheapest-looking equity on Latin America's carrier shelf is the one the credit market will not finance cheaply. Liberty Latin America's third-quarter revenue was $1,117m, up 0.4% year on year, with operating income down 11.7% to $181m and a net loss of $70.9m against a small profit a year earlier.

The arithmetic behind the shares is a shrinking share count: more than $150m of buybacks this year retired close to a tenth of the common, and a $500m preference issue distributed in June now trades at $19.16 against a $25 liquidation price. América Móvil re-rated on accelerating revenue and falling leverage; Telefônica Brasil grew revenue 7.2% and spent November in a downtrend. The same regional discount is landing on very different balance sheets.

Liberty Latin America sells fixed broadband, mobile and subsea capacity in Puerto Rico, Panama, Costa Rica, Jamaica and the Caribbean, and in the third quarter it sold about as much of it as a year earlier. Revenue came in at $1,117m, up 0.4% as reported and down 2% on the company's rebased basis. Operating income fell to $181m, and the net loss widened to $70.9m from a $3.3m profit.

The shares have been read as an operating turn. They are not one. Over the twelve months to 27 November the common gained 64.6% and over six months 83.2%, and over the same period the company's revenue line contracted in three of its four consumer markets: Liberty Caribbean down 1% rebased, C&W Panama down 5% on the phasing of government business projects, Puerto Rico down 3%, Costa Rica flat. What moved was the denominator.

The buyback is the growth story

More than $150m went into repurchases during 2026, concentrated in the third quarter. "We have now deployed over $150 million to date this year with a considerable portion of this in Q3, resulting in us buying back nearly 10% of our common shares outstanding as of 2025 year-end," chief executive Balan Nair told investors on 5 November. "This reflects the continued conviction that our stock trades at a meaningful discount to the intrinsic value of the business." The diluted count fell from 199.9m shares in the fourth quarter of 2025 to 194.7m in the third quarter of 2026.

That is being done against $8.5bn of total debt and consolidated net leverage of 4.5x, with $579m of cash and $860m of undrawn capacity, per the company's third-quarter release. Leverage is run silo by silo: Cable & Wireless at 3.8x with roughly five-year weighted average tenor, the Liberty Costa Rica silo below 2x, and a substantial majority of debt fixed or hedged to maturity. For comparison, América Móvil runs at 1.21x and Telefônica Brasil at 0.5x.

June's special dividend set a price on the risk

On 21 May the board committee declared a special dividend of newly issued 9.0% fixed-rate cumulative perpetual redeemable Series A preference shares, one for every ten commons, at a $25 initial liquidation price and roughly $500m in aggregate, distributed on 16 June and listed on Nasdaq. That paper closed at $19.16 on 27 November. The $2.25 annual coupon — 9.0% of the $25 face — is therefore a current yield near 11.7% on claims that rank ahead of the common.

The two lines have moved apart since the summer. The preferred has fallen 8.7% from $20.99 on 27 August while the common rose 17.2% over the same three months. The preference line is far thinner, trading between roughly 12,900 and 85,200 shares a day in November against 287,700 to 584,700 in the common, so its marks deserve that caveat. But the instrument explains the gap: one prices on yield, the other is the residual on a levered capital structure.

Price rises, not volumes, are carrying the margin

Adjusted operating income before depreciation and amortization reached $454m, up 3% rebased, at a 41% margin, 160 basis points wider. Net additions of 56,000 in postpaid mobile and broadband beat the first two quarters, including a fourth consecutive positive postpaid quarter in Puerto Rico. The mechanism is tariffs: 10% increases on fixed broadband and postpaid mobile in Jamaica, selective rises in Panama, higher video and broadband prices and fewer discounts in Puerto Rico, with more written into the 2027 budget. "So far this year, all of our price increases, we've not seen the kind of churn we've seen before in price increases," Nair said on 5 November.

Cash is improving at the margin rather than transforming. "Full year free cash flow remains on track to outperform 2025," chief financial officer Christopher Noyes said on the same call, with year-to-date adjusted free cash flow before distributions to partners running about $140m above the first nine months of 2025. Working against it: a roughly $120m year-to-date working capital headwind in Puerto Rico, vendor financing paydown, and integration and spin-off discussions with Puerto Rico debt holders on which no timing has been given.

The discount is not sorting on the fundamentals

Liberty Latin America now trades at 7.45x trailing enterprise value to EBITDA, the highest of the seven carriers on this shelf, above Millicom's 6.87x and well above América Móvil's 5.36x and TIM's 4.33x. Its 0.41x price-to-sales looks like the cheapest number in the group only because $8.5bn of debt and $500m of preference shares sit ahead of a $1.82bn equity.

The operators whose results improved most got less for it. América Móvil's quarterly revenue growth accelerated through 2026 to 6.0% in the third quarter with operating income up 16.0%, and its shares held a downtrend from 2 September to 28 October before turning up in mid-November; at 15.9x trailing and 14.95x forward earnings with a 10.1% free-cash-flow yield, that repair is the only one on the shelf matching price to operations. Telefônica Brasil grew revenue 7.2% and EBITDA 10% to a 44.5% margin and was in a downtrend from 13 November, at 4.89x EBITDA and a 9.5% free-cash-flow yield. TIM, whose EBITDA margin widened about a point to 41.3% while net income fell 18.0% as a 13.75% Selic rate taxed its financing line, was down 11.9% over twelve months at 4.33x EBITDA and a 17.2% free-cash-flow yield; its nine-month capex of BRL 3.3bn sat against BRL 20.84bn of revenue, and its dividends per share through September were BRL 0.85 against BRL 1.08 a year earlier. The Brazilian pair then gained 9.1% and 8.0% on 27 November, after Lula named Bradesco chairman Luiz Carlos Trabuco finance minister and the Ibovespa rose 6.07% — a discount-rate event, not a company one.

So almost none of Liberty Latin America's advance is explained by what it sold. A tenth fewer shares, a 160-basis-point margin gain bought with price increases, and the market's willingness to look through to a consensus that has the company losing $91m this year and earning $11.5m next — that is the move. The part the results do not cover is the part the preferred is voting on.

The company's own securities disagree about what it is worth, and the disagreement is priced to the dollar. Equity holders have paid up roughly 65% in a year for the residual; the holders of the paper in front of them want nearly 12% a year to stay. One of those two is wrong about the leverage.
