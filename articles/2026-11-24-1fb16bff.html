Fair Isaac Charged More for Fewer Score Pulls; the Regulator Took the Price Lever Away

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Fair Isaac's credit-score business just had the best year in its history, and the mechanism behind it has been capped by the agency that oversees Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. Scores revenue rose 41% in the fiscal year ended 30 September while mortgage score pull volumes fell high single digits, so the growth was per-score price.

The Federal Housing Finance Agency opened the same mortgage pull to VantageScore 4.0 for all approved lenders in September and then folded it onto Classic FICO's single loan-price grid, removing the handicap that had made the cheaper score worth less. FICO responded by holding its $10 wholesale price flat for 2027 against a $0.99 alternative and declining to guide fiscal 2027 at all.

Trailing numbers do not explain a 58.6% twelve-month fall; consensus revenue growth of 2.5% for 2027 does.

Fair Isaac, which licenses the FICO credit score into lenders' transaction flows and sells decision-management software to banks, grew its Scores business by more than two-fifths in the fiscal year ended 30 September while being paid for fewer mortgage score pulls than a year earlier. Scores revenue reached $1.652bn, up 41%, and in the September quarter alone mortgage-origination score revenue rose 72% even as unit volumes fell high single digits. The growth was price.

That is a defensible way to run a business when the buyer has no alternative, and until this autumn the buyer did not. FICO's wholesale royalty on a mortgage-origination score went from about 60 cents to $10 over roughly five years, including a doubling for 2026 from $4.95, while Experian and TransUnion offer a VantageScore 4.0 mortgage score at $0.99. The gap did not matter while government-sponsored enterprise loan pricing treated the two scores unequally.

A ten-to-one price gap stopped being academic

On 9 September the Federal Housing Finance Agency directed Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac to let all approved lenders use VantageScore 4.0, removing the prior-written-approval requirement after a four-month pilot with 50 lenders. VantageScore 4.0 was already used to securitise about 9% of Fannie and Freddie mortgages between 1 May and 31 August. Then, on 28 September, FHFA director Bill Pulte said the enterprises would merge their two loan-level pricing grids into one, with VantageScore 4.0 joining the Classic FICO grid from 1 October. The prior arrangement effectively required a VantageScore roughly 20 points higher to earn the same loan price; the single grid ends that.

FICO's shares fell 29.2% close-to-close the next session, from $846.22 to $599.51, its worst day since 1989. In October the company launched the FICO Preferred Program, bundling Classic FICO with FICO Score 10T and holding the $10 wholesale price flat for 2027 for lenders agreeing to pull FICO on all applications. It then declined to issue fiscal-2027 guidance, citing unresolved mortgage pricing negotiations and weak loan volumes.

Chief executive William Lansing was blunt about what the company has learned. "I think that transparency and detail on our programs and pricing have not been particularly helpful for FICO," he told investors on 4 November. "On a go-forward basis, our intention is to not share every detail. We're not going to share every pricing program. We're not going to share what the prices are."

The trailing numbers argue the other way

Nothing in the reported results looks like a company in trouble. Revenue grew 13.5%, then 15.9%, then 27.3% across the last three fiscal years to $2.535bn; gross margin widened from 79.7% to 86.1%; free cash flow rose 40% to $1.034bn. The software side is compounding independently of mortgages, with Platform annual recurring revenue of $453m, up 72%, and net revenue retention on that platform of 156%. FICO repurchased $3.028bn of stock, about a tenth of the shares, which left shareholders' equity negative and leverage at 3.29x before buybacks paused in the fourth quarter to repay term debt.

Against that record the shares are 58.6% lower than a year ago and 60.8% below their December peak. Twelve months ago the stock carried roughly 66x trailing earnings; it now carries 19.8x trailing and 16.3x forward.

The forward estimates are where the two pictures reconcile. Consensus models fiscal 2027 revenue of $2.592bn, growth of 2.5% after 27.3%, with net income down 4.3% and earnings per share up 3.8% to $44.27 across a range of $40.41 to $49.04. On those numbers the step from trailing to forward multiple is share count, not growth. A 16x multiple on a business compounding at low single digits with negative book equity is not obviously cheap; it is cheap only against FICO's own history.

Volumes make the point harder. The Mortgage Bankers Association forecast total single-family originations of $2.2tn in 2026, up about 8% from roughly $2.0tn, so a high-single-digit decline in score pulls is worse than the market behind it. Management attributed the decline to elevated rates and said there had been no meaningful loss to competition; the mix also shifted from first-lien mortgages toward lower-priced home equity lines.

What was priced, and what the shelf did not price

The 20.7% recovery from the September low is the market handicapping the defence rather than dismissing it. FICO has signed direct licences with resellers representing roughly 73% of mortgage reseller volume, and its 10T Adopter Program covers 84 lenders with $684bn of eligible annual originations, with the Federal Housing Administration making 10T available from 1 January 2027. Conforming-market use still awaits enterprise sign-off. "10T is the best score available," Lansing said on 4 November. "Everyone is waiting for GSEs to sign off finally so that the conforming market can use it."

This is not a sector being marked down for software disruption. Over the same thirty days to 23 November, Vertex, which sells corporate indirect-tax software and raised full-year guidance on e-invoicing growth, rose 16.4%; Tyler Technologies, the public-sector software vendor, rose 4.2%; Q2 Holdings, which sells digital banking to community institutions and lifted guidance on 12% revenue growth, fell 4.1%. Over twelve months those three are down between 6% and 10.2%. BlackLine, which automates the accounting close, is the other broken name at -54%, and its cause is its own: a 25.1% one-session fall on 6 November after guiding fourth-quarter revenue to $195m to $197m against consensus near $207.8m, with dollar-based net revenue retention of 103.5%.

FICO's de-rating, then, is not a multiple catching down to a weak business. It is the market repricing one line of a strong one: a royalty that behaved like a regulated toll and now faces a competing bid at a tenth of the price on the same pull. How much of Scores' 41% growth repeats depends on how many lenders enrol in a program whose terms the company has just said it will stop disclosing.

The next twelve months of that negotiation will happen in private, by design. Investors will learn the outcome the way they learned this one: from a revenue line, after the fact.
