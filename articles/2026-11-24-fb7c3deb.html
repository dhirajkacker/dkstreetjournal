Fervo Contracted Its Geothermal Power at the $95 a Megawatt-Hour Ormat Already Bills

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Two geothermal companies now disclose enough to price the same product — firm, round-the-clock power — and the rate turns out to be nearly identical. What differs is volume and funding.

Ormat's operating fleet billed $99.6 per megawatt-hour in the third quarter, a figure derived from its quarterly filing. Fervo's binding book of 1,054 MW and $11.9bn of contracted revenue implies roughly the same rate, from a business that recorded $116,000 of electricity revenue in the quarter.

The split has a cause. Ormat's geothermal segment shrank and its gross margin fell to 22.2%, while its battery business grew 145% and carried most of the quarter's profit improvement. Fervo's month of gains came from reaching commercial operation ahead of contract, against a share price still down 70% from its May debut.

Ormat Technologies, the Reno geothermal group that owns and operates power plants, sells turbines to third-party developers and runs battery storage, and Fervo Energy, the Houston enhanced-geothermal developer that drills horizontal wells to make hot rock produce, both reported third-quarter results in the first half of November. Between the two sets of numbers, the price of a firm American geothermal megawatt-hour is now legible at both ends of the industry — from the already-drilled fleet and from the construction site — and the two are selling at close to the same rate.

The data-center bid for clean power that runs all day has been read as a re-rating event for geothermal. The quarters say the clearing price is already set near $100 and is sitting in one company's revenue line, while at the other it is a schedule. That changes what each stock is actually exposed to: at Ormat, the mix of segments; at Fervo, a financing that has not closed.

One megawatt-hour, two states of matter

Ormat's third-quarter filing shows electricity revenue of $514.5m on 5,476,168 megawatt-hours for the nine months to 30 September, a realized $93.9 per megawatt-hour. Backing the six-month figures out of the nine-month ones leaves a third quarter of $163.6m on 1,642,807 megawatt-hours, or $99.6 — the year's best quarterly rate, and a seasonal summer one. Against $92.6 in 2025 and $94.3 in 2024, the contract book has barely moved.

"PPA pricing today are well above $100 per megawatt hour. We do not see any trend of reducing this pricing," chief executive Doron Blachar told investors on 5 November. The catch is tenor: Ormat's plants sell substantially all output under contracts averaging about fourteen years remaining, and 190 MW running to 2031-2034 is priced at a weighted average near $86. Sixty megawatts have been repriced under blend-and-extend amendments, with roughly 200 MW of recontracting upside through 2034.

Fervo, meanwhile, guides 2027 revenue of $60-80m. Against roughly 100 MW of Cape Station Phase I at a 90% capacity factor — 788,400 megawatt-hours — that is $76 to $101 per megawatt-hour. Its $11.9bn binding book across 1,054 MW implies about $95 on a fifteen-year, 90%-availability assumption. The same price, on about 0.5% of Ormat's volume.

Batteries carried Ormat's quarter

The weakest line at Ormat is the geothermal one. Electricity revenue fell 2.1% in the quarter and its gross margin dropped to 22.2% from 25.4%, hurt by a hurricane-related outage at Puna that cost $5.1m of revenue; excluding Puna, margin would have been about 24.5%. Energy storage grew 145.2% to $49.9m at a 62.1% margin on strong PJM merchant pricing, producing roughly $31.0m of the $77.4m consolidated gross profit from 18% of revenue. The product segment shrank 1.2%, and its backlog fell to $183m from $202.8m.

Consolidated revenue still rose 10.2% to $275.1m and full-year guidance went up, to $1.17-1.2bn of revenue and $640-650m of adjusted EBITDA, with 1,358 MW of electricity capacity. "We expect to see an increase in margin already in Q4, and we expect to see a jump already in 2027," chief financial officer Assi Ginzburg said on the same call. Consensus does not share the optimism past then: 2027 revenue is modelled 3.5% lower than 2026, with earnings per share down 6.0%.

What $1.3bn buys at Fervo

Fervo's news is delivery. GeoBlock 1 at Cape Station reached commercial operation on 30 September, ahead of contract, 33 MW after 23 months of construction. The binding book grew 60% from the May listing, half of it hyperscalers and data centers; the Google agreement is 396 MW grid-connected on a fifteen-year take-or-pay with mid-2028 delivery. On 18 November Fervo added a 105 MW firm transmission agreement with Longroad Energy, taking contracted transmission to 505 MW from 2028.

Costs are falling where they must. Early Nevada pilot wells took 70 days to 11,220 feet; Phase I averaged 21 days to 14,483 feet, with the first four horizontal wells at Cape dropping from $9.4m to $4.8m apiece. "Our long-term target remains at $3,000 per kilowatt... we are positioned to achieve $5,500 per kilowatt of installed CapEx for GeoBlocks 4 through 11," chief executive Timothy Latimer said on 9 November, against about $7,000 for Phase I. Second-half capital spending guidance was cut to $700-750m from $850-900m while the 2028 schedule held.

What is not settled is the money. Fervo ended the quarter with $1.9bn of unrestricted cash against $301.6m of debt, facing $450-500m of fourth-quarter capex and about $2.2bn on Cape Google 400 through 2028, with project financing expected mid-2027 at roughly 70% loan-to-value. At a $3.18bn market capitalization and 1.14x book, the market is paying something near $1.3bn above the cash for the contracted megawatts.

How much the quarters explain

Fervo rose 35.5% over the month to 23 November, most of it on 9 November, when the results lifted it 26.1% in a session. That month is explained by company news, and it came against a power complex de-rating after the midterms on tighter data-center permitting odds, with SMR down 34.6% and Vistra down 10.5%. What the month does not explain is the year: Fervo remains 70% below its 13 May first close of $36.54, and neither it nor Ormat has broken the downtrend its moving averages have shown since the summer. On 20 November, as the S&P 500 tracker fell 3.6%, Fervo gave back 10.3% in one session.

Ormat went nowhere over the same thirty days and is down 21% over twelve months. Its trailing EV/EBITDA has compressed to 15.8x from 19.6x in late September and forward earnings to 31.1x from about 36x, still above the 10-12x typical of owners of contracted power, with trailing free cash flow negative. The group is growing; the geothermal fleet inside it is not, and the repricing that would change that arrives in 60 MW increments against a 190 MW block locked near $86 for another five years.

Blachar, asked about enhanced geothermal on 5 November, gave the test both companies now face: "The question that matters is not whether initial flow can be achieved. It is whether a project can sustain temperature, flow, water balance, and well integrity over many years with seismicity safely managed at the cost the PPA supports." Fervo's own pilot, three and a half years old, is already showing the thermal decline it was built to measure.

Originating hypothesis

category emerging bull per mwh unit economics · subject: ORA, FRVO

Geothermal power is the one tech-adjacent energy shelf on this universe snapshot turning UP at gradual intensity while the AI-infrastructure complex de-rates around it — the segment is +17.6% over 30 days against -46.6% over twelve months, and the turn is still unconfirmed in the bands, with Fervo having held a strongly bearish band for 115 consecutive days since 29 July even as it rallies — and the question is mechanical rather than directional: establish what one megawatt-hour of firm, round-the-clock geothermal actually pays each of these two businesses today, since Ormat earns contracted PPA revenue from an owned, already-drilled operating fleet plus a separate equipment-and-product backlog, while Fervo Energy is a development-stage enhanced-geothermal driller whose entire value sits in contracted-but-unbuilt capacity and a drilling cost-per-well curve that has to keep falling, so the same "data centers will pay for 24/7 clean firm power" narrative can be already-billed electricity revenue at one name and an unfunded construction schedule at the other.
