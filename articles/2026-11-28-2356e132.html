Chilean Inflation Sets Santander Chile's Margin, and the Bank Expects Less Next Year

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Chile's central bank has not moved the policy rate since July, yet Santander Chile's quarterly profit swung from a 40% year-on-year gain in the second quarter to a 4.2% decline in the third. The variable doing the work is the inflation adjustment on a disclosed net gap of CLP 7.9tn in indexed instruments, which the bank enlarged in the second quarter to capture the inflation rebound and then cut.

That makes the margin a function of Chilean consumer prices, and management has told investors to expect less of them: 2027 guidance assumes 3.1% indexed inflation, with return on equity of 22-24% against more than 24% this year. Banco de Chile quantifies the same lever at CLP 91.1bn of net financial income per 100 basis points of inflation and sees roughly 20 basis points of margin going away.

Both carry the regional group's richest book multiples, 3.16x and 3.37x against Itaú Unibanco's 2.25x, into a headwind they forecast themselves.

Santander Chile's third-quarter profit fell by well over a third from the second quarter, and Chile's central bank never touched the policy rate. The lender, a Santiago retail, middle-market and corporate bank majority-owned by Spain's Santander, earned CLP 237.1bn in the quarter against CLP 382.6bn in the one before, and the drop was 4.2% measured against a year earlier.

The quarter was read in New York as deterioration in a bank whose returns had been among the best in Latin America. It was something narrower. The policy rate has sat at 4.50% since well before the unanimous October hold, the fifth-plus consecutive meeting without a change. What moved was the inflation adjustment: the variation in Chile's unidad de fomento, the inflation-indexed accounting unit that reprices a large slice of Chilean bank assets, dropped from 2.46% in the second quarter to 0.58% in the third, and net interest and readjustment income fell 18.1% sequentially with it.

The CLP 7.9tn line that moves the quarter

The size of the lever is disclosed. Santander Chile reported a net gap in inflation-indexed instruments of CLP 7,906,527m at the end of September, against an interest-earning asset yield of 7.6%, cost of funds of 3.8% and a year-to-date net interest margin of 4.1%. The bank does not hold that position passively: it widened the gap during the second-quarter inflation rebound to capture the higher print, then reduced it in the third.

Underneath the inflation trade sits the part of the franchise that does not depend on a price index. Year-to-date efficiency was 33%, an improvement of 290 basis points on a year earlier, and deposits grew 9% year to date against loan growth of 3.9%. "I think the first position in the Chilean industry is still safely on our side," Cristian Vicuña, head of strategy and investor relations, told investors on November 4.

Management's own 2027 assumptions are where the mechanism turns. The bank guided to 3.1% indexed inflation, a flat 4.5% policy rate, net interest margin of about 4.0% and return on equity of 22-24%, below the more than 24% it expects for 2026. Consensus still models 2027 earnings growth of 4.3%. The macro backdrop it guides into is thin: "Mining fell to its lowest level since 1996 in August due to lower ore grades, maintenance, and weather," chief economist Andrés Sansone said on the same call, with mining output now expected to contract 6% to 8% this year.

Banco de Chile holds the same lever and a worse consumer book

Banco de Chile, the 1893-founded commercial bank that is the country's other large listed lender, puts a number on it directly: 100 basis points of inflation is worth CLP 91.1bn of net financial income, while 100 basis points of interest rates moves margin by only about 30 basis points, and only after roughly three years of full repricing. Its readjustment income contracted 64.6% in the third quarter; third-quarter net income was 1.8% below a year earlier on revenue up 8.8%. It guides 2026 inflation at 4.7% falling to 3% in 2027, an effect it sizes at roughly 20 basis points of margin.

It also has the weaker credit trend of the two, with credit losses up 21.2% year on year on retail delinquency, even though its 0.96% third-quarter cost of risk remains well inside Santander Chile's 1.4% year to date. Yet Banco de Chile carries the higher valuation on the lower return: 3.37x trailing book against Santander Chile's 3.16x, with full-year return on average capital and reserves guided to 22%. Both sit above Itaú Unibanco at 2.25x and Credicorp at 2.51x.

Twelve months in pesos, twelve months in dollars

In Santiago, nothing has broken. Santander Chile's local shares rose 19.1% over twelve months and Banco de Chile's 10.8%. The American depositary receipts returned 7.1% and 0.3% over the same dates, a gap of roughly ten percentage points that is the peso, which closed at 1,038 per dollar on November 27 after copper fell 21.5% from its September peak. The dollar proxy for the whole market, the iShares MSCI Chile ETF, fell 8.7%.

So the flat dollar year is currency, and the earnings record contradicts it: Santander Chile's year-to-date return on equity was 24.5%. The three-month slide in both receipts, however, has a genuine companion in the accounts, because the third quarter is the first in which the indexed adjustment worked against the margin rather than for it. What has not happened is any de-rating in pesos. Investors are paying the regional set's richest book multiples for returns that both managements expect to be lower next year, for a reason neither bank controls.

Chile's central bank projects headline inflation back near 3% by the second quarter of 2027, which is target achieved for the country and a shrinking revenue line for its two largest listed lenders. Every quarter from here, the number that decides how much these banks earn will be published by the statistics office before either of them reports.
