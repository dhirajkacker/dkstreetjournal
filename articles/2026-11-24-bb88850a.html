Synopsys Books Less of Its Revenue on Contract Than Cadence Does Since Buying Ansys

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The two companies that split the market for chip-design software were marked down together this month on one question — whether the artificial-intelligence buildout keeps funding design budgets. Their own filings say the contracted dollar sits at the smaller of the two.

Recurring revenue was 79% of Cadence's September quarter, its backlog is a record $8.2bn, and 58% of that converts to revenue within twelve months. At Synopsys, time-based products fell to 40% of July-quarter revenue from 53% a year earlier, because the Ansys simulation book it bought recognizes multi-year lease licenses at lease commencement; 49% of its backlog converts inside a year, and it carries $10.0bn of senior notes.

Business momentum and the cheaper earnings multiple sit at opposite names: Cadence raised guidance and accelerated, while Synopsys's organic tool growth slowed to 8.5%.

The software duopoly that designs the world's semiconductors was marked down this month on a single question: whether hyperscale computing budgets keep paying for chip design. What the two companies' filings show is that the more contracted of the pair is the smaller one. Cadence Design Systems, which sells simulation, synthesis and sign-off tools plus Palladium emulation hardware and design IP, booked 79% of its September-quarter revenue as recurring and 21% upfront, against 82% and 18% a year earlier.

That matters because the ranking is usually read the other way. Synopsys, the larger business, has long been described as the ratable one: multi-year time-based licenses for its electronic-design-automation tools, with hardware and silicon IP as the lumpy edge. Its third-quarter filing says time-based products were 40% of the July quarter's $2.477bn, down from 53% of revenue a year earlier, with upfront products at 27%. The de-rating is landing on already-signed backlog at one name and on delivery-timed revenue at the other.

Ansys moved the Synopsys dollar off the ratable line

The mechanism is in the acquired company's own accounting. Ansys recognizes its multi-year subscription lease licenses up front at lease commencement, a practice Ansys said makes revenue "more volatile" and the reason it reported annual contract value separately. Synopsys closed the deal in 2025; Ansys contributed $622.2m of the third quarter's revenue increase, and roughly $2.98bn of guided fiscal-2026 revenue.

The rest of the dollar moved with it. Of Synopsys's $10.9bn backlog, excluding $1.9bn of non-cancellable commitments, about 49% is expected in revenue over the next twelve months — nine percentage points less forward conversion than Cadence. Gross margin was 72.6% in the quarter against 78.1% a year earlier. Organic growth in the design-software business slowed to 8.5%, a deceleration Morgan Stanley had flagged in a downgrade, and the $10.0bn of senior notes that financed the purchase cost $429.3m of interest over nine months. Design IP did return to growth, up about 11%.

None of this is order weakness. The sequential dip in backlog was attributed to the sale of the processor IP business, which GlobalFoundries completed on 2 June 2026.

Cadence's upfront line is building supply into orders

Cadence's lumpy segment is doing the opposite of clearing. Its chief financial officer commentary says inventories were raised specifically to work through a significant emulation order backlog, with no current capacity or supply constraints. "Q3 revenue grew 22% year-over-year, with strong double-digit growth across all product categories, driven by growing momentum from AI-driven designs," John Wall, chief financial officer, said on 26 October. Recurring revenue grew 18%, double the prior year's 11%, and full-year guidance went to 21% revenue growth with the earnings target up $0.30 to $8.40.

China, the exposure usually invoked to explain either discount, is the larger line at Cadence — 16% of the quarter, down from 18% — against 10% at Synopsys.

Both are also digesting a simulation deal. Cadence completed its purchase of Hexagon's Design & Engineering business on 23 February 2026 for about €2.7bn, adding roughly $160m of 2026 revenue at minimal operating profit and about 28 cents of dilution before turning accretive in 2027.

The cheaper earnings sit with the lumpier revenue

Cadence trades at 31.2x consensus fiscal-2027 earnings of $9.85, against roughly 50x forward in late July. Synopsys is at 22.7x the 2027 consensus of $18.84.

Shares in both are well below their highs of the past year — Synopsys by 20%, Cadence by 26% — so the markdown predates November. The month's move is concentrated in two sessions: both fell about 8% on 20 November, when the Nasdaq Composite wiped out its 2026 gain and Nvidia dropped 14% despite record revenue, then rebounded 8% to 9% on 23 November. No disclosure specific to either company sits on those dates.

The quantified bear case is a customer-budget case rather than a backlog case: Bank of America estimates that holding cloud spending near current levels implies roughly a 20% cut to projected capital expenditure and 35% to 40% downside to artificial-intelligence semiconductor earnings. That would reach tool renewals eventually. It has not reached them yet: Cadence's $4.2bn of revenue expected from contracted obligations over the next twelve months is already signed, core tools grew 20%, IP 24% and system design 30%, and the board authorized a further $2.5bn of repurchases after buying $400m of stock in the quarter.

So the results account for Synopsys's discount and not for Cadence's. Synopsys pays for its lower multiple with mix, margin and leverage; its headline 42% revenue growth is acquisition arithmetic sitting on 8.5% organic expansion. Cadence pays a premium for a dollar that arrives on contract, and that dollar grew faster this quarter than last. The demand both managements point to is physical. "TSMC showed their process road map and said in the next 5 years, the chip size will go up by 48x. 48x," chief executive Anirudh Devgan told investors on 26 October, explaining why verification hardware keeps selling.

Synopsys has not spoken to the market since 26 August, when chief executive Sassine Ghazi said "AI is driving unprecedented complexity and increasing demand for the silicon IP and engineering solutions necessary to deliver next-generation AI compute." Its fourth-quarter and fiscal-year results land after the close on 8 December — the first test of whether the organic tool growth management promised would reach double digits in the quarter actually did.
