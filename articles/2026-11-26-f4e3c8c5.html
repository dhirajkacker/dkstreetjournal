Cisco's Growth Hit a 15-Year High and the Shares Fell Through the Guidance Raise

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Enterprise networking was marked down through November on the belief that the artificial-intelligence premium was deflating out of switch demand. The reported quarters point the other way: the selling scaled with AI exposure and skipped the companies furthest from it.

Cisco grew revenue 24% to $18.52bn in the October quarter, lifted operating margin to 30.2% from 22.6%, raised its forecast for the year to July 2027 by $2.5bn and now guides hyperscaler AI infrastructure revenue to $9bn. Its shares still fell 15% over the 30 days to 25 November, nearly all of it before the numbers were public.

The business explains none of that. What it does explain is the split: Cisco gave up 350 basis points of product gross margin to hardware mix and memory costs, while Extreme Networks, with components locked through calendar 2027, expanded margin and raised guidance, and Ubiquiti grew 39.9% with margin up too.

Cisco told investors on 12 November that it had just grown faster than at any point in more than 15 years, and by then its shares had already done most of their falling. Revenue of $18.52bn for the quarter ended 24 October was up 24% from a year earlier, operating margin reached 30.2% against 22.6%, and the company lifted its revenue forecast for the year to July 2027 by $2.5bn, to $74.8-75.8bn.

"In Q1, we delivered record quarterly revenue of $18.5 billion, up 24% year-over-year and record non-GAAP earnings per share of $1.40, up 40% year-over-year. These are our strongest top and bottom line growth rates in over 15 years," chair and chief executive Chuck Robbins said on the 12 November call. The stock closed 5.1% lower the following session.

November's markdown across enterprise networking was read as the artificial-intelligence premium draining out of switching and routing, the incumbent vendors assumed to be losing the rack to merchant silicon. The reported quarters describe something narrower. The selling scaled with AI exposure and skipped the businesses furthest from it: over the 30 days to 25 November, Ubiquiti, which ships UniFi gateways, Wi-Fi and switching outright through distributors and online retailers, rose 6.6%, and Extreme Networks, a campus and edge vendor mid-migration into subscription, rose 1.8%, while Cisco fell 15.4% and Arista Networks, whose switches go into hyperscaler AI fabrics, fell 16.9%.

The campus grew; the AI book grew three times faster

Cisco's product revenue rose 32% in the quarter. Networking was up 43% with triple-digit growth in AI infrastructure, Nexus data-centre switches for AI up 47%, and the ordinary enterprise refresh that funds the whole franchise kept growing: campus up 14%, enterprise orders up 15%, public sector up 13%. Service-provider and cloud orders rose 47%. Total product orders were up 21% year over year, with subscriptions supplying 44% of revenue, and the contracted book got bigger in the quarter the shares were marked down: remaining performance obligations of $46.2bn, up 8%, against deferred revenue of $30.1bn.

Hyperscaler AI infrastructure revenue is now guided to $9bn for the year, raised from $7.5bn a quarter earlier and more than double the prior year, on 18 design wins across five quarters. "So the top line moving from 12% all the way up to 19% and the bottom line moving from 14% growth to 27% growth," chief financial officer Mark Patterson told analysts of the raise.

What the AI ports cost, and who avoided paying

One line moved the wrong way. Non-GAAP product gross margin came in at 63.7%, down 350 basis points, which Cisco attributed to a heavier hardware mix — hardware revenue grew 50% — and to elevated memory costs. The input shock behind that is severe: the contract price of a 16Gb DDR5 chip went from $8.70 in October 2025 to $48 by September 2026. Cisco signed a multiyear memory agreement and reported no material supply constraints.

Extreme bought earlier. "Our components are secured through calendar 2027 while our competitors are quoting extended lead times," chief executive Edward Meyercord said on 28 October. Its gross margin expanded to 62.1% from 60.6%, product revenue rose 12% in a seventh consecutive double-digit quarter, software-as-a-service annual recurring revenue reached $264.7m, up 22%, and management raised full-year growth guidance from 8% to 10%. Ubiquiti, which holds no earnings calls and carries no recurring-revenue line at all, grew quarterly revenue 39.9% to $1,026.2m with gross margin up 131 basis points to 47.3%; enterprise technology supplied $941.3m of it, roughly 92%, on a payroll of 1,818.

The selling came before the numbers

Cisco went from $111.38 on 27 October to $93.85 on 6 November, six days before it reported anything. The datable catalyst in between was a valuation call rather than an order-book event: on 5 November Morgan Stanley cut the stock to Equal Weight from Overweight and its target to $120 from $135, arguing AI-driven growth was already priced and the AI margin mix left less upside. Cisco fell 7% that day, Arista 10.8%, Ciena 15%. On 20 November the group was hit again together, Ubiquiti down 10.9% and Extreme 9.2% in a single session.

So the results account for none of Cisco's decline and the guidance accounts for less. What the quarters do account for is the split inside the group. Cisco is repriced as a hyperscaler supplier because it has chosen to be one, and it books those ports at a margin concession it has disclosed; Extreme and Ubiquiti sell into campus budgets that are still spending and had their components bought in advance. Cisco now trades at 17.1x forward earnings against 24.9x trailing, against roughly 28x after its May advance, with consensus earnings of $5.51 a share for the year sitting inside the company's own raised range. Extreme carries the group's widest compression, 17.7x forward against 60.9x trailing, on a GAAP operating margin that nearly doubled. Ubiquiti is the dearest of the three on a margin-neutral measure, at 19.9x forward price to gross profit against Cisco's 7.7x, and its forward estimates rest on a single analyst.

Patterson told investors to "expect a slight gross margin headwind as we move through FY 2027 as we address these very high growth opportunities." That is the sentence the next three quarters will test. Cisco has told the market what the $9bn of AI revenue costs it; the open question is whether the campus base keeps growing fast enough to pay for it.
