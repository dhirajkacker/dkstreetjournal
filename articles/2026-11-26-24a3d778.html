DigitalOcean Earns $18m a Year Per Megawatt and the Next Generation Costs $40m to Build

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A cloud provider that raised its growth commitment has lost more than half its market value since May, and the argument against it is no longer about demand. DigitalOcean's September quarter grew revenue 39.9%, the third straight acceleration, annual run-rate revenue reached $1.285bn and management lifted 2027 guidance to 60%-plus growth from 50%-plus.

The cost side is where the case gets harder. Gross margin has fallen for four consecutive quarters to 50.2% as new data-center and graphics-processor capacity switches on, and management's own unit figure is $18m of run-rate revenue per average active megawatt against roughly $40m of capital spending per megawatt for the Nvidia generation it is evaluating. Remaining performance obligations slipped to $800m from $894m three months earlier.

NetApp's squeeze is different: memory it buys, with prices locked through the fiscal year.

DigitalOcean, a New York cloud provider that sells metered compute, storage and managed databases to developers, start-ups and small businesses, told investors in early November that it expects to grow revenue by 60% or more in 2027, raising a commitment it had set at 50%-plus. Since then the shares have gone one way. The argument moved off the revenue line and onto what revenue costs to install.

The markdown across artificial-intelligence infrastructure in late November has been read as capital-market pushback against buildout spending rather than evidence of empty data centers. DigitalOcean is the cleanest test of that reading available, because it publishes the two numbers the argument turns on.

What a megawatt pays and what one costs

Management's unit metric is $18m of annual run-rate revenue per average active megawatt across the fleet, with chief executive Padmanabhan Srinivasan telling investors on the 3 November call that recent deals are landing above $20m per megawatt. Against that, the company is evaluating Vera Rubin generation equipment at roughly $40m of capital spending per megawatt. Capacity committed stands at about 180 megawatts: 75 active, 75 for 2027, 30 for 2028, after Richmond, Kansas City and Memphis came online ahead of schedule.

That arithmetic shows up in the margin. Gross margin has compressed for four straight quarters, from 58.7% in December 2025 to 50.2% in September, as new sites and graphics-processor capacity switch on ahead of the revenue they will carry. Everything above that line accelerated: revenue of $321.2m grew 39.9%, after 28.6% and 22.4% in the two prior quarters; a record $160m of incremental run-rate revenue took total annual run-rate revenue to $1.285bn, up 40%; artificial-intelligence customer run-rate revenue rose 288% to $376m. Adjusted EBITDA was $144m, a 45% margin against a 38-39% guide.

The demand is also less GPU-dependent than the label suggests. More than 90% of AI customer run-rate revenue comes from accounts that also consume core cloud primitives, roughly $900m of core-cloud run-rate revenue sits outside the AI tailwind, and the top 25 customers are 27% of revenue with no single account reaching high single digits.

The one metric going the wrong way

Remaining performance obligations - contracted revenue not yet recognized - fell to $800m at 30 September from $894m three months earlier, with $345m expected within twelve months. A year earlier the figure was $61m, so the book is vastly larger than it was and smaller than last quarter. That is the single disclosed support for a softening-backlog reading.

The funding question is quieter than it was. DigitalOcean repurchased about $472m of its 2030 convertible notes in July, booking a $57m induced-conversion charge that produced a GAAP net loss of $42.6m for the quarter, is retiring the remaining $312m of 2026 notes, and holds $1.2bn of liquidity at 1.2x net leverage with a $785m equipment-lease facility arranged in September. "Our balance sheet is a competitive advantage for us right now," chief financial officer Matt Steinfort said on 3 November.

The shares had already turned down before any of this was disclosed: the trend broke to a decline in late October, nine sessions before the print, and the stock fell more than 8% on 3 November on adjusted earnings of $0.45 against a $0.32 consensus. At $71.72 it is down 55% from May and still up 60% over twelve months. Price-to-trailing-gross-profit is 14.0x against 26.0x in mid-August, a 46% de-rating against gross profit that grew 17.6%; the trailing price-to-earnings ratio is an artifact of the convertible charge and says nothing.

NetApp's squeeze arrives in a purchase order

The other storage name marked down alongside it has a cost problem of a different kind. NetApp, the San Jose data-management company selling all-flash arrays running its ONTAP software plus first-party storage services inside the three large public clouds, reported record July-quarter revenue of $2.03bn, up 30%, with all-flash array revenue of $1.3bn, up 47%, and billings up 36%. "Product gross margin was 54.6%, down 150 basis points sequentially, mainly driven by higher component costs and partially offset by better pricing," chief financial officer Wissam Jabre told investors on 2 September. Management called that quarter roughly the trough and has locked component prices through the fiscal year, with group gross margin guided to 67-68% for the quarter it reports on 1 December against 70.6%. TrendForce has since cut its fourth-quarter NAND contract-price forecast to 8-13% and expects declines in 2027, which would leave those locked prices looking like a peak-cost position. NetApp trades at 16.4x forward earnings against 23.0x trailing, and its price-to-gross-profit has fallen to 6.2x from 8.2x on 22 August.

Neither income statement explains the move. Revenue growth accelerated at both companies and gross profit grew at both, so the de-rating is being applied to the category, not derived from the quarters. What the quarters do leave open differs by company. For NetApp the exposure is a margin event rather than a demand event, and it is contractually bounded for three more quarters. For DigitalOcean the exposure is structural: every megawatt it commits is spent before it earns, and the gap between $18m of revenue and $40m of installed cost per megawatt is precisely the spending the market spent November repricing.

Seventy-five megawatts are scheduled to come alive in 2027. Each one has to find more than $18m of customers.
