Sanmina Can Bill Customers for Parts They Never Use; Jabil Carries the Inventory Itself

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Building server racks against a hyperscaler's forecast means someone buys the memory and boards before the order is firm, and the two largest listed assemblers of that work have settled the question differently. Sanmina's supply agreements make the customer liable for materials ordered to an agreed procurement plan and left unused, and its days of supply held at roughly 100 through a year in which sales grew 79%.

Jabil carries the exposure itself: net inventories reached $7.41bn, a 58.4% rise against 20.6% revenue growth, lifting implied days of supply from about 63 to 83. Neither company's unit economics deteriorated. Incremental gross margin on the added revenue was 10.6% at Sanmina and 10.9% at Jabil, both above group level, and both guided fiscal 2027 to wider margins.

Both shares are down by roughly a third since late September. The income statements do not explain it; the balance sheets explain only part, and only at Jabil.

Sanmina's annual report for the fiscal year ended 3 October contains a sentence that decides who pays for a hyperscaler's change of mind. Customers are typically liable for the cost of materials, tariffs and components the contract manufacturer has ordered to meet the customer's own forecast and not used, provided the purchase followed an agreed procurement plan. No minimum purchase is promised; the parts bill, if the forecast moves, is the customer's.

That clause matters now because the markdown in contract electronics has been read as proof that assembling cloud racks is thin-margin work that swallows working capital. On margin the filings say the opposite. The working-capital half of the charge is real, but it lands at one of these two companies and not the other, and the reason is contractual.

The parts bill that stays with the customer

Sanmina, which builds components, subassemblies and complete systems for industrial, healthcare, defense, telecom and cloud customers, grew fiscal 2026 revenue 78.7% to $14.53bn after buying ZT Systems' data-center manufacturing business from an AMD subsidiary for $1.62bn on 27 October 2025. Inventories rose 81.5% to $3.61bn — a shade faster than sales, leaving derived days of supply roughly flat near 100. Receivables grew more slowly than revenue and cash rose to $1.68bn.

"Revenue grew 79% year-over-year to $14.53 billion. Non-GAAP operating margin expanded from 5.7% operating margin to 7.4% operating margin year-over-year," chairman and chief executive Jure Sola told investors on 9 November. The incremental gross margin on the extra $6.40bn of revenue was 10.6%, above the 9.60% group level. Guidance for fiscal 2027 is $17.5bn-$19.5bn with a 7.0%-7.5% operating margin.

The cost is concentration: two customers at 16.7% and 10.1% of sales, the ten largest at 63% against 52% a year earlier. At $129.91 the shares fetch 4.91x trailing gross profit, against 11.55x in early May, and 7.9x the midpoint of the company's own fiscal 2027 earnings guide.

Jabil holds the memory itself

Jabil, whose plants cover cloud infrastructure, semiconductor capital equipment, automotive, healthcare and packaging, grew revenue 20.6% to $35.95bn with incremental gross margin of 10.9% against a 9.23% group figure and incremental operating margin of 8.5% against 4.74%. Its fiscal 2027 guide is $44.5bn of revenue and a 30 basis point rise in core operating margin to 6.1%, with artificial-intelligence revenue up 54% to about $22.1bn and five customers each above $1bn. The legacy book is not collapsing underneath it: fourth-quarter Regulated Industries revenue rose 9% to $3.4bn and Connected Living held near $1.4bn.

What changed is the balance sheet. Net inventories reached $7.41bn from $4.68bn, a 58.4% rise against 20.6% revenue growth, which lifts implied days of supply from roughly 63 to 83. Some of that is price, not quantity: the contract price of a 16-gigabit DDR5 memory chip went from $8.70 in October 2025 to $48 by September 2026, so parts dollars can swell without a single extra box. Jabil discloses one customer at 20% of revenue and its five largest at 37%. The stock trades at 6.70x trailing gross profit against 12.77x in mid-May.

The peer with no racks fell too

Flex told investors its cloud business is steering away from low-margin standard racks, and reported inventory up 39% with customers participating in the builds. Celestica, contesting the same AMD rack-scale programme from the switch layer, guided 2027 revenue up about 90% to roughly $40bn while cutting 2026 free cash flow to $460m from $600m and calling next year a net use of cash; it trades at 11.92x trailing gross profit.

Plexus settles the argument. It serves healthcare, industrial, aerospace and communications with essentially no hyperscaler rack work, grew revenue 23% to $4.97bn, raised its operating-margin target to 6.5%-7.0% and posted a record 62-day cash conversion cycle, adding $1.5bn of annualized revenue on $250m of working capital. "We took a significant amount of share particularly through the last downturn within the semi cap market," chief executive Todd Kelsey said on 29 October. The shares still fell 13.6% over the following month, and 28.8% from the late-September high, while holding a valuation of 10.75x trailing gross profit — double Sanmina's.

So the per-unit economics test fails. Both protagonists booked the hyperscaler dollar at or above group margin, both raised guidance, and the multiple on gross profit roughly halved at each while gross profit dollars grew 95% at Sanmina and 25% at Jabil. The likelier reading is a re-pricing of how the buildout gets funded, visible across the whole complex: Dell raised its forecast for the third time this year and fell 7.2%. "Investors are focused on the big and rising cost of building AI, so good earnings and impressive earnings forecasts no longer lift sentiment," Swissquote Bank's Ipek Ozkardeskaya said on 25 November.

One open question survives the exercise, and it is Jabil's December quarter. A contract manufacturer holding eighty-odd days of increasingly expensive memory is making a bet on its customers' forecasts that Sanmina, by contract, does not have to make.
