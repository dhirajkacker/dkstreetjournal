Flex Is Paid a Fee to Integrate the AI Rack, and the Margin on That Fee Keeps Rising

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Flex was marked down this autumn as a pass-through assembler whose dollar content per artificial-intelligence rack rises while the money it keeps on that content does not. Its own September quarter says the opposite: group gross profit grew 32.2% on 24.8% revenue growth, and the Cloud & Power Infrastructure segment turned $2.5bn at a 10.7% operating margin against 8.3% a year earlier.

Nobody cut the numbers. Consensus earnings for the March 2027 year sit inside management's raised $4.84-$5.16 guide, yet on that unchanged estimate the shares went from 28.6x in late May to 13.4x. The piece of the story that does deserve a discount is written into the Axiom separation: a $2.0bn preferred struck at a $37.5bn initial enterprise value, paying 10% in cash, redeemable at a premium if the spin misses the end of 2027.

Further up the same coolant loop, Dover is paid per part on shipment and SPX Technologies per megawatt at quote.

Flex gets paid to put the artificial-intelligence rack together, and in the September quarter that fee got better rather than thinner. The company's Cloud & Power Infrastructure segment — power shelves, switchgear, busways, enclosures and, since the JetCool purchase, liquid-cooled racks — booked $2.5bn of revenue, up 54% year on year, at a 10.7% operating margin against 8.3% a year earlier. The power sub-segment alone reached roughly 16%.

That matters because the premise behind this autumn's markdown of everything that supplies the data-center build was that integrators like Flex carry more customer-funded material each year and keep the same sliver of it. Group gross margin is thin, at 9.6%. But it was 9.0% a year earlier, and group gross profit grew 32.2% on revenue growth of 24.8%. The content is rising and so is the take.

The fee is not for a standard rack

Flex's cloud unit is deliberately turning away the commodity end of the work. Management said it is concentrating on custom configuration, vertical integration and aftermarket service while avoiding low-margin standard racks, with cooling products still early and launches planned over the following six months. The hardware backs the claim: Flex's liquid-cooled racks integrate JetCool direct-to-chip cold plates and cool up to 120 kW per rack and beyond, and JetCool has launched a 6U in-rack coolant distribution unit rated at 300 kW and scalable to 2.1 MW at row level. Flex builds the box, not only the frame around it.

It comes with capital attached. Fiscal 2027 capital spending is guided at $1.5-1.6bn, about two-thirds of it for the cloud and power segment, and component shortages are already inside guidance as a multi-year headwind.

"You cannot walk away from the fact that hyperscalers are spending the most amount of money," chief executive Revathi Advaithi told investors on 28 October. "If you want to be able to grow your market share, you have to grow with these hyperscalers."

Three ways to get paid for one coolant loop

The same water that leaves a cold plate passes two other listed cash registers, each on different terms. Dover sells the per-part hardware — quick disconnects through Colder Products, dozens per liquid-cooled rack, and brazed-plate heat exchangers through SWEP — and is paid on shipment: its Climate & Sustainability Technologies segment turned $481.5m in the September quarter, up 18% organically, on bookings of $594.1m, up 43.1%, a book-to-bill of 1.23. Dover trades at 19.2x forward earnings, against 20.2x in late May on the identical estimate; almost nothing moved.

SPX Technologies is paid at the far end, per megawatt, at the price struck when the job is quoted — roughly $100,000 of revenue per megawatt for evaporative cooling and about $200,000 via its dry-cooling system, according to disclosed unit economics — against a heating-and-cooling backlog that closed the quarter at $1,102.5m, up 69% organically. Its forward earnings multiple is 17.1x, down from 26.1x in May on an unchanged estimate.

On price to forward gross profit, the only comparison that survives a 9.6%-margin integrator sitting beside 39-40%-margin equipment makers, Flex is at 7.3x, Dover 7.9x and SPX 6.5x. Parker Hannifin, which competes with Dover for the coupling socket but told investors on 5 November that data centers are about 1.5% of sales, is at 12.1x.

What the discount is actually attached to

Flex has fallen 53% from its 26 May close, including a 13.6% session on 5 November when the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.4% on hyperscaler funding and post-midterm permitting fears. Robert W. Baird cut its target to $84 from $135 on 23 November. Earnings estimates did not follow: consensus of $5.01 for the March 2027 year sits inside the raised $4.84-$5.16 guide, and that guide was lifted by roughly $950m of revenue in October. Hold the estimate still and the multiple went from 28.6x in late May to 13.4x.

So the markdown is price, not profit — but one piece of it has a name. On 2 October Flex, Axiom and General Catalyst signed a $2.0bn Series A preferred agreement, 200,000 shares at $10,000 each, struck at an initial enterprise value for Axiom of $37.5bn and paying a 10% annual cash dividend. Flex guarantees redemption at 115% of purchase price in cash, or 125% in Flex shares, if the separation is not completed by 31 December 2027; the shares fell 6.9% in an afternoon as investors worked through those terms. At its 10 November Innovation Day in Austin, Flex put Axiom's calendar-2026 revenue near $10bn and the separation on track for the first quarter of calendar 2027.

That is the honest split in the de-rating. The operating business is converting the build at widening margins, and Dover's shipment book and SPX's quoted backlog say the loop they all feed is still filling. What repriced in November was the financing and permitting of the halls themselves, and in Flex's case a dated obligation of its own making.

Until the spin clears, the preferred keeps paying 10% in cash, and the calendar keeps running toward a deadline Flex wrote down in a contract.
