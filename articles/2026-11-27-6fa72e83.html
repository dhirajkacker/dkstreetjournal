Subsidized Mexican Buses Are Why Volaris Could Not Pass On Its Fuel Bill and Copa Could

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Latin America's five listed carriers all bought the same dollar-priced jet fuel this year, and the share of the increase each got back from passengers ranged from about 86% at Copa Holdings to 49% at Volaris. The sorting variable is the currency the fare is written in: Copa prices dollar itineraries through Panama with only 15-20% of costs in local money, while Volaris's peso domestic fares are capped by subsidized long-distance buses and Azul's reais lost 15.6% against the dollar after Brazil's October election.

November's rally treated the group as one trade as Brent fell about 22% from its late-October peak. The business evidence splits them: Copa still earned an 18.6% operating margin and cut full-year guidance to 16-17%, Volaris beat its own EBITDA margin guidance at 25.3% on a net loss, and Azul reported record unit revenue with operating income down 32.6%. Copa's shares have re-rated to 10.0x trailing earnings from 7.4x at end-2025 while consensus 2026 earnings fell.

Jet fuel across Latin America reached about $4.61 a gallon by mid-October, 108.6% above a year earlier, taking fuel from 28% of the region's airline operating cost to 38%. What sorted the five listed carriers through that shock was not the fuel bill, which everyone pays in dollars, but the currency the fare is written in. Copa Holdings, which sells connecting itineraries through a single Panama hub where tickets are priced in dollars, recovered roughly 86% of its fuel increase last quarter. Volaris, the Mexican ultra-low-cost carrier, recovered 49%.

The market has been reading the group as one position. Brent is down about 22% from a late-October peak above $117 a barrel to $90.97 on 25 November, and in the 30 days to 27 November every one of the five rose, from LATAM Airlines at 9.5% to Volaris at 36.8%. The recapture numbers describe three different businesses, and the largest gains went to the two that passed on the least.

Dollar in, dollar out

Copa's September-quarter revenue rose 31.9% to $1.204bn, with unit revenue up 13.5% to 12.6 cents and load factor at 88.7%. Its fuel cost rose 61.5%. "Strong demand and higher yields enable us to recover approximately 86% of the increase in fuel-related expense in the quarter through higher revenues," chief financial officer Peter Donkersloot Ponce told investors on 19 November. Only 15-20% of Copa's costs sit in local currencies; the main driver of its 1.1% rise in ex-fuel unit cost was Colombian peso appreciation.

Even so, the operating margin fell to 18.6% from 23.2%, gross profit grew 9.3% on that 31.9% revenue gain, and the company cut 2026 operating-margin guidance to 16-17% on a $3.90-a-gallon fuel assumption. Net debt is 0.8x EBITDA against $1.6bn of cash. The shares now trade at 10.0x trailing and 10.3x forward earnings, against 7.4x at the end of 2025 and 6.0x at the end of 2024 — the forward figure sits above trailing because consensus 2026 earnings of $14.95 a share are 9.7% below last year's $16.28.

A bus timetable sets the Mexican fare ceiling

Volaris crossed $1bn of quarterly revenue for the first time, up 32.4%, with unit revenue up 19% to 10.31 cents and an EBITDA margin of 25.3% against its own 22% guidance. It still lost $26m.

The split inside that number is the story. Transborder US-Mexico revenue grew 53% and fully covered its fuel; peso-denominated domestic fares covered only 20-25%, because subsidized long-distance diesel buses cap what Volaris can charge Mexicans. So the airline is moving revenue into the currency its leases and fuel are in: international reaches 49% of fourth-quarter capacity, and on published fourth-quarter schedules Volaris is the largest carrier in the transborder market by seats. Grounded Pratt & Whitney-powered aircraft averaged 21 in the quarter against 41 at the start of the year, with compensation covering most fixed costs of each engine off wing but not lost revenue. The fleet still shrinks, from 154 to about 137 by end-2027, cutting lease liabilities from $3.2bn to $2.8bn.

The peso gave no help: it weakened from 18.80 to a 19.70 reference rate on 26 November, 8.8% down on the year. At 3.9x trailing EV/EBITDA and 0.26x forward sales, with consensus still modeling losses until 2028, the stock remains below its $8.88 close at end-2025. Mexico's competition authority was reported in October as set to approve the Volaris-Viva Aerobus merger before year-end.

Azul recaptured half, in a currency that fell 15%

Azul produced record unit revenue of BRL0.4723 per available seat kilometer and flat revenue of BRL5.636bn, while operating income fell 32.6% to BRL560.1m. "If you just compare our 22% with our 42% fuel RASK, that's about a 50% recapture already," president Abhi Shah said on 12 November. "Our intention is to recover 100%."

Half of a doubled dollar cost, earned in a currency that lost 15.6% after Lula's 25 October re-election, lands on BRL22.6bn of gross debt whose dollar obligations convert at R$5.18 at an average 9.9% rate. The $552m of listed equity is roughly BRL3.2bn behind that stack. The shares fell 32.6% in the 26 October session on 988,600 shares against roughly 100,000 on a typical day; American Airlines' $100m warrant exercise on 19 October moved them less than 1%. What Azul has is density — 82% of its routes carry no nonstop competitor, and loyalty, cargo and maintenance supply about 20% of unit revenue.

The spectrum holds at the top

The two larger carriers sit where the mechanism predicts. LATAM offset about 80% of a $594m quarterly fuel increase with $410m of incremental pricing and cargo revenue, guides 2026 EBITDA to $3.9-4.1bn, and trades at 10.3x forward earnings — Copa's multiple on roughly half Copa's margin. Grupo Aeroméxico recaptured 90-93% with premium cabins at 42% of revenue; its 81x forward multiple is an earnings trough, 2026 consensus of $0.22 a share rising to $1.55 in 2027.

So the durable work is the recapture, and the November prices are mostly the fuel curve. Volaris went from $5.68 on 23 October to $6.10 after its print, then to $8.68 by 11 November as crude collapsed: the quarter was worth about 7% and oil the rest. Copa's advance is a 35% multiple expansion over 2026 while its trailing earnings fell from $16.28 to $15.30 a share, with consensus assuming a 29% earnings rebound in 2027 that requires the margin squeeze to reverse. Azul's de-rating needs no explaining beyond its own balance sheet.

What none of it settles is whether the fares stay once the fuel does not. "I think it is very sticky, and we will not expect pricing to change that much if fuel comes down, especially if it comes down in a significant way," Copa chief executive Pedro Heilbron said on 19 November, noting that average fares are only about 5% above 2019 before inflation. Every dollar of that stickiness is margin for the carrier billing in dollars, and the test arrives with the next set of unit-revenue numbers.
