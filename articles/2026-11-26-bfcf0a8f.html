Arrow and TD Synnex Earned Wider Margins on Memory Inflation and Burned Cash Doing It

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The three American companies standing between the memory makers and the buyers of computers were marked down through November on the belief that cost inflation is crushing the channel's spread. Their September and October quarters say the opposite at the operating line, and the real bill shows up on the cash flow statement.

Arrow Electronics held gross margin flat at 10.82% and more than doubled operating income, then built inventory $1.4bn sequentially to $7.3bn and used $441m of operating cash. TD Synnex expanded operating margin to 2.98% while burning $975.6m of free cash flow in a single quarter, with interest and finance charges up a third. PC Connection shipped roughly 5% fewer notebooks for 26.6% more device revenue.

Arrow finances the build cheaply on falling debt; TD Synnex does not. Connection, the dearest of the three, has barely been marked down at all.

Memory prices more than doubled this year, and the three companies that stand between the chipmakers and the buyers of computers all got larger and more profitable passing the cost along. The bill arrived somewhere other than the income statement.

Arrow Electronics, TD Synnex and PC Connection have been marked down hard, Arrow by 19.9% in the 30 days to 25 November, TD Synnex by 12.8% and Connection by 7.1%, on a reading that a rising bill of materials is compressing the channel's spread. The reported quarters do not support that where it would show first: all three expanded operating margin. What the pass-through actually cost them is in working capital, and there the three have nothing in common.

From 16% of a parts bill to 23%

J.P. Morgan Global Research estimates DRAM prices will rise more than 400% from the start of 2024 to the end of 2026, because data centres will absorb over 70% of high-end memory output in 2026 and the three makers of more than 95% of dynamic random-access memory are diverting wafers to high-bandwidth memory for accelerators. Gartner puts personal-computer memory at 23% of a machine's total bill of materials, up from 16% in 2025, with average prices up 17%. Worldwide shipments fell 22.4% in the third quarter.

Arrow's spread held and its warehouse filled

Arrow, which distributes semiconductors and interconnect parts to industrial and embedded manufacturers and sells data centre systems to resellers, reported record revenue of $10.72bn for the quarter to 3 October, up 39%, with gross margin flat at 10.82% against 10.83% a year earlier. Operating income rose 140% to $430.3m. Components sales of $7.9bn carried a 5.2% operating margin, 160 basis points better than a year earlier, and management said most of that growth came from memory price inflation it does not expect to repeat.

"If we had gotten our hands on more product, we would have sold more," chief financial officer Rajesh Agrawal told investors on 5 November. "And so the environment itself is very, very strong." Interim chief executive William Austen said book-to-bill ratios remain well above parity and backlog "continues to build into mid-2027."

Getting hands on product is the expensive part.

Inventory rose $1.4bn sequentially to $7.3bn, nearly half of it data-centre related and about $600m of it for the unit that assembles compute and storage racks, and Arrow used $441m of cash in operations in the quarter, leaving year-to-date operating cash flow at $577m. It is funding that cheaply: interest and other expense was $40m on lower average debt, which fell $378m year on year to $2.8bn. The shares trade at 7.2x forward earnings against 8.6x trailing, though the forward figure rests on consensus 2026 earnings of $24.02 a share, up 131%, after annual revenue fell 15.7% in 2024.

TD Synnex billed faster than it could fund

TD Synnex nets roughly 32% of what it bills out of reported revenue, so the $31.8bn it billed in the quarter to 31 August became $21.56bn of revenue, up 37.7%. Gross margin slipped to 6.61% from 6.73%; operating margin still rose to 2.98% from 2.47%. Hyve, its hyperscale build-and-integrate arm, more than doubled billings to $7.0bn, about 22% of the group, earning a 3.61% non-GAAP operating margin on those billings against 1.95% in Distribution, down from 5.04% a year earlier as artificial-intelligence infrastructure took more of the mix.

Free cash flow was negative $975.6m for the quarter, and interest and finance charges rose 32% to $120.5m. Management said in September that it expects positive cash flow in the fourth quarter and that all its businesses should become sustainable cash generators in fiscal 2027. The shares trade at 10.3x forward earnings on a trailing free cash flow yield of minus 4.68%.

Connection sold fewer notebooks for more money

PC Connection, a reseller serving small businesses, large corporates and government and education buyers, grew net sales 23.3% to $874.3m while gross margin fell 104 basis points to 18.51%. Its finance chief attributed half the decline to a thinner mix of netted-down software and half to endpoint hardware sold into large rollouts. Notebook units fell about 5% as device revenue rose 26.6%. Public Sector Solutions managed 4.6% growth, to $138.6m, against 27.8% in the small-business arm.

A $144.8m receivables build and $83m of inventory for large deployments took nine-month operating cash flow to minus $39.8m, against $343.3m of cash on hand. "We think that endpoint demand might be down slightly in '27 from '26," chief executive Timothy McGrath said on 28 October. At 18.4x forward earnings, roughly 2.5 times Arrow's multiple, Connection is the most expensively valued of the three, and consensus already has its revenue growth falling from 13.6% this year to 3.7% next.

One session did most of the marking down

On 5 November the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 12.2% after midterm voters backed measures to slow data centre construction; Arrow closed down 10.4%, Connection 9.9% and TD Synnex 2.6%. Across the 30 days the distributors fell less than the companies on either side of them, with Micron down 33.5%, Dell 36.4% and Hewlett Packard Enterprise 34.8%, while CDW rose 2.1%. The likelier reading is that the group was repriced for artificial-intelligence hardware demand rather than for anything in its own spreads.

The spreads are the part that held. What the market has not marked is the financing: the same cost shock that inflates revenue obliges a distributor to buy the inventory and carry the receivable first, which is why TD Synnex's interest bill grew by a third while Arrow's shrank, and why Connection funded other people's rollouts off its own balance sheet. Arrow's position is the sturdier, with a backlog, falling debt and an assembly book it expects to ship; TD Synnex's is the more exposed, because Hyve programmes consume cash before they bill. Arrow's fourth-quarter guide of $11.55bn to $12.15bn assumes unit volume, management having said component price increases were completed in the third quarter, and TrendForce now sees conventional contract prices rising 10% to 15% in the fourth quarter after far steeper increases earlier in the year.

If the price tailwind fades on that schedule, the revenue it created leaves the income statement on its own, and the $7.3bn of inventory stays in the warehouse waiting for someone to want it at cost.
