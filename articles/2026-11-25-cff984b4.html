MongoDB's Customer Commitments Grew Three Times Faster Than Its Revenue

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The standing objection to selling a database by the hour is that no customer has promised to keep the meter running. MongoDB's own filing says otherwise: contracted obligations reached $1.52bn at the end of July, up 91% against revenue growth of 30%, and the artificial-intelligence startups that could switch off abruptly are 3% to 5% of its cloud revenue.

Teradata, which sells the opposite model, is converting an on-premise subscription base into cloud contracts. Cloud annual recurring revenue added about $67m over the year while the non-cloud base shed about $45m, which is why total recurring revenue compounds at 1% to 2% while the cloud line compounds at 11%.

Both businesses firmed up. Only Teradata's shares did, up 32% in 90 days while MongoDB fell 24% through a September guidance raise, a founder's return as interim chief and a market-wide software selloff.

The worry about selling a database by consumption is that nobody has promised to keep using it. MongoDB, which sells a document database both as a licensed server and as the Atlas managed cloud service, answered the objection in its filing for the quarter ended 31 July 2026: remaining performance obligations, the revenue it has contracted but not yet delivered, reached $1,519.2m, up from $794.2m a year earlier. Revenue over the same stretch grew 30%.

That ratio is the whole question for a metered software business, and the market has been trading the other side of it. MongoDB closed at $308.50 on 24 November, down roughly 24% over 90 sessions, on a reading that consumption revenue is the most fragile kind, invoiced only as workloads actually run and cancellable without breaching anything. The disclosures describe something else: a business whose promises are growing about three times faster than its meter.

What backs 73 cents of every MongoDB dollar

Atlas was $565.9m of the $771.8m quarterly total, so roughly three of every four dollars MongoDB books is metered cloud consumption. Atlas grew about 29% and the licence line about 36%, and gross margin widened to 73.8% while free cash flow nearly doubled to $137.6m. Reported operating income swung from a $65.3m loss a year earlier to $28.4m of profit.

The company's 10-Q is candid that timing on that $1.52bn "are generally dependent upon customers' future consumption, which is inherently variable at the customers' discretion." The customers most capable of switching off are a smaller slice than assumed: at its 29 September investor day MongoDB said artificial-intelligence-native customers are 3% to 5% of Atlas revenue, and raised three-year targets to above 20% revenue growth with 80-100% free-cash-flow conversion. "Atlas is completely consumption-driven, as you are fully well aware," then-chief executive CJ Desai told analysts on the Q2 call. Four weeks later he left.

The honest offset is the near term. Full-year guidance was raised to $2.99-3.03bn with Atlas growth lifted to about 27%, but the third-quarter guide of $756-761m implies roughly 20-21% growth and a sequential decline. Acceleration is guided to stop.

Teradata's migration clears its churn by $22m

Teradata sells the inverse: Vantage, a multi-cloud analytics platform bought by banks, governments and telecoms, historically installed on their own hardware. Its unit of revenue is a subscription being moved, not a workload being metered. Total annual recurring revenue was $1.512bn at 30 September, up 1% as reported and 2% in constant currency, with public-cloud recurring revenue of $700m against $633m — cloud now 46% of the base.

Subtracting gives the mechanism. Cloud added about $67m over the year; the residual non-cloud base fell about $45m, or roughly 5%. Migration is outrunning erosion by something near $22m, which is precisely why total recurring revenue compounds at 1-2% while the cloud line compounds at 11%. Quarterly revenue still fell 4.1% to $399m and gross margin narrowed to 59.6%.

What the cash did was different. Year-to-date adjusted free cash flow rose 90% to $254m, full-year earnings guidance went to $2.86-2.90 from a $2.60 midpoint at the start of the year, and adjusted free cash flow was reaffirmed at $330-350m with the midpoint lifted to $340m. "Our hybrid and sovereign capabilities have an unmatched advantage in the marketplace and we remain really confident in our annual outlook," chief executive Stephen McMillan said on 5 November.

How much of the fall the filings explain

For Teradata, cash delivery has set the price. The shares fell 6% to $31.80 the session after the print because revenue missed a $403.66m consensus, then recovered to $35.71, up 32% over 90 sessions at 3.3x forward gross profit and 12.4x forward earnings, a roughly 10% free-cash-flow yield on the guided midpoint.

For MongoDB almost none of the decline is in the filings. Two dated sessions carry it: a 13.5% fall on 2 September, the day after a quarter that grew 30% and raised the guide, and 18.2% on 28 September, when Desai resigned for Meta after about ten months and founder Dev Ittycheria returned as interim chief with third-quarter and full-year guidance expressly reaffirmed. Mizuho and Stifel both kept their ratings. The November leg is harder to pin on the company at all: capital left high-multiple software broadly and selling intensified from 20 November, with the Meritech software index down about 10% over the same stretch. MongoDB's forward price-to-gross-profit of 11.3x now sits below both anchors in its own year, 13.3x in late July and 11.7x in early May, though 47x forward earnings is not cheap in absolute terms.

So two database businesses firmed up on different mechanics, contracted commitments at one and migration arithmetic at the other, and only one of them was paid for it. A governance shock explains a day; it does not explain a quarter of the market value of a company whose obligations nearly doubled.

The arbiter arrives on 1 December, when MongoDB reports the quarter ended 31 October after the close. The guide it set is a deceleration; whether the backlog behind it keeps outrunning the meter is the only line in that release that settles anything.
