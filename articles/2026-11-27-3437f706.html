Logitech Locked In Its Memory for the Year; Corsair Resells It at Half Last Year's Margin

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

One component market, two bookkeeping fates. Corsair's memory line set a third-quarter record and now accounts for 45.6% of group revenue, up from 33.8% a year earlier, while the cases, coolers and power supplies sold beside it shrank about 29% as enthusiasts postponed builds.

Logitech met the same inflation differently: it pre-bought memory for its video-collaboration room systems through fiscal 2027 and raised that line's prices in May, so a 12% constant-currency gain there came roughly 60% from price. Corsair raised full-year revenue guidance and guided fourth-quarter memory gross margin to 18-20% against about 35% last year.

The revenue line flatters the reseller and the margin line indicts it: Corsair's gross profit grew 5.1% on 6.2% revenue growth, with a GAAP operating loss of $1.6m.

Corsair Gaming told investors that its memory business had just posted a record quarter and that the margin it earns on that business will be roughly half last year's. Logitech International, buying into the same component market, described the opposite arrangement: supply bought forward for the year and a price list raised in May. Both sell gaming hardware. Only one of them is reselling the shortage.

The pair has been read as a post-pandemic recovery finally turning up. The quarters say something narrower. At Corsair, the growth in the revenue line is the cost shock, arriving at the expense of everything the same customer used to buy alongside it; at Logitech, the shock is a decision about what to charge. The distinction decides which company's reported growth survives a turn in memory contract prices, which TrendForce projects rising 10-15% quarter over quarter in the fourth quarter as cloud buyers absorb capacity.

Nearly half of Corsair's revenue is now a price it does not set

Corsair, which sells peripherals and Elgato streaming gear alongside power supplies, cooling, cases, branded memory modules and pre-built PCs, reported third-quarter revenue of $367.0m, up 6.2%, with memory revenue of $167.3m. "Memory revenue was a record for a third quarter at $167.3 million, up 43% year-over-year," chief executive Thi La told investors on the third-quarter call. "Demand was strong, prices were higher, and we gained market share sequentially."

The share is gained inside a small residual: Kingston has held 62% of the global memory module market for a 23rd consecutive year, with Corsair, G.Skill, Crucial and ADATA splitting what is left behind it.

Memory is now 45.6% of group revenue against 33.8% a year ago, and the rest of the segment paid for it. Computed from the segment lines in the same report, components and systems excluding memory fell from roughly $117.1m to about $83.2m, a decline near 29%: memory inflation added some $50m of revenue while the hardware beside it lost about $34m. Management reads the weakness as deferred rather than lost, which is the same enthusiast declining a $550-plus 32GB kit and declining a case and a cooler with it.

Gross margin fell to 26.7% from 33.2% in the June quarter. Gross profit grew 5.1% against revenue growth of 6.2%, and the company recorded a GAAP operating loss of $1.6m on a quarter that beat its own revenue guidance by $17m. La raised the full-year revenue midpoint $36m. Chief financial officer Gordon Mattingly, on the same call, put the other half on the table: "Last year's fourth quarter was unusually high at about 35% due to average selling prices rising rapidly. We expect memory gross margin of approximately 18%-20% this year, which is still above our historical average."

Logitech answered with a price increase and a purchase order

Logitech's memory exposure sits in video-collaboration room systems, not in mice. Chief financial officer Matteo Anversa told the October 27 call the company had proactively secured supply, which is why inventory is elevated and turns have fallen to 4.8 from 5.2, and that memory components for video conferencing were "pretty much secured throughout the full fiscal year 2027". It raised video-collaboration prices in May; the line's 12% constant-currency growth last quarter split roughly 60% price, 40% volume.

That is the posture of a price-setter, and the share data supports the claim to be one: Logitech leads gaming peripherals at roughly 19% against Razer's 16%, with Corsair near 7-10%. Mouse share rose 320 basis points in the quarter on management's own count.

It did not spare the stock. Logitech reported September-quarter sales of $1.22bn, up 3%, with non-GAAP gross margin of 45.2% and a beat on earnings, then guided the December quarter to down 5% to flat with gross margin of 41-42%, about 200 basis points below last year. Roughly 150 of those points are inflation, split about half freight and half materials. The guide also absorbs $70-80m from a semiconductor supplier's manufacturing incident that closed a facility in late June, cut from an initial estimate of up to $200m through secondary sourcing and redesign. The shares shed about $2bn of market value in a single session, which Vontobel's Michael Foeth attributed to the margin guide and soft European demand.

The market paid for the pass-through and marked down the list price

Over twelve months Corsair has nearly doubled and Logitech has fallen about 10%, and the earnings do not carry much of either. Corsair's price-to-trailing-gross-profit is 2.79x against roughly 1.72x a year ago, so only about 19 percentage points of that advance came from gross-profit growth. Its 13.0x on fiscal 2026 consensus of $0.91 becomes 14.2x on the fiscal 2027 consensus of $0.83, which is 9% lower: the street already treats this year as the peak. Logitech trades at 17.9x trailing and 17.5x forward, cheaper than the 19.0x it closed its last fiscal year at, but consensus has fiscal 2028 earnings only about 3% above fiscal 2027, and the company returned $406m in the September quarter against a $14.4bn market value.

The cause of the split is the invoice. Corsair's growth is denominated in a price the chipmakers set; Logitech's is denominated in a list it publishes, which is why its gross profit grew 6.3% on 2.8% revenue growth while Corsair's did the reverse. Neither escapes the demand side: IDC cut its 2026 global PC shipment forecast to a decline of 11.3% while still expecting market value to rise to $274bn on 17% price growth — fewer units, dearer ones, which is precisely the shape Corsair reported.

A pass-through runs both ways. In late November the memory complex broke hard, with Micron down about 13% in a session as the same researcher trimmed its price outlook. Cheaper kits would release the builds that buy Corsair's cases and coolers, and deflate the line that now supplies nearly half its revenue, and mark down the memory it deliberately stockpiled for the holidays. Nothing in the company's disclosure says which leg lands first.
