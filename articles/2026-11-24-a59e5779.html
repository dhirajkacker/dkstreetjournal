AI Research Seats Pay Gartner $220,000 a Head and Data Labeling Pays Innodata $14,100

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Two companies on the same information-services shelf are paid for the same thing — helping enterprises make sense of artificial intelligence — and the unit economics are not remotely comparable. Gartner sells annual research subscriptions at a 71.2% gross margin; Innodata builds and labels training data by the task at 37.2%, down from 46.1% three months earlier.

Gartner's contract value grew 2.5% on a currency-neutral basis to $5.3bn, its third straight quarterly acceleration, and the shares still fell 13.2% in the earnings session. Innodata's revenue rose 52.9% year on year while gross profit fell 16.3% sequentially and its historically largest customer ended a program.

The erosion at Gartner is visible and slow — wallet retention of 98% and 99%. The shock at Innodata is concentrated: two customers were 41% and 32% of nine-month revenue.

Gartner raised every line of its 2026 guidance on 3 November, reported contract value growth accelerating for a third consecutive quarter, and lost 13.2% of its market value in the session. Two days later Innodata, which builds and labels the training and evaluation data that frontier artificial-intelligence labs consume, reported revenue up more than half from a year earlier and gross profit down 16.3% from the prior quarter. Both sell work created by the same force. Neither is paid for it the same way.

The assumption underneath both share prices is that generative models absorb the labor of interpreting information, so the firms selling that interpretation shrink. What the filings show is a question of price per unit rather than volume. Gartner's Stamford-based research, conferences and consulting business converted $1,504.8m of third-quarter revenue into $1,071.6m of gross profit, a 71.2% margin, 230 basis points wider than a year earlier. Innodata's Q3 revenue of $95.6m carried a 37.2% gross margin, against 46.1% in the prior quarter. Annualized across the 19,460 and 10,064 people each company employs, that is roughly $220,000 of gross profit per Gartner employee and $14,100 per Innodata employee — a derived figure, and a fifteen-fold gap between renting a seat and delivering a dataset.

The substitution at Gartner is in the wallet, not the logo

Gartner's 10-Q for the quarter ended 30 September reports Global Technology Sales contract value up 2% year on year, driven primarily by new clients, with client retention improving to 85% from 84%. Wallet retention — what existing clients spend against the prior year — was 98%. In the business-functions segment it was 99% against 104% a year earlier, and the filings attribute the decline to lower spending by existing clients. Clients are staying and buying slightly less. US federal work is 2% to 3% of the contract value base, too small to carry the move.

"One of the things that we are seeing now is clients coming back to us and sort of saying, 'Wow, it is a lot harder than I thought to get any benefits from AI. I have got the cost, but not the benefits,'" chairman and chief executive Gene Hall said on the 3 November call. Chief financial officer Craig Safian was blunter about the trajectory: "We fully expect the CV growth rate in total to accelerate from where we are today." The 2026 price increase, effective 1 November, was 3%, the low end of the company's historical 3% to 4% range.

The socket is shrinking around a share gainer. Forrester, Gartner's nearest listed rival, reported third-quarter contract value of $281.9m, down 3.5%, revenue of $86.1m against $94.3m, and client retention of 78%.

Innodata's volume held and its per-task price did not

Innodata's growth barely decelerated — 54.4%, 57.8% and 52.9% across the first three quarters of 2026 — while gross profit growth fell from 82.7% in Q2 to 37.3% in Q3. Operating income declined 19.6% year on year as revenue grew.

Then the mechanics arrived. Around 550 staff in India were terminated on 26 October, with further cuts in the Philippines, a termination email citing a "ramp-down of the business assignment" rather than performance; the shares fell 25.3% the next session. On 5 November management disclosed that the historically largest customer had ended one program in mid-October, a roughly $12m revenue headwind in the fourth quarter. The September 10-Q puts 73% of nine-month revenue in two accounts, at 41% and 32%.

The replacement book is contractual. A customer under 5% of revenue a year ago became the largest, signing a $29m pre-training data program plus roughly $18m more; another hyperscaler went from $3.1m of trailing-twelve-month revenue to programs chief executive Rahul Singhal said "may contribute more than $20 million in revenue in 2027". Full-year growth guidance went up, to 42% or more. Innodata ended the quarter with $153.7m of cash net of customer prepayments and no debt.

It is also the small vendor in its own market. Private trackers size the expert-data business at about $2.6bn in 2026, with Mercor near $2bn of annualized gross revenue, Surge AI about $1.4bn and Scale AI around $1bn against Innodata's roughly $382m run rate; Mercor raised $750m at a $20bn valuation, some eighteen times Innodata's $1.1bn market capitalization.

What each decline is actually pricing

Gartner's shares fetch 2.48x gross profit on both trailing and forward numbers, against roughly 4.0x a year ago and about 10x at the 2025 high; the de-rating exceeds the 24.9% fall in the price because $1.7bn of buybacks cut the diluted share count 14.7%. Consensus has low-single-digit revenue growth with double-digit earnings growth, which is margin and share count doing the work. Barclays' Manav Patnaik cut the stock to Underweight on 6 November while raising his target to $185 from $150, calling per-seat exposure "already too high for a world in which the macro pressures are likely to continue".

Innodata trades at 7.65x trailing gross profit, down from roughly 18.3x a year ago and about 29x in late May. Its forward earnings multiple of 27.2x sits above the trailing 21.9x, because consensus full-year earnings of $1.18 against $1.12 already reported implies about $0.06 in the fourth quarter — the $12m headwind, priced.

So the shelf-level slide blends two unlike events. Gartner's renewal base is eroding by one or two points of wallet a year while gross margin widens and the share count shrinks, which is why a quarter that beat could still cost a quarter of the equity: the market marked down the terminal growth rate of a seat, not this year's cash. Innodata's problem is narrower and sharper — the volume of AI data work is still compounding, but the price per task fell nine points of margin in a single quarter and two customers decide the revenue line.

Hall's clients are now paying Gartner to tell them why their AI spending has not paid off. Innodata is paid to build the thing they bought. One invoice renews every twelve months at 3% more; the other ends when a program does, by email, mid-shift.
