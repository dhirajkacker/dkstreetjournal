Lula's Fourth Term Cost Brazil's Banks a Fifth or More in One Session; Vale Fell 3%

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

One session priced a Brazilian election, and it priced it almost entirely inside the lenders. The banks and the digital-banking platform in the iShares MSCI Brazil ETF lost between a sixth and nearly a third of their value the day after Lula's runoff win; the iron-ore exporter in the same fund lost 3.2%, and the Mexico, Peru, Chile and Colombia funds barely moved.

What was marked down was earnings in reais exposed to the federal budget and to a Selic held at 13.75%, not Latin America. Petrobras, 17.3% of the fund, reported third-quarter revenue up 40.4% and net income up 28.4% on record output, and its shares still fell 38.6% from 23 October to 25 November. Vale is the one large holding whose earnings actually deteriorated, and it fell the least of any of them.

Brazil's banks did the losing. In the first session after Lula won a fourth term with 51.8% of the runoff vote on 25 October, Itau Unibanco's American shares fell 20.4% and Bradesco's 28.0%, and the Sao Paulo exchange paused trading when the index breached a 10% circuit breaker on its worst day since 1998. Vale, the Rio de Janeiro miner that sells iron ore to Chinese steelmakers in dollars, fell 3.2%.

That asymmetry is the episode. The day was read as a country being de-rated, and the country funds obliged: the iShares MSCI Brazil ETF lost 19.8% close to close and its small-cap sibling 31.6%. But the selling landed on earnings denominated in reais and exposed to the federal budget, and it stopped at the border. That same session the Mexico fund closed up 0.05%, Peru up 0.2%, Chile down 1.2% and Colombia down 0.7%.

Two different businesses inside one wrapper

About 46% of the Brazil fund sits in five names as of 30 October: Petrobras at 17.3%, Vale 8.7%, Nu Holdings 8.5%, Itau 8.0% and the electric-motor maker WEG at 3.4%. Two of those earn dollars from commodities; two lend in local currency. Crash day sorted them accordingly. Banco do Brasil's local line fell 29.3%, Nu Holdings, the Sao Paulo digital bank with 86 million monthly active users in Brazil, fell 17.0%, and Petrobras fell 14.0%. The iShares Latin America 40 fund lost 11.2%, close to its Brazil weight multiplied by the Brazil fund's move; Petrobras, Nu, Vale and Itau are its four largest holdings as well.

Petrobras's quarter accelerated into its own selloff

The state-controlled oil group reported third-quarter revenue of $32.96bn, up 40.4% year on year, and net income of $7.74bn, up 28.4%, on production averaging 2.823m barrels of oil equivalent a day. It raised full-year operating cash flow guidance to $45-50bn from $35bn. "In the last two years alone, the production went up 3x as much as it did during 10 years," chief executive Magda Chambriard told the 11 November call.

The shares fell 38.6% between 23 October and 25 November, against 29.2% for the fund that holds them. What the market was pricing was not the quarter but the policy attached to it: fears of renewed state intervention, fuel-price controls, higher taxation and dividend risk, the levers Lula used in his third term. On the same call management confirmed fuel pricing is under internal review, with a "situational" adjustment possible by year-end and no structural change announced. Petrobras now trades at 3.5x forward earnings against 4.3x trailing, on consensus 2026 earnings of $4.80 a share, up 55% year on year, with a trailing free-cash-flow yield of 16.8%.

Vale is the counter-case, and it is the one that makes the point. Its third-quarter operating income fell 40.9% and net income 53.3% as gross margin compressed to 24.4% from 36.4%, and consensus 2026 earnings have been cut to $1.47 a share, down 27.1%. It is the only large holding whose earnings genuinely turned down, it trades at 7.0x forward earnings, and it is the holding that barely moved. A weaker real cuts its Brazilian mining costs; it does not cut the value of its iron ore.

The small-cap side travelled twice as far, both ways

The Brazil small-cap fund owns the domestic book directly: Totvs at 6.0%, the water utility Copasa at 4.9%, the grocer Sendas Distribuidora at 4.6% and Lojas Renner at 3.4%, with consumer cyclicals, real estate and utilities dominating 83 holdings. Those earnings move against the policy rate, and the central bank held the Selic at 13.75% on 4 November in a unanimous vote, ending five consecutive quarter-point cuts and citing unanchored inflation expectations.

Then the rate story reversed. Lula named Bradesco board chairman Luiz Carlos Trabuco finance minister on 26 November; the Ibovespa rose about 6.1% and long swap rates fell roughly 50 basis points. Because the US market was shut for Thanksgiving, two Brazilian sessions landed in one print: the small-cap fund gained 13.8%, the large-cap fund 10.5% and the 2x leveraged Brazil fund 20.6%. Itau rose 12.3% on 27 November; Vale rose 5.1%.

Moving-average trend measures were no help through any of it. They stayed bullish on the Brazil fund through 5 November, three weeks after the 19.8% day, and turned bearish on the Latin America 40 and leveraged funds on 18 November, two sessions before the Brazil fund's lowest close of the episode at 28.54.

Why the fund never looked cheap

The Brazil fund's trailing price-to-earnings ratio was 10.70 on 27 November, essentially where it stood in May. A drawdown of nearly a third produced no cheapening because the real went from 5.33 per dollar a year ago to 5.97 on 23 November, and a 12% devaluation cuts the dollar price and the dollar-translated local earnings together. The cheapening is specific to holdings paid in dollars.

Nor is the leverage telling anyone anything about Brazil. Over the 30 days to 25 November the 2x fund returned minus 20.4% against twice the Brazil fund's minus 20.6%, a fifth of a point of slippage. Compounding costs show up only over long windows: minus 28.0% over a year against twice minus 13.5%.

So the drawdown accounts for a policy path rather than an earnings path. The lenders and domestic small caps fell because a held Selic and a looser budget hit their revenue directly, and they recovered for the symmetrical reason when a private banker took the ministry. Petrobras fell hardest of the big names with its production and cash generation rising, which leaves its discount resting on the fuel-pricing and dividend decisions of a government that has made those decisions before.

Trabuco takes office on 5 January. Petrobras's year-end pricing review comes first.

Originating hypothesis

category band break index composition mechanics · subject: EWZS, COLO, EWW, EWZ, ILF

The LatAm regional-ETF shelf has been marked down at genuinely gradual intensity — the ten-fund group averages -10.3% over 30 days against only -5.5% over twelve months, with not one member in any 1-month, 3-month, 6-month or 12-month mover list, yet almost every fund has stepped strongly bullish to strongly bearish across the 180- and 365-day windows (ILF, EWZS, FLN, EPU, ECH, BRZU) while two have gone the other way or barely moved (COLO is the only member in a bull band, mildly bullish over both 90 and 180 days, and EWW has repaired strongly bearish to mildly bearish inside 30 days) — and rather than another "LatAm is selling off" regional read, the question is a what-do-you-actually-own-inside-each-wrapper question told end to end: establish, holding by holding, what each fund is paid by, since EWZ is a cap-weighted bet on a handful of dollar-priced commodity exporters and big Brazilian banks, EWZS owns the domestically-geared Brazilian small caps whose earnings move inversely to the Selic and whose index is the one that broke hardest, BRZU is a daily-reset leveraged wrapper whose band flip from strongly bullish to strongly bearish in 30 days is partly compounding mechanics rather than a view on Brazil, ARGT's weight sits heavily in a Nasdaq-listed dollar earner rather than in peso assets, ECH and EPU are effectively copper-and-precious-metals funds with a pension-and-currency overlay, and COLO is a two-name oil-and-bank fund — so the same regional discount is landing on a dollar-revenue export book, a local-rate domestic book, a leverage-decay product and a commodity proxy at once.
