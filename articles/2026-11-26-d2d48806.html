Vehicle-Tracking Subscriptions Now Pay Three-Quarters of Ituran's Record Revenue

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A markdown that was read as proof that device demand had rolled over has now hit the one business on the comparison that barely sells devices. Ituran reported its September quarter on 24 November: subscription fees of $80.5m, up 19% and 76% of revenue, against product sales of $26m, up 5%. The subscriber base grew by 40,000 net adds from ongoing operations to 2.68 million, and operating margin widened to 23.8% from 21.3%.

The two hardware-weighted names on the same comparison went the other way on price and the same way on results: Digi International closed fiscal 2026 with revenue up 24.4% and record recurring revenue of $197m, and Zebra Technologies raised its full-year sales growth guidance to about 17%. All three fell. The weight of the evidence points to a cost-and-rating event, not a demand one: memory inflation is measurable at Zebra, and Ituran's monthly meter is the part of the shelf it cannot reach.

Ituran Location and Control, which sells stolen-vehicle recovery and fleet-tracking services to insurers, carmakers and drivers from a base in Azor, Israel, reported its September quarter on 24 November with more subscribers than it started with. Net additions from ongoing operations came to 40,000, revenue set a record at $106.6m, up 15%, and over the following two sessions the shares closed at their lowest level in a year.

The drawdown running through edge and industrial connectivity names since late October has been read as a demand event: retail and warehouse buyers deferring devices, the connected-device cycle peaking. Ituran was the cleanest test of that reading, because almost nothing it books depends on winning a new hardware order. The base grew, the fees grew, the margin widened, and the price went down with the box sellers.

The meter is about $10 a month, 2.68 million times over

Subscription revenue of $80.5m across the 2.68 million subscribers on the books at the end of September works out, by derivation from the company's results release, to roughly $10.01 per subscriber per month. Product revenue, the in-vehicle devices themselves, was $26m. Operating income rose 29% to $25.4m, lifting operating margin to 23.8% from 21.3% a year earlier, and net income reached $18.0m. Diluted earnings of $0.91 a share, up from $0.74, still came in below consensus. The quarter's blemish was a one-time removal of 67,000 low-average-revenue subscribers after a US customer's bankruptcy, which the company put at about $1m of 2027 revenue. The board declared a dividend of $0.50 a share. Israel supplies 56% of revenue, Brazil 22%.

The annuity is now the cheapest thing in the comparison: 14.4x trailing earnings and 13.0x forward, against 16.6x trailing in August, on a 9.0% trailing free-cash-flow yield. The forward figure rests on a single analyst's estimates and carries correspondingly less weight.

Both hardware names raised their numbers into the fall

Digi International, which sells cellular routers and embedded wireless modules with a cloud management subscription attached, closed fiscal 2026 with revenue up 24.4% to $535.2m, operating income up 42.1%, and record annual recurring revenue of $197m, up 30%; it pulled its $200m recurring-revenue target forward a year, into fiscal 2027. The qualifier is the guide: 10-15% revenue growth for the new year, with first-quarter revenue of $140-144m against a $144.7m consensus. The shares fell 13.9% the session after the 11 November print. Digi now trades at 5.3x forward gross profit, against 9.6x on 18 August.

On the memory shortage, chief executive Ronald Konezny told investors on 11 November: "We don't want to take advantage of any kind of one-time or transitionary increase in pricing."

Zebra Technologies, which supplies barcode scanners, rugged mobile computers and thermal label printers to retail, logistics and healthcare customers, grew third-quarter revenue 22.3% to $1.614bn, its fastest rate of the year, and raised full-year sales growth guidance to about 17% from about 15% with per-share earnings of $21.20. Its full-year memory-cost headwind rose to $145m from $120m, attributed to higher mobile-computing volumes rather than worse pricing, and gross margin fell 228 basis points to 45.7%. Management expects memory prices to keep rising through the first half of 2027 and declined to size next year's hit. "It generates about a 15% increase on our mobile computing products," chief financial officer Nathan Winters said of the October price rise on the 3 November call. The stock sits at 14.9x forward earnings against 26.2x trailing and a five-year range of roughly 20-25x.

What the markdown was actually about

Over the 30 days to 25 November, Digi fell 30.1%, Zebra 17.1% and Ituran 15.0%. Across twelve months Digi is up 27.4% and Zebra 27.7%, while Ituran is up 7.2%; on a 180-day view the annuity is the worst of the three, down 28.8%.

The reported numbers account for almost none of that. What they do explain is why the three should not be marked identically. Zebra buys memory by volume for every mobile computer it ships, and the contract price of a 16Gb DDR5 chip went from $8.70 in October 2025 to $48 by September 2026 as artificial-intelligence buyers outbid everyone else. Digi carries the same input and chose not to pass it through, protecting the subscription relationships it is still converting. Ituran's cost base is only about a quarter hardware, and its margin went up. The rest looks like rating compression inside a general repricing: the S&P 500 fell 8.6% in the week to 20 November, leaving it 11.6% below its October high.

All three have now reported, and nothing on their calendars tests the demand question again until February. Until then, 2.68 million vehicles get billed every month whether or not memory is scarce.
