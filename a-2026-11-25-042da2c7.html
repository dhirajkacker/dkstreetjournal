Appian Sells Its AI as a Tier Price Increase; JFrog Books It as a Commitment Before Usage

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Enterprise software has been marked down since late October on the belief that artificial intelligence shrinks the seat count a vendor can invoice. Two names climbed through it, and what they share is how the AI dollar reaches the income statement: Appian charges a 25-35% premium for its Advanced Tier, and JFrog's record $812m of contracted obligations explicitly excludes usage overages.

Both businesses genuinely accelerated. Appian's third-quarter revenue grew 25.5% with cloud net annual recurring revenue expansion at 120%, its best in more than five years; JFrog's grew 30.9%, a fourth straight acceleration, with net dollar retention of 124%.

The pricing mechanism explains who rose; it does not make the two advances the same. Appian's price-to-gross-profit went from 3.07x in May to 6.41x against roughly four points of growth improvement, so the re-rating did the work. JFrog's multiple is flat since August at 25.1x, the dearest of the four names here. And Figma, growing 42.8% with 131% retention, fell 31% in a month.

Appian reported the strongest cloud expansion rate in its history on 5 November, and the shares have held an uptrend since mid-August while seat-priced enterprise software was being marked down hard. JFrog, whose Artifactory repository stores the binaries and containers that pass between a developer's source control and production, reported the same day and gapped higher. Both sell into software budgets that the market spent November discounting.

The discount has a dated trigger. ServiceNow fell 27% on 29 October after guiding fourth-quarter growth in its current contracted backlog down to 18.5% from 21%, reviving the fear that AI will break seat-based software pricing. Read through that lens, these two exceptions look like proof that pricing structure decides who survives. The structure holds up. What it does not do is make the two advances equivalent: one was paid for by growth and the other by a doubling of its multiple.

Appian's AI arrives as a tier upgrade, not a meter

Appian, which sells a low-code platform that generates workflows, data models and interfaces instead of hand-coded applications to banks, insurers and government agencies, grew revenue 19.1% in the second quarter and 25.5% in the third, to $234.6m. Cloud subscriptions reached $136m, up 20%, and the cloud net annual recurring revenue expansion rate stepped to 120% from 115%. Management attributed most of the quarter's uplift to customers moving to the Advanced Tier, which carries a 25-35% premium to Standard; AI usage rose more than 50% sequentially, with consumption overages growing but not yet material.

"Appian has claimed a distinctive and valuable place in the AI stack," chief executive Matthew Calkins told investors on 5 November. "We connect the world's most important technology to the world's most important work."

The operating line moved with it: a margin of 9.1% against -2.7% in the second quarter. The fear that the profit is borrowed from somewhere else fails on its own arithmetic, since net income of $12.3m sits below operating income of $21.3m, and the $2.036bn trade-secret award Appian won against Pegasystems was vacated with a new trial ordered, contributing nothing pending a retrial set for January 2027.

Cash has not yet followed. Appian disclosed roughly -$15m of cash flow against +$19m a year earlier, blamed on collection timing; an aggregator computes free cash flow of -$20.0m after capital spending. Two more drags sit outside the subscription book: a guided 2-3 percentage point currency headwind to fourth-quarter reported growth, and professional-services margin down to 24% from 31%. The 28% growth in total subscriptions also flatters the cloud line, because 70% of on-premises bookings are recognized upfront.

JFrog's $812m is a floor, not a meter

JFrog's revenue grew 30.9% to $179.2m, a fourth consecutive acceleration, with gross margin up to 79.6% and gross profit up 34.6%. Trailing-four-quarter net dollar retention reached 124%, six points higher than a year earlier, on gross retention of 97%. Remaining performance obligations hit a record $812m, up 60%, and management stressed that the figure excludes usage above customer minimum commitments. Customers above $1m of annual recurring revenue grew to 112 from 71.

"This deal represents 167% growth in cloud data consumption compared to the customer's prior usage commitment," chief executive Shlomi Ben Haim said on 5 November of a $76m multi-year contract with a major telecom. Cloud revenue reached $101.5m, up 60% and 57% of the total, while self-managed revenue grew 6%. Free cash flow was $66.5m at a 37% margin.

Figma's 131% retention bought it nothing

The de-rating is not sorting cleanly on business quality. Figma, the browser-based design and prototyping platform, grew revenue 42.8% to $391.4m with net dollar retention of 131%, a fifth straight quarter above 130%, and raised full-year guidance to $1.497-1.499bn. The shares fell 31% over the following 30 days at 7.26x trailing gross profit. What Figma has that the other two do not is an inference bill it pays rather than charges: gross margin fell three points to 82% after its agent product became generally available, and the GAAP operating margin was -30.4%.

Only monday.com, the work-management vendor, is deteriorating on its numbers: growth of 19.2%, retention of 108% guided down to 107%, fourth-quarter revenue guided to 14% growth, and 690 jobs cut in July. "I think that we are probably a few quarters from getting back to something that implies more stability," chief financial officer Eliran Glazer said on 9 November. At 2.51x gross profit and 11.7x forward earnings, the cheapest here, its markdown is doing rational work.

Growth paid for one advance, the multiple for the other

JFrog's price-to-gross-profit is 25.1x against 24.93x on 22 August, so the intervening gain was bought with gross-profit growth rather than enthusiasm. The level is the problem: three to eight times every name above, and roughly 91x the midpoint of its own $1.14-1.16 full-year adjusted earnings guide. Brokers raised targets after the print to a cluster just above the price, KeyBanc to $118 and RBC to $121, with Piper Sandler at $95. And the advance is one session: the stock drifted lower from late August before gapping 35.3% on 6 November.

Appian is the reverse. Its multiple of trailing gross profit was 3.07x on 3 May and 3.63x on 29 July; it is 6.41x now, 6.17x forward, against roughly four percentage points of growth improvement. Forward earnings of 45.5x on this year's $1.21 consensus falls to about 34x on next year's $1.60, which is the whole of the case. Citi went to $69 from $40, TD Cowen to $58 from $50 and Morgan Stanley to $57 from $32 — three of the four raising numbers while keeping a non-buy rating, which is marking to market. Consensus itself models the acceleration away, at 14.1% revenue growth next year against 22.7% this year.

So the pricing mechanism survives the test and the valuations absorb most of its reward. A tier premium and a contracted minimum are both invoices that an AI-assisted customer cannot shrink by cutting headcount, which is more than ServiceNow or monday.com can say. Neither protagonist is cheap on the strength of that, and Figma's quarter is the standing evidence that the market is not currently paying for retention.

What neither company has shown is what customers do above the floor. JFrog's record backlog is contracted; the overage revenue sitting on top of it is the line the market stopped trusting this month, after Datadog told investors its largest customer had cut usage and guided the fourth quarter assuming that customer pays only its minimum.
