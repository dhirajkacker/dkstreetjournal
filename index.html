The companies that build the electrical room of a data center, the medium-voltage switchgear and substation transformers that sit between the grid and the racks, spent election week being repriced for a rule that has not been written.

On 5 November the Korea Composite Stock Price Index fell 4.46% on heavy foreign selling of chip and power-equipment shares after the American result, with HD Hyundai Electric down 8.10% and Hyosung Heavy Industries down 12.59%; the stated concern was that tighter oversight of data-center construction, particularly restrictions on electricity and water consumption, could delay new projects. The next session the index fell a further 6.02% and a trading halt was triggered. In the United States, Eaton, the Dublin-domiciled power-management group whose Electrical Americas unit sells switchgear and power distribution, fell 7.4% on 5 November as the Nasdaq entered correction.

Six listed makers of this equipment fell an equal-weighted 26.7% over the thirty days to 6 November, against 5.4% for the S&P 500. The implied reading is that the scarcity these firms have been charging for since 2023 is about to be curtailed by policy, or cured by the capacity their own windfall financed. In the window the market marked them down, neither happened.

Nothing in the queue got shorter

Large power transformers still quote beyond 120 weeks, substation units above 160 weeks and the largest high-voltage units out to four years, while 15-kilovolt medium-voltage switchgear runs 52 to 80 weeks on June 2026 procurement data, with some manufacturers' capacity committed into 2028. American transformer demand is projected to exceed domestic manufacturing capacity by 30% to 40% through at least 2028.

The relief is dated, and the dates are not soon. Eaton's new three-phase transformer facility arrives in the first half of 2027. HD Hyundai Electric's second Montgomery, Alabama plant completes in April 2027, lifting extra-high-voltage transformer capacity by half, and LS Electric is spending KRW250bn to expand its Cedar City, Utah switchboard plant to about six times its current footprint.

Transformer prices stopped; switchgear prices did not

Where the hypothesis of a cure has purchase is narrower than the selloff. Transformer producer prices are up 91.4% since January 2020 but rose only 8.7% year-on-year in July 2026, while switchgear prices accelerated to 13.3% year-on-year in August.

That split shows up in guidance. Hubbell, the Shelton, Connecticut maker of electrical and utility distribution products, raised full-year guidance on 27 October to adjusted earnings of $20.40 to $20.60 a share, lifted Electrical Solutions organic growth to 17-18% and cut Utility Solutions, the transformer and substation segment, to 6-7%. "We're anticipating PCP to be in the neutral to dollar positive next year, with incrementals in line with our long-term algo at 25%-30%," chief financial officer Joe Capozzoli said on the call, meaning price-cost-productivity is no longer expected to add to margin in 2027. Hubbell's Utility sales rose 9% to $1,028m, about 4.9% of it organic. Data-center sales grew over 80% organically.

Eaton, meanwhile, has announced general price increases for Electrical Americas effective 2 January 2027, and ABB's third-quarter pricing contributed three percentage points to Electrification growth.

One income statement rolled, and it is the transformer house

HD Hyundai Electric reported September-quarter sales of KRW1.18tn and operating profit of KRW284.5bn, up 18.5% and 15.1% respectively, with margin at 24.1% against 25.1% in June and net income down 3.6%. Group operating margin had climbed from 0.55% in 2021 to 24.40% in 2025; the September quarter interrupts that arc.

The same morning it reported, the company approved a KRW198bn second phase at its Cheongju distribution campus running to June 2029, explicitly for AI data-center package demand. Its shares, at 15.0x forward earnings against 15.7x trailing on consensus 2026 earnings growth of 25.7%, carry almost no gap between the two: the market has stopped expecting growth to compress the multiple.

Powell has said nothing since August

Powell Industries, the Houston builder of custom switchgear and modular E-House substations, last spoke on 4 August, when it booked a record $934m of orders, a 3.0x quarterly book-to-bill and backlog near $2.4bn against $1.4bn a year earlier, with gross margin at 30.6%. Asked whether margins had peaked, chief executive Brett Cope said they had not. The stock has since fallen 32.4% from 7 October to 6 November, two-thirds of that before any vote was counted, and trades at 19.3x forward earnings against roughly 35x forward in May.

The business evidence accounts for part of this month and not most of it. At the transformer end the markdown has a cause in kind, though HD Hyundai's 74% fall from its May peak is a long way past a 100-basis-point margin slip. At the switchgear end the results point the other way: nVent Electric, which closed its $700m-revenue Maverick Power switchgear acquisition on 1 October, raised full-year organic growth guidance to 38-39% on 30 October and fell 41.3% anyway. What the last two sessions added was a political risk to the buyer's permit, not a supplier's price concession.

Powell releases fiscal fourth-quarter results after the close on 16 November, the first of these companies to speak since the election. Whatever backlog it reports was booked before anyone voted.

Between 28 October and 5 November, the four companies that buy computer hardware from manufacturers and sell it on to corporate, small-business and public-sector buyers each reported a record quarter and a thinner spread. Ingram Micro's net sales rose 20.8% to $15.2bn; its gross profit rose 14.6%. CDW, PC Connection and Insight Enterprises printed the same shape, and all four lost gross margin percentage year on year.

The quarters were read as AI demand finally arriving in the channel. What they document instead is a bill of materials inflating faster than the markup charged on it, with the earnings growth arriving from somewhere else entirely.

What one more dollar of hardware pays

CDW's net sales rose 17.7% to $6.75bn while gross profit rose 10.3% to $1.39bn. Netting the two quarters, $1,017m of additional revenue carried $130m of additional gross profit: 12.8 cents on the incremental dollar, against 20.5 cents across the whole book. Ingram Micro, the wholesale rung above the resellers, managed 4.9 cents against a 6.55-cent average, because its GPU and AI infrastructure revenue went from roughly $700m a year ago to $1.8bn. Strip those deals out and its gross margin was 7.06% against 7.10% — flat. PC Connection earned 14.1 cents against an 18.5-cent average.

The mechanism sits in the component. Memory and storage now run about 35% of a PC's bill-of-materials cost against a historical 15% to 18%, and more than half the materials cost of a traditional server. A reseller buys at the inflated component price and sells at a percentage markup that does not scale with it, so revenue rises by the full cost increase and gross profit only by the markup on that increase. Contract prices for conventional memory are expected to rise a further 10% to 20% quarter on quarter in the fourth quarter on the research firm TrendForce's projection, after 58% to 63% in the second.

The volume data make the point sharper. CDW reported client-device average selling prices up 19% even as units declined, attributing the rise to price increases and richer configurations with more processing power and memory. PC Connection's notebook revenue grew 30% while notebook units fell about 5%. Insight's devices revenue grew 30% on units up low single digit.

Demand itself is not the constraint. "Writings continue to outpace invoicing, and backlog is building, particularly in enterprise infrastructure," chief executive Chris Leahy told CDW investors on 4 November.

Insight's half-services book

Insight is the one name whose new revenue was essentially non-dilutive: 20.3 cents of gross profit per incremental dollar against a 21.4-cent average, on net revenue up 21.8% and gross profit up 20.5%. The reason is mix. Cloud gross profit reached $164m, up 26%, and core services $95m, up 21% — together roughly half the company's gross profit. Infrastructure hardware gross profit rose more than 50%, but hardware gross margin fell 100 basis points on device pricing and client mix.

"We are raising our gross profit growth expectations to 12.5% to 14%, and our gross margin will be approximately 21.5%," chief financial officer James Morgado said on 5 November.

The rescue came from overhead

At CDW, non-GAAP selling and administrative costs grew 5.6% against 10.3% gross profit growth, lifting non-GAAP operating income 17% to $620m; chief financial officer Al Miralles credited "Geared for Growth efforts kicking in." Ingram Micro cut operating expenses to 4.62% of sales from 5.13% and lifted adjusted operating income 25% to $322m. PC Connection improved overhead by 150 basis points of sales and grew operating income 35.4%. Carrying the bigger invoices costs cash: PC Connection burned $39.8m of operating cash over nine months on a $144.8m receivables increase.

Priced inversely to the gross profit

CDW has risen 15.6% in three months to 3.81x trailing gross profit, the dearest of the four and above the 3.74x at which PC Connection stood in September, on the second-thinnest incremental spread — while management guides full-year gross margin below 2025. Ingram Micro, which beat a $13.85bn consensus revenue estimate and cut net leverage to 1.9x, trades at 7.11x forward earnings and 1.34x book; it gave back a 6.1% after-hours gain the next session and is down 10.4% over three months. Insight sits at 12.66x forward earnings and fell 12.3% on the day it raised guidance — the same session the Nasdaq 100 fell 6.4% on fears of a slower data-center buildout, which also took PC Connection down 9.9%.

So the results account for the earnings growth. They do not account for the ranking.

Every one of these companies converted a slower-growing gross line into a faster-growing operating line, which is a cost story with a floor under it, and none of them captured the component inflation as margin: it was passed through to the customer and spent on the component. Insight's exception is structural, built out of cloud and core services gross profit that now carries half the book. Ingram Micro's discount contains a forward cycle call as well as a valuation: management has guided 2027 to moderate single-digit growth as the PC refresh ends and pricing moderates, so the cheapest name in the group also carries the clearest statement that the revenue wave stops.

Ingram Micro's fourth-quarter guidance assumes supply constraints add two to three percentage points to its sales. That is the trade these companies are in: the shortage is worth something real on the invoice, and so far almost nothing on the spread.

The swipe held, the software did not

Fiserv's terminals processed more volume last quarter and the company earned almost nothing extra for it. Clover, the point-of-sale system Fiserv sells to roughly 700,000 businesses in eight countries, grew processing revenue 12% on gross payment volume up about 10%, while total Clover revenue grew 1%. The gap is the attach: value-added services, meaning software subscriptions and Clover Capital lending, declined 3% and fell to 23% of Clover revenue from 26%, after growing 18% at 27% of the mix in the first quarter.

The 30-day bounce in this corner of financial technology was read as payments bottoming, a year after investors began marking the sector down on the fear that stablecoin rails would disintermediate card settlement. The November prints point somewhere nearer and duller. The invoice is being reassembled one layer above the rail, by whoever sells the merchant its software, and in bank processing by whoever wins the renewal. Scale at the terminal is not the constraint: Clover's annualized volume of $272bn runs ahead of Square's $220bn and Toast's $135bn, and the same reporting has Clover losing to vertical specialists like Toast among the larger, more attractive small businesses.

Fiserv's group organic revenue was flat, with Merchant Solutions organic revenue down 2% and gross margin of 53.1% against 58.9% a year earlier. A $6.45bn non-cash goodwill impairment, triggered by the sustained decline in its own market capitalization, opened a $12.92 gap between GAAP earnings of -$11.17 a share and the adjusted $1.75. Year-to-date free cash flow conversion is running near 71% against a 90% full-year target. Management reaffirmed 2026 adjusted earnings of $7.20 to $7.40 and doubled its cost program. "We have identified a total of $1 billion in savings opportunities, up from the prior $500 million," chief financial officer Paul Todd told investors on November 3. At $62.88 the shares sit on 8.6x consensus 2026 earnings of $7.27 — the cheapest conventional multiple in the group — but forward price-to-gross-profit of 3.7x sits above the trailing 3.5x, which is the market saying gross profit shrinks from here.

A renewal business, not a price list

FIS, which hosts core processing for more than 90m bank accounts, shows the bank-side version. Reported revenue rose 27.1%, almost all of it the Worldpay and issuer-asset reshuffle; on a pro-forma basis revenue grew 3.7% and recurring revenue 2.5%, with Banking recurring revenue decelerating to 2.4% as fewer than ten large clients pushed contracted go-lives into late 2026 and 2027. Non-recurring revenue grew 22.8% on one-off distribution partnerships. Preliminary 2027 guidance strips that out: banking revenue flat to up 1%, adjusted earnings down high-single to low-double digits. "The massive swing factor is this 250 to 300 basis points on nonrecurring," chief financial officer James Kehoe said on November 4, the day before his departure took effect. "Simplistically, take 3% of 13.5 billion, that is a $400 million change."

Price itself is holding — renewal rates in the high 90s, up 100 basis points, with 72% of issuing revenue contracted through 2029 or beyond. The account base is the problem, shrunk by bank consolidation that paid FIS termination fees on the way out. The shares fell 23.2% on November 4 as J.P. Morgan cut its rating to Neutral and its target to $38 from $53. Against $20.8bn of debt at 3.5x leverage and a suspended buyback, 7.4x trailing EV/EBITDA and 0.91x book are doing rational work; the 16.2% trailing equity free-cash-flow yield compresses to roughly 6% measured on enterprise value.

Jack Henry is winning the renewals it needs

Jack Henry, which sells core platforms and card processing to US banks and credit unions, took revenue up 8.8% with operating income up 12.5% and raised fiscal 2027 guidance to 6.6-7.4% non-GAAP revenue growth. It booked 9 competitive core wins against 4 a year earlier, 63% of them bundling core, digital and card. "A lot of our wins recently have been longtime clients of that particular provider. A couple of them were 28-year clients," chief executive Greg Adelson said on November 4. The buyer's budget is cooperating: the Bank Director 2026 technology survey it cited found 81% of banks raising technology spending, up from 71%, at a median 10% increase. Jack Henry, Fiserv and FIS together serve roughly 70-72% of US banks and are each launching or consolidating platforms rather than cutting price. At 23.5x forward earnings against 7-8% guided earnings growth, it is the dearest name in the group and has no re-rating left in the number.

The one company that discloses what a transaction earns is cutting it deliberately. Wise, the cross-border transfer business, ran a 49 basis point take rate, down 3 basis points year on year, and grew revenue 21% in constant currency on volume of $72bn. "We follow the cost-plus margin logic, and we are in a driving seat," chief financial officer Emmanuel Thomassin said on October 15. Wise raised guidance and its shares still fell 5.6% over the month.

What the move actually bought

So the group average is carried by two names and one week. Excluding Fiserv and NCR Voyix — whose own gain follows a hardware exit that took revenue down 21.2% while lifting gross margin — the other four averaged 4.5%, and Broadridge's contribution rests on a November 2 mandate to supply the governance layer and digital transfer agency for the New York Stock Exchange's planned tokenized equity marketplace, which has nothing to do with payment transactions.

Results account for the whole of Jack Henry's move and the whole of FIS's fall. They do not account for Fiserv's. A 38.8% gain on flat organic revenue, compressing gross margin and conversion below target is buying a fourth quarter in which Merchant organic growth is guided at 6-8%, from minus 2%. Each 1x of 2027 earnings is 12.6% of today's price, so the multiple, not the attach rate, is still what the position depends on.

Fiserv told investors it takes nine to twelve months for better bank retention to show up in revenue. The repricing of its shares took one earnings week.

Zebra Technologies beat its own quarter on 3 November and fell 8.8% in the session. The maker of barcode scanners, enterprise mobile computers and thermal label printers, sold to retailers, warehouse operators and hospitals, told investors memory prices would keep climbing into 2027, and that its answer is a price increase taken in October.

The shares had held an uptrend since the start of July, read as an enterprise device refresh cycle with recurring software riding on top of it. The quarter tests the second half of that. Zebra's full-year memory-cost headwind rose to $145m from a prior estimate of $120m, entirely because mobile-computing volumes came in higher, and on the call management put software revenue at a low single-digit percentage of the total. The component shock is being absorbed on the invoice, because there is no subscription meter to absorb it instead.

The memory bill became a price list

Revenue of $1.614bn in the quarter ended 3 October, reported to the SEC on an 8-K, was up 22.3%, and operating margin improved to 14.7% from 13.9%. Gross margin went the other way: 45.7%, against 48.0% a year earlier. Conventional dynamic random-access memory contract prices rose 90-95% quarter on quarter early in 2026, with memory forecast to absorb 68% of major cloud providers' capital spending in 2027 — device makers now bid for parts against hyperscalers. Of roughly $50m of memory cost in the quarter, pricing offset about $25m, with more than $55m mitigated by restructuring, productivity and currency.

"It generates about a 15% increase on our mobile computing products," Nathan Winters, Zebra's chief financial officer, said of the October action on the 3 November call. That increase is expected to add roughly 1.5 points to 2027 revenue. On the same call, chief executive Bill Burns said: "We would expect growth, as you suggested, for 2027 to be at the high end of our long-term guidance range" — a range of 5-7%. At the top of it, by that arithmetic, about a fifth of next year's growth is Zebra's own cost inflation handed to its buyers.

Demand is real, and it is hardware demand

Not all of the quarter was price. Organic growth was about 11% of the reported 22.3%, with the Connected Frontline segment up 35% in total but 15% organically after the Elo Touch acquisition, and Asset Visibility & Automation up 7%. Transportation and logistics was the fastest-growing end market, with a refresh cycle beginning in 2027. Executives said they had seen no revenue effect from customers pulling orders forward ahead of the increase, and described demand as broad-based across regions and both segments. Inventory rose about $90m on memory stocking; five new memory suppliers have been qualified against a target of five to seven sources for each memory type. Backlog and pipeline support 10-14% growth in the current quarter, and full-year sales growth guidance went to about 17%.

What none of it produces is a recurring line. Zebra's Frontline artificial-intelligence products were described as drivers of device refresh rather than as a subscription business of their own.

Digi's meter runs on its own clock

Digi International, which sells cellular routers and embedded modules alongside a cloud device-management subscription, is the counterexample in the same niche. Its annualized recurring revenue reached $191m at the end of June, up 52% from $126m and roughly a third of annualized revenue, with gross margin at 64.8% and rising. About $20m of that recurring base arrived with the Particle acquisition, so organic growth is nearer the mid-30s percent. Digi traded below its 200-day moving average on 6 November with no company fundamentals since August; results land 11 November. It trades on 20.2x forward earnings against 48.6x trailing.

What is being repriced is 2027, not the quarter

Zebra's shares fell 15.3% over 30 days against a 5% decline in the S&P 500, and almost all of it sits in two sessions: the post-earnings drop on roughly double normal volume, after Zebra warned memory prices would keep rising, and the 5 November unwind of the artificial-intelligence hardware trade that took the index down 4.4%.

At 15.2x forward earnings against 26.5x trailing, Zebra sits below its own stated five-year history of 20-25x. The gap is what consensus expects: earnings per share of $21.24 this year, then growth of 5.7% next. The forward figure already assumes 2026 does not repeat, which is also what Burns guided to.

So the results account for the direction of the business and not for the direction of the shares. Zebra converts installed devices into reorders at a price it sets, and it has just demonstrated it can set one; Digi converts installed devices into renewals it collects monthly, at a gross margin nearly twenty points higher. The first depends on when customers decide to replace equipment. The second does not.

Zebra has qualified five new memory suppliers and is hunting more. That is a procurement answer to a margin problem, and a sound one. It still leaves the top line waiting on a decision made in somebody else's warehouse.

Okta's revenue growth has decelerated for four consecutive quarters, and the shares still more than doubled in the six months to 6 November. Revenue at the identity company, which sells single sign-on, multi-factor authentication and the acquired Auth0 developer portfolio to enterprises and governments on annual subscriptions, rose 10.6% year over year in the July quarter, the slowest of the four. What moved was margin: gross margin reached 79.6% against 76.9% a year earlier, operating margin 13.3% against 5.6%, and operating income grew 161%.

The advance in Okta and in Rubrik, which sells cyber-recovery software priced on the volume of data under management rather than per user, has been read as proof that identity and backup are the meters agentic AI feeds: every autonomous agent has to authenticate like an employee, and everything it writes has to be recoverable, while workflow software loses human seats. Neither income statement carries that revenue yet. Asked about agent-identity revenue on the 26 August call, Okta chief financial officer Brett Tighe answered "Still immaterial," and said AI-related revenue stays immaterial through fiscal 2027 with a meaningful contribution possible only in fiscal 2028 and beyond, according to the company's transcript. Okta for AI Agents went generally available on 30 April and has closed dozens of deals, several of them worth more than $1m.

Okta's order book is being guided down, not up

Current remaining performance obligation, the contracted revenue Okta expects to recognize within a year and the closest thing it prints to a forward order book, reached $2.585bn, up 14%, ahead of billed revenue. Management guided that growth back to 11-12% for the following quarter. Trailing net retention was flat at 107%. Customers paying more than $100,000 a year grew 6%, to 5,255, while those above $1m grew 22% to more than 600, so the base is concentrating rather than widening. Free cash flow was $227m, a 28% margin against 22% a year earlier. Consensus carries 9.9% revenue growth for the year to January 2028.

The fast-growing bookings line Okta does disclose is a different product set: governance, privileged access and threat protection were 30% of bookings in the quarter at roughly 40% average uplift per deal, up from about 25% in the April quarter. Agent sign-on was folded into the core product at the Oktane conference in September rather than sold as a separate machine-identity line, and chief executive Todd McKinnon told that audience on 23 September that in agent identity "there is going to be massive growth and the market share is going to be set probably in three years."

Rubrik's contracted book accelerated in the same quarter

Rubrik's subscription annual recurring revenue grew 33% to $1.66bn at 31 July, with net new subscription ARR up 35% and subscription net retention above 119%. Customers paying $100,000 or more rose 23% to 3,084 and supply 88% of subscription ARR; cloud is 89% of the mix and grew 39%. Dividing those disclosures gives roughly $474,000 of ARR per large customer against about $438,000 a year earlier, so most of the 33% is landing and graduating big accounts and about a quarter of it is deeper spend inside them.

The income statement is the weaker half. Revenue growth decelerated to 37.9%, gross margin slipped to 78.4% from 79.5% so gross profit grew more slowly than revenue, and the GAAP operating margin was still minus 16.8% on a $61.8m quarterly loss. Rubrik is also not the share leader in backup: Gartner's 2026 Magic Quadrant places Veeam, Rubrik, Commvault and Cohesity in the Leaders quadrant with Veeam ahead, and Veeam reported over $2.1bn of ARR for 2025. Its own agentic product, Code Guardian, which runs Anthropic's Claude through a security harness against cloned copies of customer code repositories, was expanded on 9 October and remains in private preview.

The faster meter carries the dearer price

Rubrik trades at 17.60x trailing gross profit, against 10.48x in early May and 15.59x in early September. It is the one multiple in the pair that kept expanding through the autumn drawdown, and it expanded faster than the 36% growth in gross profit underneath it. Okta's equivalent is 11.74x, more than double its early-May level near 5x but below 13.50x on 25 September. On forward revenue Okta stands at 8.75x against roughly 6.9x in mid-July, when the shares were $139.53.

Both gave back a fifth or more of the advance in October and November. Okta closed 27.3% below its 9 October high of $232.13 and Rubrik 22.2% below its 28 October high of $136.00, with the sharpest session on 5 November, when the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.52% on doubts about AI data-center spending and the whole security complex sold together. No company news for either name was discoverable in that window. Okta fell 11.2% on 2 November, three days after S&P Dow Jones Indices said it would join the S&P 500. Both have held uptrends since June, which Palo Alto Networks and ServiceNow lost in that single November session; Zscaler, billed per user per year, held one too, so survival does not sort by pricing basis. CrowdStrike, billed per endpoint, grew revenue 25.8% with record net new ARR up 51% in its July quarter, faster than either protagonist's contracted book.

Strip the agent premium out of both and what is left is one accelerating meter and one fading one. Okta's margins account for the profitability half of its doubling and nothing of the growth half, because its base is human seats sold against Microsoft's bundled Entra, where 107% retention is what a mature directory earns. Rubrik's 119% retention compounds instead, because protected capacity inside an account grows whether or not the headcount does, and that is the business the market has priced the more expensively of the two.

The test arrives in the same week for both. Okta reports third-quarter results on 1 December and Rubrik on 3 December, which settles the question of whose quarter had merely printed first.
