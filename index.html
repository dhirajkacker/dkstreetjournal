The molecule is a pass-through; the pipe is the business

In the June quarter ONE Gas, which operates as Oklahoma Natural Gas, Kansas Gas Service and Texas Gas Service, booked $411.6m of revenue against a $90.3m cost of natural gas, versus $423.7m and $117.9m a year earlier. Revenue fell 2.9%; margin net of gas cost rose about 5.1%. That gas cost is recovered dollar for dollar through periodic adjustment clauses, so a cold winter or a spot spike inflates both lines and leaves the earning line untouched.

What does move it is approved capital recovery. ONE Gas attributed its first-quarter operating income gain "primarily" to "an increase of $27.3 million from new rates". Its 2026 docket is a stack of between-case trackers: a $28.7m Oklahoma performance-based increase recommended by an administrative law judge in June, a $36.9m Texas increase approved in June, a $14.3m Kansas reliability surcharge requested, all against authorized returns on equity of 9.4% to 9.8%. One Texas statute, House Bill 4384, which lets a gas utility defer carrying costs on plant not yet in rates, is worth about $0.42 of 2026 adjusted earnings per share, roughly 9% of guidance. Capital spending runs near $800m against a $6.3bn average rate base, while customer count grew 0.43%.

"This performance gives us the confidence to raise our adjusted earnings expectations for the full year," chief executive Robert S. McAnnally said in the second-quarter results release.

The cash wedge the pass-through famously creates has closed. At 30 June ONE Gas carried $19.8m of under-recovered gas costs against $22.4m over-recovered; the Winter Storm Uri bill was refinanced with ratepayer-backed securitized bonds, including $336m of Kansas bonds in November 2022. Its negative 3.7% trailing free-cash-flow yield is the capex program, not the molecule.

Its September filings show who the real counterparty is. The rating arrived, and it went against the company

Alignment Healthcare, a Medicare Advantage insurer that runs its own health plans in California, North Carolina and Nevada, lost the fourth star on its largest contract. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services published the 2027 star ratings on 8 October, and the company disclosed the same day that California HMO contract H3815, which serves approximately 75% of its health-plan membership, expects 3.5 stars for 2027 against 4.0 for 2026. Six other eligible contracts held four stars or better, three of them at 4.5. The share of Alignment's members sitting in four-star-or-better plans next year falls to roughly 25% from about 98%.

Through the summer Alignment was read as evidence that a small capitated insurer can out-execute the majors while they retreat: it raised full-year guidance on 30 July with the best cost ratio in its public life. The file contradicts none of that. It removes money from a line none of those meters touch, and removes it in 2028.

What a star is worth, and when it pays

The rating is a price lever. In most counties a plan at four stars or better has its county benchmark raised by five percentage points, ten in designated double-bonus counties, and the rebate percentage, the share of the gap between bid and benchmark a plan keeps to fund supplemental benefits, steps from 50% below 3.5 stars to 65% and then 70%. H3815's half-step forfeits the benchmark bonus while staying inside the 65% rebate band. Industry-wide, quality bonuses run about $13bn a year, and the 2027 ratings govern what is paid in 2028, which is why Alignment can say it anticipates no impact on 2026 or 2027 revenue and be right. TD Cowen put the annual cost of the lost star at roughly $175m, against the $96.7m of EBITDA consensus expects for all of 2026. That single comparison is the case for the de-rating.

The accounts were going the other way

June-quarter revenue was $1,335.6m, up 31.6%, with gross profit up 35.5% and operating income up 85.1%; the adjusted medical benefit ratio came in at 86.3%, forty basis points better than a year earlier. Derived from reported revenue and the disclosed 294,100 members, revenue per member per month was about $1,514 against roughly $1,507 a year earlier. The growth line is enrolment, almost none of it price, which is precisely why a badge matters: the contract carrying three-quarters of the members will be shopped at 3.5 stars when open enrollment starts on 15 October.

The next print is guided lower by design, to $1.30–1.32bn of revenue and $20–30m of adjusted EBITDA on a seasonally higher cost ratio. At Baird's healthcare conference on 15 September management named more aggressive hospital billing, heavier disputes and appeals and longer skilled-nursing stays, and disclosed $10–11m of unplanned second-half spending; the shares fell 19.9% that session. "Whenever you go through a claims application implementation, those are messy and noisy," chief executive John Kao said that day. A whistleblower suit disclosed in early July, alleging about $8–10m of operating expenses were booked as capital expenditure in 2024, remains unresolved; the company calls the claims meritless.

Clover's fifth star is sitting in an appeals court

Clover Health, which sells PPO and HMO Medicare Advantage plans built around physician software, was awarded 5 stars on its PPO plans and 4.5 on the HMO, with the PPO carrying about 97% of its members and a HEDIS score of 4.82 out of 5. "Success in the Star ratings system means an enhanced ability to support our members and to continue to provide them market leading plans," chief executive Andrew Toy said on 8 October. The top rating is conditional: CMS calculated it excluding the twenty measures covered by a 27 May 2026 district-court decision in Clover's favour, which is on appeal, and with those measures included the PPO would have been 4.5.

The underlying book is one business, not three. Insurance revenue of $737.8m was 99.3% of the $743.2m June-quarter total, the insurance benefit expense ratio improved eighty basis points to 87.6%, and gross profit per member works out near $324 a month on 157,309 members, against roughly $205 at Alignment on a book nearly twice the size. Clover trades at 4.47x forward price-to-gross-profit; Alignment is at 2.44x, having started September at 4.13x.

What the file settles and what it leaves open

None of Alignment's decline is explained by its reported results, which improved on every meter it discloses. The lost bonus dollar is nonetheless larger than this year's expected EBITDA and will not appear in a financial statement for eight quarters, so both readings of the price are defensible, and the sell side has written both at once: JPMorgan cut its target from $22 to $10 while keeping its Overweight rating, KeyCorp from $28 to $12. The real question is sequencing: whether the appeals and the measures Alignment says it intends to litigate restore the star before the 2028 payment year, and whether a selling season spent at 3.5 stars slows the enrolment engine that is the whole revenue story. The company says it remains confident of returning the California contract to at least four stars.

Seniors start choosing on 15 October from a page that shows one number per plan. Whatever the lawyers eventually produce, it will not change the number on that page this season.

Navitas Semiconductor closed its purchase of Claros on 6 October, paying up to $232.8m — about $216.0m at closing, roughly $125m of it cash — for the ability to put a voltage regulator directly beneath a high-current processor. The dollar arrives late, and through someone else

Monolithic, a Kirkland, Washington designer of DC-to-DC converters that step voltage down for servers, cars and industrial gear, is paid for content on a board that was designed years before it shipped. It is also paid at one remove: 88% of sales in both the three and six months to 30 June 2026 ran through distribution arrangements whose enforceable rights and obligations are with distributors and resellers, not end customers, and two of those distributors were 28% and 15% of revenue. Reported revenue is, to that extent, a distributor ordering pattern.

What it reported was acceleration. On the 30 July call, management said channel inventory "remains very low" and book-to-bill was "well above 1.0x." Founder-chief executive Michael Hsing raised the year's floor for the segment: "I think right now, based on what we can see, we're willing to raise the floor for that particular end market from 85% for the year to 130% for the year," he told investors.

The number nobody discloses

The quarterly release breaks out revenue by end market against a single consolidated gross margin. There is no margin by segment, and there is no disclosure anywhere of how many of the power phases on a given accelerator board are Monolithic's rather than a second source's. That is the quantity in dispute. Rosenblatt, cutting the stock to Neutral, allowed that Monolithic is "capturing the pole position in power delivery to accelerator sockets" while arguing Nvidia's roadmap looks to have Texas Instruments and perhaps Analog Devices in the Rubin cycle. Its $880 target, set on an assumed $22 of fiscal 2026 earnings per share, now sits far below both the $1,386.29 price and the $27.45 consensus — a stale price view carrying a live competitive observation.

By the time a lost phase reaches the income statement, the platform carrying it has already shipped.

The other side of the socket

Navitas is what the same end market looks like before the revenue arrives. EMCOR Group, the US and UK specialty contractor that designs, installs and commissions the electrical and mechanical systems inside buildings, finished June with more work under contract than at any point in its history and raised its profit forecast for the year. Its shares have gone nowhere since the summer and sit 16% below their 2026 high.

The layer of the artificial-intelligence buildout that actually pulls the conduit has been sold off since industry executives publicly called for the industry to slow its own spending, which Reuters described as investors repricing AI capital-expenditure risk across the supply chain. A contractor's revenue line cannot settle whether that worry is right. Under percentage-of-completion accounting, revenue is cost incurred to date measured against management's estimate of a job's total cost, so reported revenue is a progress estimate and reported margin is a forecast, revised every quarter, with jobs signed two years ago landing in the current period.

The order book grew faster than the work burned off

Remaining performance obligations, meaning contracted work not yet performed, reached $17.14bn at 30 June, up 43.9% from a year earlier, with the largest gains in network and communications, water and wastewater, and institutional and healthcare work. That balance was $13.25bn entering the year and $15.62bn at the end of March. Set the $1.52bn quarterly increase against the $5.155bn of revenue recognized, and the implied book-to-bill, derived from those two balances, is roughly 1.3x. Operating margin widened by about one percentage point to 10.6% on 19.8% revenue growth, and guidance went to $32.00–33.25 of diluted earnings a share on revenue of $20.00–20.50bn.

The buildout's effect on the work itself is scope, not square footage. An AI data center carries a content "multiplier of 1.5 to 2" for mechanical systems and "probably 1.5" for electrical work against a conventional facility, chief executive Anthony Guzzi told investors on 30 July. Electrical construction revenue in the network and communications sector rose 45% in the quarter.

Where the customer's cash actually goes

EMCOR holds $2.609bn of contract liabilities, amounts invoiced and advanced ahead of revenue recognized, against $377.3m of contract assets at the end of March, per its quarterly filing. That float is not an earnings engine: net interest income was $4.322m in the June quarter, under 1% of the $547.3m of operating income, and cash fell from $1.112bn to $924.4m over the half as the customer money financed unbilled work and growth.

What would settle the margin question is the split between fixed-price jobs, where labor inflation and an unpriced change order are the contractor's loss, and cost-reimbursable work, where they are the customer's. EMCOR's annual report describes that risk allocation without quantifying the mix.

The one meter that would settle it is not in the filings.

The exposure is not small in principle: electrical work runs 45% to 70% of a data center's construction cost, and construction labor costs in primary North American data-center markets rose 8% to 12% a year in 2024-25. Microsoft president Brad Smith has called the electrician shortage the "#1 problem" for US data-center expansion. EMCOR's answer is union labor mobility and prefabrication, and the trace shows in output per head: FY2025 revenue across 44,000 employees works out near $386,000 each, and the 2026 guidance midpoint near $460,000, a derived 19% gain without more bodies.

Argan is the undiversified version of the same accounting

Argan, a lump-sum engineering and construction firm that builds whole gas-fired power plants, is where estimate risk has already bitten. Consolidated gross margin stepped down from 25.0% to 21.0% to 19.3% over three quarters, and the industrial segment fell to a 7.3% margin on two projects with higher estimates-to-complete than initially estimated. "As projects ramp and get into their second and third year of construction, we expect to see higher revenues and with successful execution, we have the opportunity to enhance margin," chief financial officer Joshua Baugher said on 2 September. Backlog fell from $2.9bn to $2.5bn, 80% of it four US gas plants. Here the float does flatter earnings: FY2026 net income of $137.8m exceeded operating income of $134.7m despite tax, against cash and investments of $895.0m.

Dycom, the telecom-infrastructure contractor, is barely on the same meter. It is paid per unit of work under master service agreements, which were 76.9% of contract revenues in fiscal 2022, and AT&T, Verizon with Frontier, and Lumen together supplied about half of fiscal 2026 revenue. Its backlog rose 53.2% to $12.242bn, of which more than $1bn is fiber tied to data centers, about 8% of the book.

Three names, one shelf, three different reasons for the price

Over the three months to 9 October, EMCOR was up 0.8% while Argan fell 42.9% and Dycom 37.0%. Only Argan's decline has a reported business change behind it: a margin trajectory down three quarters, a backlog that shrank, and a year carried by a handful of jobs against a company capacity management puts at 10 to 12 at once. At 29.7x forward earnings and 23.6x trailing enterprise value to EBITDA after a 51% fall from its high, that reads as a justified re-rating. EMCOR's own multiple has compressed from 31.4x trailing in early May to 24.6x, against Comfort Systems at 42.2x trailing and 35.0x forward, a gap part-explained by Comfort's 50.3% June-quarter revenue growth. Dycom trades at 15.8x forward against 24.3x trailing. For EMCOR and Dycom the order books and guidance moved the other way from the shares, and the likelier reading is the group being repriced as one block; what cannot be checked from outside is whether EMCOR's growing book is taking on fixed-price risk as it goes.

That is the uncomfortable part of a business whose revenue is an estimate. EMCOR reports third-quarter results on 29 October, and the order book will be printed to the decimal. The figure that decides what it is worth will not be.

Newmont told investors in July that producing an ounce of gold had cost it $1,621 in the June quarter, against $1,029 three months before. Nothing in the mine plan of a company that digs gold in ten countries changed by 58% in ninety days. The step came from the timing of sustaining capital, from by-product credits that had flattered the earlier figure, and from the incremental costs of running Cadia in New South Wales through the downtime that followed a magnitude 4.5 earthquake there on 14 April.

All-in sustaining cost is the figure the gold industry publishes as its margin meter, and revenue is where investors look for the metal price passing through. For a producer this size, neither line does the job it is asked to do. Newmont's realised price was $4,900 an ounce in the first quarter and $4,414 in the second; set against the two cost figures, the spread per ounce narrowed 27.9% while reported revenue fell 16.3%. The income statement understated the squeeze by roughly twelve percentage points, and the cost line did marginally more of the damage than the weaker gold price.

What the cost per ounce leaves on the floor

The measure is defined to exclude the next mine. Agnico Eagle, which mines in Canada, Mexico and Finland, reported total cash costs of $1,054 an ounce and all-in sustaining cost of $1,459 in the June quarter: the $405 difference is sustaining capital and related charges. Growth capital sits outside both. Newmont plans $1.4bn of development capital this year and spent $524m of it in the first half, which against guidance of 5.26m to 5.3m attributable ounces works out at roughly $265 an ounce of spending the cost line never shows.

Which is why the cost step and the cash machine can run in opposite directions. Newmont still generated record free cash flow of $2.2bn in the June quarter, after $3.1bn and a raised $6bn repurchase authorisation in the March quarter. The shares trade at 12.6x forward earnings against 14.8x trailing, with a 7.8% trailing free-cash-flow yield.

The $580 an ounce that geography costs

AngloGold Ashanti, which mines in Africa, Australia and the Americas and reports in dollars, spent $2,039 an ounce in the same quarter, up 22.4% year on year, with sustaining capital of $332m against $273m. Of the increase in its cash costs, the company attributed $216 an ounce to macroeconomic factors and $29 to operational ones. "Royalties, fuel, broad inflation, FX basically accounted for all of the increase," chief executive Alberto Calderon said on the results call; chief financial officer Gillian Doran told the same 31 July call that "the upward pressure on costs for the industry were particularly acute this quarter." A fatality at Obuasi on 24 April cost two weeks of operation, and the mine has been running without its ore-pass system after a catastrophic failure.

Agnico's ounce came out of record throughput: the Detour Lake mill ran at 80,275 tonnes a day and produced 207,279 ounces, up 23.2% year on year. The company has hedged about 60% of its remaining 2026 Canadian-dollar exposure in a 1.38 to 1.42 collar. Dollar Tree's summer gross margin was the widest the chain has printed in years, and four-fifths of the improvement was a refund of duties the Supreme Court decided the government never had the authority to collect. After February's ruling that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act did not support the tariffs levied under it, roughly $166bn of duties began returning to importers of record, with interest, through a Customs and Border Protection refund portal. The bridges were read as proof that the trade-down shopper is finally paying off at the margin line. All three companies are instead handing the refund to that shopper as lower shelf prices, which makes the expansion a one-year event; and underneath it, only one of the three businesses got better.

The refund, by how much of the margin it bought

At Dollar Tree, which sells a fixed-price assortment of food, household basics and seasonal general merchandise, 680 of 850 basis points of gross-margin expansion in the quarter ended 1 August was net tariff impact, taking gross margin to 42.9%. At Ollie's, the closeout chain that buys brand-name overstock and liquidation lots, the refund was worth 380 basis points of a 360 basis-point expansion: all of it, and more. At Dollar General, the rural small-box chain, 81 of 127 basis points.

None of the three banked it. "We will not hesitate to invest beyond this level to strengthen our price leadership position," Eric van der Valk, Ollie's president and chief executive, told investors on 2 September. Dollar Tree's price is printed before the cost is known

Dollar Tree publishes its price in advance, so landed cost, freight and duty land in gross margin rather than in revenue. The levers are the item behind the price and the share of the assortment lifted above the $1.25 anchor: multi-price merchandise reached 17% of total sales, up about 400 basis points year over year, across roughly 6,600 stores. "Rather than passing those costs on to the customer, we've taken advantage of the fact that we're getting that lower tariff rate in and that tariff rate is absorbing inflation," chief financial officer Stewart Glendinning said on 27 August.

That absorption has a term. Ollie's buying was never the problem

Ollie's margin is a buying outcome, and the buying was fine: TJX, Ross and Burlington all describe closeout supply as highly favorable. Demand was the problem. What the pump price decides

The refund accounts for four-fifths of Dollar Tree's margin gain, roughly two-thirds of Dollar General's and all of Ollie's. What it does not explain is why the traffic went one way. There is also a claim on the windfall itself, since retailers that passed the duties to shoppers now face consumer class actions arguing the refunds belong to those shoppers.

All three report again in December. By then the refund is spent money, and what is left on the shelf is a price each company has promised to hold without it.

Meta launched a consumer artificial-intelligence assistant called Muse in late September, and among the shares that fell hardest were those of a company whose largest earnings engine is holding other people's idle cash. Charles Schwab, the retail brokerage and custodian for independent advisers that also owns banks, dropped 6.1% on 22 September to close at $100.35, on 16.9m shares against a typical 5m to 9m, its heaviest-volume down day of the quarter. LPL Financial fell 7% and Raymond James 3.5% on the same reasoning; the Nasdaq Composite rose 0.45% that day.

The trade priced a belief — that an assistant managing a household's money erodes demand for paid advice. Schwab's own revenue decomposition says that is the thinnest part of the franchise. Managed investing solutions produced $707m of the record $7.07bn of net revenues reported for the June quarter, about a tenth. Net interest revenue was $3.4bn, 47% of the total and up 19% on the year.

The line that got sold is not the line that pays

That $3.4bn is a banking spread wearing a brokerage's clothes. Uninvested client balances are swept off the brokerage statement into Schwab's own banks, reinvested, and paid back to the client at a rate Schwab sets. Transactional sweep cash ended June at $485.7bn, roughly 3.7% of $13.08trn in client assets, with on-balance-sheet bank deposits of $249.7bn and a further $70.6bn parked at third-party banks, where fees rose 35% on improved net yield. Margin loans add a second spread, at $165.1bn of client balances.

Six days before Muse, the Federal Reserve raised its target range 25bp to 3.75-4.00% in a unanimous vote, its first increase since 2023. A higher funds rate lifts the reinvestment yield on swept cash while the client rate stays administered. Net interest margin had already widened 12 basis points in the quarter to 3.00%, with management guiding fourth-quarter margin to 3.25-3.30%.

One caution the pitch usually omits: the cheap part of that repair is done. Higher-cost bank supplemental funding fell $44.8bn during 2025 to $5.1bn, so further expansion has to come from securities repricing and the policy rate. Asset gathering, meanwhile, accelerated: $120bn of core net new assets in the quarter, then a record August gather of $64.8bn, up 46% year on year.

Schwab lost 9.9% over the thirty sessions to 9 October, to $96.70. Interactive Brokers lost 3.8% in the same stretch and an S&P 500 index fund rose 2.2%, so the de-rating was Schwab's own.

Robinhood is paid rebates — and so is Schwab

Robinhood Markets, the 2,900-employee US platform for stocks, options, gold and crypto, is the supposed mirror: order-flow rebates instead of commissions. The mirror is cloudy. Schwab booked $528m of commissions and $624m of order-flow revenue in the June quarter, and that order-flow line alone exceeded Robinhood's entire $342m options business.

Robinhood's net revenues rose 32% to $1.31bn with net deposits of a record $21.7bn, while crypto revenue fell 38% to $100m and event contracts — regulated betting on outcomes — rose more than tenfold to $156m. Its cash spread is the laggard: net interest revenues grew only 9% to $389m, held back by lower short-term rates and weaker securities lending. Where the two firms differ is deliberate. Schwab chief executive Rick Wurster told the 21 July call the firm would support financial-event and economic-data products but has "no interest" in sports gambling or non-financial betting.

Robinhood fell 6.7% over the same thirty sessions, as bitcoin broke below $81,000 on 8 October and crypto liquidations approached $1bn. At 49.6x forward earnings against 47.2x trailing, consensus has FY26 earnings up 7.7% after 2025's 114%.

Nu's benchmark sits in Brasília

Nu Holdings, the São Paulo digital bank with 139m customers, shares the sector label and none of the plumbing. Its cost of deposits was 88% of the Brazilian interbank CDI rate, three percentage points better than a year earlier, funding an unsecured card and lending book: net interest income of $3.7bn and margin up 180 basis points to 22.9%. Copom cut the Selic to 13.75% on 16 September, the same week the Fed hiked. The blemish is credit — 90-day-plus non-performing loans rose 35 basis points to 6.9%. Nu rose 6.8% over the thirty sessions, trades at 18.5x forward against 21.8x trailing on a 33% return on equity, and reported-dollar revenue growth of 49.9% against 39% excluding currency, so roughly ten points of the headline was the real.

Which anchor you pick decides the answer

The business results account for none of Schwab's fall and arguably argue the other way. What they cannot settle is value, because Schwab's two anchors disagree. On earnings it is 14.9x forward against 17.5x trailing, the gap implying consensus FY26 earnings per share of $6.49, a third above FY25. On bank capital it is near 6.9x tangible book value of $14.10 a share, against a ten-year median of 5.29 — a premium, not a discount. Sell-side opinion split accordingly: Piper Sandler cut its target to $100 on 2 October while Bank of America raised its to $95 and stayed at Underperform.

The three names did not move together because they are not paid alike: Schwab on an administered deposit rate the Fed just made more valuable, Robinhood on volatility and product launches, Nu on a discount to a falling Brazilian benchmark.

Schwab reports third-quarter results before the open on 15 October, with consensus looking for $1.67 a share. The number that matters is the margin on cash, which is the one line Meta's assistant cannot touch.
