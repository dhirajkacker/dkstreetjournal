ServiceNow rewrote its price list this year, and the rewrite answers the question the market has been asking of it since late October. On 9 April 2026 the company replaced five legacy service-management tiers with three AI-native ones — Foundation, Advanced and Prime — bundling Now Assist, Moveworks, Workflow Data Fabric and the AI Control Tower into every one of them, with the old products reaching end-of-sale on 1 July 2026. Customers still license fulfiller users. The agent work runs on a metered consumption pool sitting on top of the seats.

That architecture matters because the belief that halved the shares is that an agent doing a service-desk job removes the person whose license paid for the platform. ServiceNow, which sells workflow software on its Now Platform to large enterprises and governments, has built the opposite billing structure: the human meter stays and a second meter is added beside it. "The customer likes the predictability of the seat-based pricing model for their budgeting purposes, people that run Fortune 2000 companies run on budget," chairman and chief executive Bill McDermott said on the October 28 earnings call, while saying the company is simultaneously driving consumption.

The pool nobody outside can measure

The limit of the disclosure is real. AI usage is charged against a contracted allowance of "assists" rather than invoiced per action, and the company has published neither the assist cost of an action nor the pool size included with a tier, so the consumption share of any contract cannot be computed from outside. Pricing trade coverage puts roughly half of net new sales on non-seat terms as of July 2026, with AI bundles carrying a 20-30% price lift; that figure is second-hand rather than filed, and should be read as indicative.

What is filed points the same way. Current remaining performance obligations were $13.65bn at the end of September, up 21% in constant currency and 100 basis points above guidance; total obligations reached $30bn, growing 23%; the renewal rate held at 98%; customers above $5m of annual contract value rose about 23% to 680. AI net new contract value grew 65%, and McDermott raised the year-end AI target a second time: "We began the year with a $1 billion AI target by year-end. We raised that target to $1.5 billion at Financial Analyst Day in May. Today, we are raising that target again this time to $1.7 billion plus."

The shares fell 25.9% the following session, reported by CNBC as a 27% intraday decline and the worst day in the company's history. The trigger was one guided number: fourth-quarter contracted-obligation growth of 18.5% in constant currency against roughly 19.8% expected. Chief financial officer Gina Mastantuono attributed it to timing. "We saw strong early renewals in Q3 that contributed about half of the beat in Q3. Now that has a negative impact in Q4 because we pulled those deals out of Q4 and into Q3," she told analysts, adding that the measure is "a very nuanced accounting metric." Subscription revenue guidance went up, not down.

Paying the AI bill before the AI invoice

The cost of running two meters shows where the damage actually landed. Cost of revenue rose 56.5% to $1.21bn against 20.5% revenue growth, on third-party cloud commitments, data-center depreciation, intangible amortization and people; gross margin fell to 70.5% from 77.3%. Professional-services gross margin went to -20.0% from +11.0% as the company funded deployment of its own AI. GAAP operating income fell 32.7% to $385m. Management's position is that the services investment carves roughly 25 basis points of subscription revenue in the third quarter and about 50 in the fourth, returning on renewal.

So the demand line and the profit line genuinely disagree, and the shares have sided with the profit line. At $92.16, down from a 20 November low of $87.61, ServiceNow trades at 19.2x 2027 consensus earnings of $4.81 against 31.2x at the pre-print close, and at 8.48x trailing gross profit, below the 8.73x of the April-May panic low. The peers frame the market's logic. What the January quarter settles is not whether agents replace seats. It is whether the assist pool ever bills enough to pay for the hardware already bought to run it.

Memory prices more than doubled this year, and the three companies that stand between the chipmakers and the buyers of computers all got larger and more profitable passing the cost along. The bill arrived somewhere other than the income statement.

Arrow Electronics, TD Synnex and PC Connection have been marked down hard, Arrow by 19.9% in the 30 days to 25 November, TD Synnex by 12.8% and Connection by 7.1%, on a reading that a rising bill of materials is compressing the channel's spread. The reported quarters do not support that where it would show first: all three expanded operating margin. What the pass-through actually cost them is in working capital, and there the three have nothing in common.

From 16% of a parts bill to 23%

J.P. Morgan Global Research estimates DRAM prices will rise more than 400% from the start of 2024 to the end of 2026, because data centres will absorb over 70% of high-end memory output in 2026 and the three makers of more than 95% of dynamic random-access memory are diverting wafers to high-bandwidth memory for accelerators. Gartner puts personal-computer memory at 23% of a machine's total bill of materials, up from 16% in 2025, with average prices up 17%. Worldwide shipments fell 22.4% in the third quarter.

Arrow's spread held and its warehouse filled

Arrow, which distributes semiconductors and interconnect parts to industrial and embedded manufacturers and sells data centre systems to resellers, reported record revenue of $10.72bn for the quarter to 3 October, up 39%, with gross margin flat at 10.82% against 10.83% a year earlier. Operating income rose 140% to $430.3m. Components sales of $7.9bn carried a 5.2% operating margin, 160 basis points better than a year earlier, and management said most of that growth came from memory price inflation it does not expect to repeat.

"If we had gotten our hands on more product, we would have sold more," chief financial officer Rajesh Agrawal told investors on 5 November. "And so the environment itself is very, very strong." Interim chief executive William Austen said book-to-bill ratios remain well above parity and backlog "continues to build into mid-2027."

Getting hands on product is the expensive part.

Inventory rose $1.4bn sequentially to $7.3bn, nearly half of it data-centre related and about $600m of it for the unit that assembles compute and storage racks, and Arrow used $441m of cash in operations in the quarter, leaving year-to-date operating cash flow at $577m. It is funding that cheaply: interest and other expense was $40m on lower average debt, which fell $378m year on year to $2.8bn. The shares trade at 7.2x forward earnings against 8.6x trailing, though the forward figure rests on consensus 2026 earnings of $24.02 a share, up 131%, after annual revenue fell 15.7% in 2024.

TD Synnex billed faster than it could fund

TD Synnex nets roughly 32% of what it bills out of reported revenue, so the $31.8bn it billed in the quarter to 31 August became $21.56bn of revenue, up 37.7%. Gross margin slipped to 6.61% from 6.73%; operating margin still rose to 2.98% from 2.47%. Hyve, its hyperscale build-and-integrate arm, more than doubled billings to $7.0bn, about 22% of the group, earning a 3.61% non-GAAP operating margin on those billings against 1.95% in Distribution, down from 5.04% a year earlier as artificial-intelligence infrastructure took more of the mix.

Free cash flow was negative $975.6m for the quarter, and interest and finance charges rose 32% to $120.5m. Management said in September that it expects positive cash flow in the fourth quarter and that all its businesses should become sustainable cash generators in fiscal 2027. The shares trade at 10.3x forward earnings on a trailing free cash flow yield of minus 4.68%.

Connection sold fewer notebooks for more money

PC Connection, a reseller serving small businesses, large corporates and government and education buyers, grew net sales 23.3% to $874.3m while gross margin fell 104 basis points to 18.51%. Its finance chief attributed half the decline to a thinner mix of netted-down software and half to endpoint hardware sold into large rollouts. Notebook units fell about 5% as device revenue rose 26.6%. Public Sector Solutions managed 4.6% growth, to $138.6m, against 27.8% in the small-business arm.

A $144.8m receivables build and $83m of inventory for large deployments took nine-month operating cash flow to minus $39.8m, against $343.3m of cash on hand. "We think that endpoint demand might be down slightly in '27 from '26," chief executive Timothy McGrath said on 28 October. At 18.4x forward earnings, roughly 2.5 times Arrow's multiple, Connection is the most expensively valued of the three, and consensus already has its revenue growth falling from 13.6% this year to 3.7% next.

One session did most of the marking down

On 5 November the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 12.2% after midterm voters backed measures to slow data centre construction; Arrow closed down 10.4%, Connection 9.9% and TD Synnex 2.6%. Across the 30 days the distributors fell less than the companies on either side of them, with Micron down 33.5%, Dell 36.4% and Hewlett Packard Enterprise 34.8%, while CDW rose 2.1%. The likelier reading is that the group was repriced for artificial-intelligence hardware demand rather than for anything in its own spreads.

The spreads are the part that held. What the market has not marked is the financing: the same cost shock that inflates revenue obliges a distributor to buy the inventory and carry the receivable first, which is why TD Synnex's interest bill grew by a third while Arrow's shrank, and why Connection funded other people's rollouts off its own balance sheet. Arrow's position is the sturdier, with a backlog, falling debt and an assembly book it expects to ship; TD Synnex's is the more exposed, because Hyve programmes consume cash before they bill. Arrow's fourth-quarter guide of $11.55bn to $12.15bn assumes unit volume, management having said component price increases were completed in the third quarter, and TrendForce now sees conventional contract prices rising 10% to 15% in the fourth quarter after far steeper increases earlier in the year.

If the price tailwind fades on that schedule, the revenue it created leaves the income statement on its own, and the $7.3bn of inventory stays in the warehouse waiting for someone to want it at cost.

Linde, which pipes industrial and specialty gases to chipmakers, refiners and hospitals, told investors in late October that electronics was its fastest-growing end market, with sales there up 15% from a year earlier. Through the month that followed, its shares rose. The companies selling filters, slurries, vacuum pumps and plating chemistry into those same fabs were marked down by about a tenth.

The fall was read as the artificial-intelligence materials trade deflating. Every order book on the shelf says otherwise, which leaves the form of the contract, not the state of demand, as the thing that separated the winners from the losers. Gas for a fab is sold under long-term take-or-pay agreements: the revenue arrives whether or not the customer's capital plan accelerates, and it therefore carries no premium for an acceleration that can later be revoked. A filter qualified into a process flow, or a power supply booked into a tool build, carries exactly that premium.

Two ways to be paid by the same fab

Linde has roughly $2bn of electronics projects under construction in the United States, Korea and China, committed $1bn on 31 July 2026 to expand its Phoenix site for a major chipmaker, and lifted full-year adjusted earnings guidance to a range of $17.82 to $17.92 a share. It gained 3.4% over the 30 days to 25 November; Air Products, the other gas member, added 1.0%. Over the same window Entegris fell 8.5%, MKS 9.1%, Element Solutions 8.7% and Photronics 10.2%.

The gas pair's calm is not better news from the fabs. It is less news of the kind that can be taken back.

The consumables annuity is the levered half

Entegris, which supplies microcontamination filters, ultra-pure chemicals, polishing consumables and wafer-handling products to logic and memory makers, reported September-quarter sales of $1,006m, up 25%, with gross margin at 49.7% and operating margin at 22.9%. Book-to-bill ran above 1.0 and backlog roughly doubled year on year. Guidance for the December quarter is $1.025bn to $1.055bn, about 26% above a year earlier.

"Unit-driven revenue accelerated from approximately 6% in the first quarter to 20% in the third quarter, that is roughly twice the rate of underlying wafer start growth we expect for the year," chief executive Dave Reeder told investors on 29 October. The company says about three-quarters of its business is unit-driven and roughly a quarter to a third capital-expenditure-linked; the two figures do not sum cleanly. What complicates the annuity story is that the capex-linked line grew more than 35% in the quarter, faster than the consumption half, so a pause in fab construction would register more than the framing suggests.

It would also land on the more indebted balance sheet of the two. Entegris paid $43.9m of net interest in the quarter on $3.26bn of long-term debt, at 2.9x net leverage, against MKS's $33m, down from $45m a year earlier, at 2.4x. China fell to 18% of Entegris's nine-month sales from 21%.

MKS says its chemistry lines are full

MKS Inc., which sells vacuum and pressure control, radio-frequency power, lasers and printed-circuit-board drilling systems alongside the Atotech plating-chemistry business, grew September-quarter revenue 43% to $1,413m at a 23.1% operating margin, and guided December to $1.49bn give or take $40m. Chemistry equipment sales rose more than 75% year on year. "Current capacity is sold out through 2027, and to meet accelerated chemistry equipment demand, we are expanding capacity in Guangzhou, China," chief executive John T.C. Lee said on 5 November. On 19 October the company agreed to sell its Ophir optics unit to TTM Technologies for $650m, at more than 18x that unit's projected adjusted earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization, against MKS's own 13.0x trailing enterprise multiple.

Of the four marked-down names, one earns it on disclosure. Photronics, which makes photomasks, widened fiscal fourth-quarter revenue guidance to $207m-227m citing increased uncertainty, and cut capital spending plans to $255m-305m from $330m. Element Solutions, whose MacDermid Alpha arm competes with Atotech in plating chemistry, grew electronics sales 18% organically; its drawdown dates to the collapse of its $14.5bn sale to Solstice Advanced Materials, abandoned by both boards on 27 August with no break fee.

What the quarters do and do not explain

None of the 30-day decline is traceable to the protagonists' own reporting. Both beat and raised. The wafer-fab-equipment makers they supply fell two to three times harder over the same days, Teradyne 31% and Applied Materials 24%, and the Nasdaq 100 entered its first bear market since April 2025 on 25 November after a tariff announcement and hotter inflation data. The likelier reading is a repricing of the discount rate applied to equipment-cycle revenue, which is precisely the revenue the gas contracts do not contain.

The repricing has been large. MKS trades at 12.4x forward earnings against 20.6x trailing and roughly 29.6x in early May; Entegris at 29.2x forward against 49.9x trailing and about 41x in May, when its 2027 ramp was still a forecast rather than a reported quarter. Entegris remains the dearest name on the shelf, cheap only against its own history. Every member is still higher over twelve months, Entegris by 74%.

The next hard figures are roughly nine weeks out, and neither company has fixed a date. Before them sits MKS's investor day on 14 December, where a management team that says its chemistry capacity is committed through 2027 meets a market that has halved what it will pay for the earnings.

CrowdStrike agreed on 23 November to buy a rival in network security entirely with its own shares, and lost roughly $21bn of market value in the two sessions that followed. The target was Zscaler, the zero-trust network access vendor, valued at about $43.9bn at a fixed 1.135 CrowdStrike shares per Zscaler share, a 34% premium that leaves existing CrowdStrike holders with roughly 84.1% of the combined company. The issuance of some 191m new shares, close to a fifth of the existing count, mattered more to the market than a quarter that beat on revenue at $1.58bn with raised guidance.

That was the last act of a month in which the whole group was marked down. Okta, Cloudflare, Rubrik, CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, Fortinet and Zscaler all fell over the 30 days to 25 November, by an average of 17.1%, with Fortinet worst at -27.2% and Okta mildest at -9.0%. The fall has been read as security's AI premium deflating — as evidence that agentic software is about to eat seats and budgets. Across three companies that are paid in three genuinely different ways, the contracted revenue says that happened at exactly one of them.

The two catalysts were company-specific

Fortinet lit the fuse. Product revenue rose 37% to $765m in the September quarter, a sharp step down from 52% growth the quarter before, and the shares fell more than 20% despite the third full-year guidance raise of the year, to $8.125-8.215bn. Both events landed on top of the 5 November session, the biggest technology selloff since Covid, which repriced anything whose value sits in profits expected years out.

Cloudflare's meter ran faster than its price

Cloudflare, which bills consumption on a global edge network rather than seats, stepped down out of its strongest uptrend on 3 November, two sessions before it reported. Revenue growth had accelerated for three straight quarters, 33.5% to 35.9% to 39.2%, reaching $782.5m. Remaining performance obligations hit $2.877bn, up 34%, with the portion due inside a year up 37%. Dollar-based net retention of 122% was a four-year high, and the company passed 5,000 customers paying more than $100,000 a year, now 75% of revenue.

The beat came from variable usage: customers burned through prepaid pools of funds faster than modeled and renewed early at larger sizes. "Between July 1st and today, we added approximately 5% of the entire web to Cloudflare," chief executive Matthew Prince told investors on 5 November. "That's wild." Chief financial officer Thomas Seifert guided GAAP profitability to the fourth quarter, against an investor-day target of 2028 at the latest. On a non-GAAP basis the quarter earned $137.1m of operating income; the GAAP statement shows a $67.9m operating loss.

What the shares still carry is the level. Cloudflare trades at 47.6x forward gross profit, roughly three times Rubrik's 16.4x, with consensus revenue of $3.914bn in 2027 required to justify it. Gross margin fell 290bp to 72.4% as lower-margin developer products took more cost of revenue.

Rubrik's net-new line accelerated into a 20% fall

Rubrik, which sells multi-year data-protection and cyber-recovery subscriptions priced on data under management, fell 20.5%. Its last reported quarter showed subscription ARR of $1.66bn, up 33%, with net new subscription ARR of roughly $96m, up 35% year over year, and cloud ARR up 39% to $1.48bn as the maintenance base converts. Full-year guidance went up, to $1.88-1.885bn of ARR and $323-333m of free cash flow. The growth is organic; the Strata Identity acquisition added nothing to subscription ARR.

The case against is two lines: revenue growth has decelerated from 46.3% to 37.9% over three quarters, and gross margin compressed 250bp to 78.4%.

Okta is where the books agree with the shares

Okta sells per-user cloud identity, and per-user is the model the agentic thesis threatens. Revenue growth has slowed for four consecutive quarters to 10.6%. The twelve-month subscription backlog was $2.585bn, up 14%, guided to 11-12% growth, and net retention sits at 107%. Chief financial officer Brett Tighe told investors in August that AI-agent contribution will not be material this fiscal year; Okta discloses no machine-identity unit count at all, even as CyberArk's 2025 landscape put machine identities at roughly 82 times human ones.

What Okta did deliver is margin: quarterly operating margin went from 3.1% to 13.3% over four quarters, and full-year revenue guidance was raised to $3.216-3.226bn. At 51.4x forward earnings, against roughly 36x in mid-July when the shares were at $139.53, the multiple is paid for that margin expansion and not for growth, since consensus models 10.0% revenue growth next year.

What the reported books can and cannot explain

One of the three de-ratings has a basis in the numbers the company reported. Okta's decelerating backlog on a per-seat book is exactly the configuration that compresses an identity multiple, which is why its own guidance already embeds the slowdown and why a 9% fall barely touches a stock up 146.8% over twelve months. At Cloudflare and Rubrik the contracted lines moved the other way, and the likelier reading of their falls is duration repricing plus the dilution shock, not demand. The split has a cause in how each is paid: a consumption meter and a data-volume subscription both grow when agents proliferate, while a seat price only grows if someone buys seats.

Two of the three answer inside a week. Rubrik reports on 30 November against its own guide of $1,829-1,839m of subscription ARR, and Okta on 1 December. The company that told the market to expect less is the one whose shares have least to lose.

Applied Optoelectronics told the market on 24 November that a hyperscale data-centre customer it already supplies had committed to roughly $2.2bn of minimum purchases running from December 2026 to December 2030. It is the company's second such agreement since October, and the larger one by a wide margin. It arrived in the middle of the worst stretch the optical suppliers have had.

That timing is the story. November's selling was read as evidence that the order book behind artificial-intelligence networking had peaked; what the disclosures of the past three weeks show is contracted demand getting firmer at both of the group's order-book businesses while the shares fell. The genuine deterioration is narrower, and it sits at the per-module line and in the share count paying for the factories.

What the minimums cover

The new agreement specifies about $1.5bn of 800-gigabit transceivers in 2027 and roughly $0.7bn of laser diodes across 2028 and 2029, some intended for co-packaged optics from 2028. Set against the $2.57bn of fiscal 2027 revenue that consensus already carries, that 2027 tranche underwrites close to 58% of next year's expected sales, a figure derived from the two disclosures. The company said the same customer's forecast demand may exceed $10bn over four years; only the $2.2bn is committed.

It was signalled in advance.

"The second LTA is in the final stage. We expect to sign it within a few weeks. The size will be much, much bigger than first one, will be even 5x bigger or even more than that," founder and chief executive Thompson Lin said on the 5 November earnings call. The announcement followed nineteen days later.

The first agreement, from October, covers 1.6-terabit transceivers through May 2029, obliges the customer to take 10% of certain 1.6-terabit needs, and is expected to produce $1.0–1.5bn. The quarterly filing also records that the company agreed to certain maximum pricing levels and that "LTAs of this type are not typical for the industry and AOI has not been a party to an agreement of this type before."

The margin is set by speed grade; the capacity is paid for in shares

September-quarter revenue of $269.9m was up 127.5% year on year, the sixth consecutive record, and net income of $6.3m returned the company to profit on a reported basis. Gross margin did not come with it: 28.7% on a non-GAAP basis against a 29.0–30.5% guide, because 400-gigabit product supplied $93.4m of data-centre revenue and 800-gigabit only $47.5m. The operating line was still negative, at -$1.4m.

Funding the ramp has cost holders their share of it. Diluted shares were 87.8m in the quarter against 63.3m a year earlier, fourth-quarter guidance assumes 96m, and the company has authorised roughly $2.25bn of at-the-market programmes, including $550m launched on 17 November. Its $124.9m of 2.75% convertible notes carry a conversion price near $43.31, above the market. Capacity, meanwhile, slipped: the end-2026 target fell to more than 500,000 units a month from 650,000, with a Sugar Land expansion phase delayed on electricity and power logistics.

Everything with an AI label fell together

From 23 October to 24 November the four optical names fell 41.4% (Applied Optoelectronics), 35.6% (Lumentum), 35.2% (Adtran) and 24.5% (Ciena). Over the same sessions Marvell fell 42.4% and Nvidia 28.6%, against a 5.4% decline in the S&P 500 tracker. Applied Optoelectronics lost 19.3% on 5 November, which was attributed to fourth-quarter guidance below Wall Street projections; the other three fell that day without a disclosure of their own, as voters rejected data centres in 40 of 41 local ballot measures. On 20 November, Nvidia alone dropped 14%.

The component-pricing explanation does not survive contact with Lumentum, which makes the lasers inside the modules: its September-quarter gross margin was 51.0% against 34.0% a year earlier, on revenue up 136.5%. Adtran, the one member with no meaningful AI revenue, shows where the cost went. "Generally speaking, I mean, it's 80%-90% supply cost," chief executive Tom Stanton said on 3 November, naming optics, lasers, semiconductors and memory; its revenue grew 0.6% and it trades at 0.8x trailing gross profit.

Ciena's $8.5bn comes up for inspection

Ciena, which sells coherent optical transport systems and Blue Planet automation software to carriers and cloud operators, left its July quarter with $8.5bn of backlog, $800m more than three months earlier, most of it with 2027 request dates, and guided to more than $10bn by year-end. Revenue rose 37.0%, gross margin reached 45.4% and operating margin went from 6.0% to 18.0%. "I think it's almost entirely just driven by a function of lead times. The demand is absolutely there," chief executive Gary Smith told analysts on 3 September. The shares change hands at 20.5x forward earnings against 52.6x trailing, and at 13.0x trailing gross profit against 31.1x in early May; brokers cut targets through November while keeping their ratings, Evercore ISI to $375 from $550.

Nothing disclosed in the past three weeks shows a customer stepping back.

So the results account for almost none of the move. Revenue at both companies accelerated through the drawdown, and the single figure that genuinely worsened at Applied Optoelectronics, its 28.7% module margin against 31.0% a year earlier, is a mix outcome the long-term agreements are designed to shift toward higher speed grades. What the market repriced is the certainty of 2027, and it repriced it across Nvidia and Marvell in the same sessions. The conditional part is specific: 8.8x fiscal 2027 consensus earnings exists only if $2.57bn of revenue arrives, and arrives at a margin that services a share count half again as large as last year's. Applied Optoelectronics now says its ceiling is its own supply, not its customers': more than $450m of 1.6-terabit orders sit behind $50–70m of deliverable fourth-quarter revenue, held up by signal-processor availability.

Both of those contracted books are promises about shipments nobody has seen yet. Ciena opens its own before the market on 10 December, the first occasion since the selling began for anyone to check whether the backlog reads the way it did in September, and whether the orders behind it still carry the request dates they were booked with.
