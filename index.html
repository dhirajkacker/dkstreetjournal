Vicor's October quarter answered a question the design-win announcements could not: how much of the 800-volt data-center power transition is billable today. The Andover, Massachusetts maker of modular DC-DC converters and custom power systems reported $197.1m of revenue for its third quarter, and $66.6m of that was royalties on a patent it is asserting against twenty respondents at the US International Trade Commission.

The belief the shares carried into October was that the architecture pays its component suppliers now. NVIDIA's own schedule puts the content later: the 800-volt rack is an optional configuration for Vera Rubin, with broader adoption expected at Rubin Ultra in the second half of 2027, the generation whose Kyber racks draw 600kW. Vicor and Navitas Semiconductor, the two specialists built for that conversion chain, each lost about a third of their market value over the thirty days to 22 October. Each has found a different way to cross the gap to 2027, and only one of them is being paid to wait.

The docket became a revenue line

On 9 October the trade commission instituted an investigation of vertical power delivery modules and computing systems on Vicor's complaint over US Patent No. 10,903,734, naming Delta Electronics, Infineon Technologies, Monolithic Power Systems, Flex, Celestica, Quanta and Hon Hai among the respondents, and seeking exclusion and cease-and-desist orders. The accused products are the systems that power AI accelerators, graphics processors and tensor processing units, which means importers of unlicensed designs need a license from Vicor. Vicor's royalty rates escalate across stages tied to how far that investigation has progressed; it has signed a first vertical-power-delivery license with a leading AI original-equipment manufacturer at stage-two rates, and that customer placed a further significant order at the start of the fourth quarter.

Royalties of $66.6m were up from $30.4m in the prior quarter. Strip them out of both periods and the implied product revenue still rose, from about $113.0m to about $130.5m, a derived increase of roughly 15.5%. Advanced Products reached $139m, up 47.5% sequentially and 70.4% of the total. One-year backlog was $406m at quarter-end, up 7% sequentially with book-to-bill above 1, and gross margin expanded from 58.0% to 68.3%. Licensing added to hardware growth here; it did not replace it.

"So as of yesterday, the backlog is actually closer to $400 million," Patrizio Vinciarelli, Vicor's chief executive, told investors on the 20 October call, marking the figure down slightly from the quarter-end number. The mechanism he described is physical. "Every generation of AI processor is stepping up its current requirements...it is now at the thousand amp level and on its way to thousands of amps," Vinciarelli said on the same call. Vicor's capacity to answer that is dated: equipment deployment at its second fab begins in the second quarter of 2027, funded from $513m of cash without a raise.

The fourth quarter steps down

Guidance is where the composition bites. Product revenue is guided to roughly 10% sequential growth and royalties to a minimum of $43m, which works out to about $186m in total, some 5% below the third quarter. The quarter that beat consensus on both lines, $1.33 against $1.29 of expected earnings per share and $197.1m against $183.7m, is not the quarter being annualized.

The shares rose 7.1% on the print to $204.14. The 13.1% break came two sessions later, on a day the whole AI-power complex fell on data-center financing-cost concerns, with GE Vernova and Bloom Energy each off more than 11% and Vertiv down 8.7%, after Lam Research's chief financial officer Doug Bettinger said supply-chain constraints had held back the December-quarter guide. Over the same thirty days onsemi rose 3.1% and Monolithic Power fell 7.9%, so the de-rating concentrated in the names whose content is dated furthest out.

Navitas has the roadmap and no gross profit

Navitas, a 190-person designer of gallium-nitride and silicon-carbide power devices, is the same timing problem without the licensing bridge. Second-quarter revenue was $10.53m, down 27.3% year on year as it exits mobile and consumer chargers, with negative gross profit in each of the first two quarters of 2026. It guided the third quarter to $13.5m, plus or minus $0.5m, on more than 50% year-on-year growth in high-power markets, and expects AI data centers plus grid and energy infrastructure to exceed one-third of sales by the end of 2026, implying roughly $4m to $5m a quarter. Management describes the 800-volt shift as three staged inflections running from the second half of 2026 through "late 2027 and early 2028". It closed the $232.8m acquisition of Claros on 6 October to reach the final conversion stage inside the processor, and at October's GTC Berlin conference Infineon announced its own gallium-nitride collaboration with NVIDIA for 800-volt racks covering the same 800-volt-to-6-volt sockets.

How much of the fall the numbers explain

For Navitas, most of it. The company trades at 46.5x forward revenue with no gross profit to value, and consensus puts fiscal 2026 revenue at $47.69m, only 4.7% above 2025, with the step up to $72.71m deferred to 2027. The calendar in the de-rating is the company's own.

For Vicor, little of it. The quarter beat, the backlog grew, the margin expanded, and the fall arrived two days later with the sector. What the results do not settle is the quality of a third of the revenue: a royalty stream whose rate rises with the progress of a trade case, against a fourth-quarter total the company itself guides lower. At 45.9x consensus 2026 earnings and 28.7x 2027, the shares have come down from roughly 68x forward in May, when they traded between $273 and $304.

The difference between the two is not the roadmap, which they share, but what each can charge before it arrives. Vicor can bill a patent portfolio at 68% gross margin while its fab waits for 2027 equipment. Navitas reports third-quarter results on 3 November, and has to sell parts.

Clearway Energy, which owns roughly 5,000 net megawatts of American wind and solar alongside a gas-fired fleet, restructured the offtake contracts on its Texas wind farms in June at higher prices, with terms now running past 2040. In January it had signed nearly 1.2 GW of twenty-year power purchase agreements with Google across three states, more than $2.4bn of investment with first power due in 2027. The shares are down about a fifth over six months.

The reading on offer for the renewable yield vehicles is that the long-dated contracted revenue which is their whole earnings engine is quietly decaying: agreements recontracted below the expiring ones, tax credits phasing out, interest absorbing the distribution. Clearway's disclosures record the reverse, and so does the market for new power contracts. What moved both it and Brookfield Renewable, the owner of hydro, wind and solar across the Americas that sells most of its output under long-term contracts, was the price of money: the Federal Reserve raised its target a quarter point on 16 September, its first increase since 2023, the 30-year Treasury printed 5.42% on 20 October against 4.60% a year earlier, and utilities fell 6% in a month to 17% below their 52-week high.

The guidance cut was weather

Clearway's June quarter grew revenue 22.7% to $481m, with operating income up 36.5% to $116m and gross margin at 69.0% against 66.6% a year earlier. In August it cut the midpoint of 2026 cash available for distribution about 8%, to $430-470m from $470-510m, on below-normal wind and solar output, and left the 2027 target of $2.70 a share and the 2030 range of $2.90-3.10 where they were.

"While we are lowering our 2026 financial guidance due to factors outlined in our mid-July operational preview, our team is focused on maintaining our trademark operational excellence to uphold our historic track record of meeting our financial targets," chief executive Craig Cornelius said in the 5 August results release.

The policy channel has gone the same way. Clearway has safe-harbored tax-credit eligibility on 14.7 GW of wind and solar, with 9 GW and more qualified for service in 2031-2034, and has sourced equipment for its entire development program through 2027 and substantially all of 2028 — all of it predating the 4 July start-construction cliff that accelerated the credit's termination. Transfer discounts on those credits remain narrow, $0.92 to $0.945 on the dollar for production credits. And new contracts are getting dearer: the average North American solar agreement rose 4.9% in the third quarter to $64.23 per MWh, with wind up 13.8% on the year.

The constraint is the balance sheet, and Clearway named it. The company needs $0.5-1.0bn of external equity through 2029 to fund a $3bn capital program and has raised about $50m, which has paused third-party purchases. "When it's not where we want it to be or valuations for third-party acquisitions, we think, are not really reflective of their intrinsic value, then we step out," Cornelius told investors on the 5 August call. At $31.43 the shares sit at 0.70 times book — below the accounting value of the plants — on 13.8 times trailing EV/EBITDA and a 10.4% trailing free-cash-flow yield, or about 11.6 times the 2027 cash target, a figure derived from the reaffirmed guidance rather than one the company prints.

Brookfield's record came partly out of the sale ledger

"We delivered another record quarter, generating FFO of $421 million, or $0.62 per unit, up 13% or 11% per unit year-over-year," Connor Teskey said on Brookfield Renewable's 31 July call. Roughly $175m of that sat in the hydro segment as other income from developed-asset and non-core sales; the partnership closed two-thirds of a sale of about 2,100 MW to the Northview platform in the quarter. Strip the gains and per-unit funds from operations fall near $0.36 against a declared distribution of $0.392, coverage of about 0.92 times on that derived basis. Interest expense rose 5.4% to $658m, more than the whole quarter's funds from operations. Reported revenue fell 1.8%.

At $28.91 the $1.568 annualized distribution yields 5.42%, the same as the 30-year Treasury on 20 October, leaving nothing for equity risk. Clearway's $1.90 annualized dividend yields 6.05%, roughly 63 basis points of spread, down from about 90 in late September.

Demand is not the dispute. Brookfield's framework with Microsoft commits it to 10.5 GW delivered between 2026 and 2030, and Teskey argued in October that supply will trail demand for compute and power by three to ten times through the decade: "There's a lot of growth in the market and that can overwhelm slightly higher cost of capital."

The year's loss belongs to a share class

The twelve-month decline attributed to this corner of the market is largely an artifact of the corporate simplification. BEPC, the corporate twin exchangeable one-for-one into Brookfield Renewable units, fell 25.0% over the year as its premium collapsed from 38% to 1.6%; the partnership units themselves rose 2.1% and Clearway 1.4%. Holders of the corporate shares approved the transaction on 14 October with more than 99.7% in favor; the unitholder meeting was adjourned to 29 October.

So the selloff is mostly explained, and explained the same way for both: a long-dated, indexed stream of cash discounted at a rate that has moved almost a full point in a year, which is why NextEra fell 17.6% and Ormat 21.8% over the same six months. What the rate does not explain is why the two deserve the same multiple. Brookfield's de-rating has a business counterpart — a payout with no spread over government paper and coverage that depends on continuing to sell plants — while Clearway's contract meter is lengthening and its credits are locked.

Clearway's fuel costs nothing and its competitors' fuel is getting scarcer. What it cannot buy cheaply, at 0.70 times the value of its own turbines, is its own equity.

Fair Isaac's competitor in mortgage credit scoring now has a published price and a published expiry date. Equifax said on 8 October that it will keep selling VantageScore 4.0 to mortgage lenders at $1 through the end of 2028, a commitment it claims could save the industry about $1bn, and TransUnion has fixed its standalone price at 99 cents through December 2028, saying it will not charge separately for that score when a customer also buys a FICO score. Both bureaus distribute Fair Isaac's score for a living.

The company's fall since September has been read as the market pricing one lost customer and a weaker origination forecast. The bureaus' commitments describe a slower problem. Price, not volume, is what has been growing the Scores segment at Fair Isaac, which sells credit scores and decision-management software from Bozeman, Montana, and the price of the alternative is now set for three more years.

Nearly all of the mortgage gain was the fee

In the June quarter Scores revenue rose 41% to $458.9m, with the business-to-business half up 49%, which the company attributed "primarily" to a higher mortgage origination scores unit price. Mortgage origination revenue rose 97%; mortgage score volumes grew low single digits. Mortgage is 62% of Scores revenue.

The royalty shows the arithmetic. Fair Isaac took its wholesale mortgage fee from $4.95 to $10.00 a score for 2026, after moving it from $0.60 to $4.95 over the prior five years. A roughly 102% price step produced a 97% revenue gain, so units and mix contributed close to nothing. Group revenue grew 25.7% to $674.2m with operating margin at 53.8%, against 48.9% a year earlier.

The rival is already inside agency underwriting

Fannie Mae's Lender Letter LL-2026-06 and Freddie Mac's Bulletin 2026-H, both dated 9 September, made VantageScore 4.0 available to every approved seller without prior written approval, and 37% of conventional applications created in Fannie Mae's Desktop Underwriter in September included a VantageScore 4.0 score. Equifax says nearly 2,000 mortgage lenders and resellers now access the model, that pulls rose 230% between April and August, and that more than 165 lenders use it exclusively at the $1 price for certain loan types. Rocket Mortgage said on 28 September that it will default to VantageScore 4.0 this quarter on all eligible loans, including loans delivered to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, citing average closing savings of $1,600 for borrowers who benefited. Senator Josh Hawley asked the Federal Trade Commission on 23 March to investigate the price increases.

"We're not crazy about lenders choice as a policy. We think it's bad policy because it encourages gaming," chief executive William Lansing said on Fair Isaac's 29 July earnings call, adding that volumes were not falling, "which suggests that they're pulling both scores." Rocket's decision came two months later.

What the company is doing about the dollars

Fair Isaac's answer is its Mortgage Direct License Program, which lets tri-merge resellers calculate and distribute FICO scores without going through the bureaus, at $4.95 a score plus a $33 fee on funded loans, or $10 flat. Six resellers are under contract — Xactus, Cotality, Ascend Companies, CIC Credit, MeridianLink and Credit Interlink — together more than half the reseller market by origination volume. "This change eliminates unnecessary mark-ups on the FICO Score," Lansing said announcing the program on 1 October 2025. FICO Score 10T becomes available to FHA-approved lenders on 1 January 2027. On 1 October the company also committed to eliminating about 15% of its positions, with roughly $27.0m of pre-tax charges in the fiscal fourth quarter, which protects margin on the revenue that arrives and does nothing for the score count.

A 10% fall inside a software rebound

The shares closed at $823.27 on 22 October, 23.7x trailing earnings against roughly 62x in January, and 16.8x the fiscal-2027 consensus of $48.92 a share drawn from 13 analysts. Fair Isaac fell 10% over the month to that date while most of the application-software names beside it rose, in a rebound that carried the S&P 500 software and services index to its highest level since November 2025. Vertex, which sells the indirect-tax engine that sits inside customers' SAP and Oracle systems, gained 29.9% and trades at 4.84x forward gross profit against 3.70x in late September, with revenue growth decelerating to 10.5%. BlackLine, whose software automates the corporate accounting close, rose 23.4% to 3.45x forward gross profit from 2.85x, on annual recurring revenue of $719m growing 6%. Neither has reported since early August.

The fee, not the quarter, is what is being priced

Fair Isaac's reported figures account for none of its decline, and the consensus estimate is where the argument actually sits: $48.92 assumes the $10 score survives into a year in which the Mortgage Bankers Association's 11 October forecast puts origination counts at 5.0 million, 5.6% below 2026. The signed direct-license channel and 10T's entry into FHA lending are genuine defences of that fee. Against them sit a rival locked near a dollar until 2028, a large lender switching its default, and a third of agency applications already carrying the other score. If lenders keep pulling both, the price holds and the forward earnings arrive. If they stop, the price step unwinds into a shrinking loan count, and the Software segment is too small to cover the gap: Platform recurring revenue grew 62% to $413m, but total Software revenue rose 2% to $215m.

Fair Isaac reports fiscal fourth-quarter and full-year results after the close on 4 November, a quarter billed before Rocket's switch took effect. The question those results cannot answer is what a score is worth to a lender who already has a cheaper one sitting in the same credit file.

American Airlines told investors on 22 October that it now expects to lose between $1.00 and $1.60 a share for the full year, abandoning a range that had allowed for a profit, in the same hour it reported the fastest quarterly revenue growth its chief executive says he has seen. Revenue rose 19.3% to $16.3bn. Operating income fell to $115m, a margin of 0.7%, and the quarter produced a $269m net loss.

The fall across this shelf since mid-September has been read as the main cabin finally giving way: domestic seats outrunning demand, coach fares capped, premium cabins and co-brand credit-card payments left holding the earnings up. The four quarters reported between 9 and 22 October test that belief and break it. Coach is the strong half, fares are the best on record, and the seats are being taken out rather than added.

The coach cabin outperformed the front of the plane

Southwest posted unit revenue up 21.6% year on year, a company record and above its own 17.5%-19.5% guide, achieved while lapping the introduction of bag fees. United's total unit revenue rose 15.9%, and inside it non-premium revenue rose 18.4% on a 0.5% capacity increase, for coach unit revenue up 17.8% against premium unit revenue of 14.1%. Basic Economy revenue rose 16% and loyalty 17%. Delta's system unit revenue rose 15.4%, with main-cabin unit revenue up 17%, a third consecutive quarter of improvement. American's rose 14.5%, domestic 13.6%, after a deliberate choice: "American traditionally has been a leader in load factor," chief commercial officer Nathaniel Pieper said on 22 October, and the company "really identified yield improvement as a top priority."

Nor is anyone flooding the domestic market. About 3% of domestic seats were pulled from the 25 November to 31 December schedule after American, United and Southwest announced cuts around the 16 September Morgan Stanley conference. American said then that demand remained strong at higher fares and the problem was the economics of low-margin flying. Delta guides fourth-quarter revenue up roughly 20% with main-cabin seats down year on year.

Every carrier that reported posted double-digit unit revenue growth. Every one of them also paid close to twice as much for a gallon of fuel.

One input took the whole gain

The Strait of Hormuz has been closed to commercial shipping since 28 February, and because refined-product flows were disrupted along with crude, jet fuel crack spreads now run $85 to $95 a barrel against roughly $15-20 before. Spot jet fuel averaged $3.72 a gallon in August, 84% above a year earlier. Fourth-quarter assumptions sit close to double last year's: American roughly $4.70 a gallon, United $4.80, Southwest $4.55, Alaska $4.65 to $4.85 against an original $2.50 plan.

"Higher fuel added $1.9 billion to our costs in the third quarter. Nearly 70% more than we paid during the same period last year," American chief executive Robert Isom said on 22 October. Delta's Ed Bastian, on 9 October: "we expect to deliver pretax profits of roughly $4.5 billion fairly close to last year's profitability. Even with our fuel bill expected to increase by 60%, or $6 billion."

Recapture and the balance sheet set the dispersion

Delta kept revenue growth accelerating all year, to 21.1% in the September quarter, and still watched operating income fall 13.7%; it has a refinery worth about $0.40 a gallon of offset, diversified revenue at 61% of the total and American Express remuneration above $9bn tracking to $10bn, and trades at 15.7x trailing earnings against 14.6x forward. United recovered 72% of a fuel increase of $1.8bn and was the only one of the three to grow operating profit, up 3.7%; its forward earnings multiple of 13.5x sits above its 9.3x trailing precisely because consensus 2026 earnings are 28% lower, and 2027 consensus puts it on 8.3x. Southwest expanded operating margin 4.8 points to 5.4% despite a fuel headwind of $820m, on 19.9x trailing earnings against 14.9x forward, though part of its reported passenger-line gain came from a credit-card reclassification.

American has no forward earnings multiple to quote, against a consensus 2026 loss of $1.03 a share, negative book value and $36bn of debt behind an equity value of $8.3bn. On enterprise value to earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization it is the dearest of the three at 13.6x, against United's 7.1x.

Alaska is the counter-example to the mix argument. Premium revenue is 36% of its mix, up three points, premium traffic rose 24.5% and loyalty cash 21% to $721m. It still lost $147m in the quarter and guides to a full-year adjusted loss of $5.00 to $5.50, and the shares sit 41% below their 52-week high.

The prices argue the same way

Over 30 days United fell 11.5% and American 7.4%; Delta, at 15.4% below its high, is the least damaged. The shelf's headline decline is mostly JetBlue, down 41.8% in a month to $2.65. The five-session slide from 12 to 19 October hit all four majors within three points of each other and reversed into the prints: every one of them rose on 22 October.

So the revenue line explains none of the de-rating and the earnings line explains most of it. Fares are not the variable in doubt; the variable is how much of a doubled fuel bill each carrier can pass through before its balance sheet decides the outcome. "To get there in 2027, we probably also need at least 1 of the 3 following things to happen: Lower fuel, lower industry capacity, or a continued willingness for the industry to trade load factor for yield," United's Scott Kirby said on 21 October. Two of those three are inside the industry's control, and both are already in motion: American says 2027 capacity will decelerate considerably, and Kirby says he will cut loss-making flights with no hesitation.

The third is not. Four ships transited the Strait of Hormuz on 18 October, against roughly 85 a day before the closure, and no one has put a date on its reopening. Every carrier's path back to profit is written against a forward curve that assumes one.

Applied Digital, which designs and operates single-tenant data centers for artificial-intelligence and high-performance computing workloads in North America, booked $341.9m of revenue in the quarter ended 31 August. The largest line inside it was fit-out construction performed for tenants, at $183.5m, against $65.8m of base rent and $37.8m of legacy bitcoin hosting. That mix pulled group gross margin to 15.3% from 42.5% two quarters earlier, even as adjusted earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization reached $64.4m against $0.5m a year earlier.

The shares have been read as a verdict on whether AI capacity is still worth what tenants agreed to pay for it. The company's own October disclosures describe the opposite happening on both sides of its economics: the leases repriced up and the debt repriced down. What moved against the equity was the price of the money that funds the gap between pouring concrete and sending an invoice.

The coupon fell 225 basis points while the stock fell by a third

"Our first Polaris Forge 1 related notes placed last year carry a 9.25% coupon. This tranche priced at 7%," chief financial officer Mohammad Saidal L. Mohmand told investors on 7 October, on $1.59bn of senior secured notes due 2031. The improvement was attributed to the investment-grade credit quality of CoreWeave, the company's anchor tenant. On the same call, chairman and chief executive Wesley Cummins said the company was targeting roughly 250MW of expansion leases by year-end at "materially higher" pricing, meaning 15%-plus rate increases and potentially longer duration, and expects to place more than 600MW into service over the next twelve months against 250MW in the prior twelve.

The contracted book is not speculative. As of 31 May the company disclosed 1,410MW of contracted critical IT load and approximately $36.2bn of take-or-pay leases with CoreWeave and investment-grade hyperscalers on initial 15-year terms.

What the equity is funding is construction

Trailing free cash flow runs at -77.2% of market value. That is the mechanism. A company placing 600MW into service spends the capital first and collects rent afterward, so its equity is a claim on the terms of the next financing rather than on the last quarter's leases. Those terms changed. The 10-year Treasury yield rose 87 basis points in the third quarter and touched its highest level since 2002 on 7 October, and the window for new data-center paper narrowed with it: listings for a Blackstone-backed AirTrunk vehicle, an Nvidia-backed developer, Firmus, and a SoftBank-backed developer, SB Energy, were pulled or paused, and a Korean data-center listing fell 30% on debut on 20 October.

The valuation has finally done work. With no earnings to anchor against, the usable measure is price to gross profit, at 33.4x trailing against 60.7x in late August; trailing price to sales is 6.8x and 6.1x forward, down from 14.3x in August and 28.7x in early May. The thin margin on fit-out revenue is why the sales multiple flatters this business and the gross-profit multiple does not.

Core Scientific's version of the problem is one payer

Core Scientific, which runs data-center facilities for digital-asset mining and colocation hosting for third parties, grew second-quarter revenue 108.8% to $164.2m with gross margin recovering to 42.7% from 6.4%, and still posted a $1.155bn net loss and negative book value. Its trailing free-cash-flow yield is -22.8%. CoreWeave supplied about 77% of first-half revenue under a 12-year colocation contract worth roughly $850m of average annualized revenue, take-or-pay, with no unilateral termination right. Durable terms, one counterparty, whose debt has traded toward distressed levels. Shareholders rejected CoreWeave's acquisition of the company in 2025, leaving it standalone. Its $13.20 close on 22 October was its low for the year, against a high of $29.16.

The split sorts by who needs outside capital

Over three months Applied Digital fell 36.5% and Core Scientific 44.1%, while Equinix fell 7.4% and Digital Realty 4.2%. Both incumbent landlords raised 2026 guidance in late July and de-rated anyway: Equinix, whose interconnection-rich colocation campuses connect carriers, clouds and enterprises, to roughly 22.2x the midpoint of guided adjusted funds from operations of $42.69–$43.29, against the 25–30x that franchise has historically commanded; Digital Realty, the wholesale and hyperscale landlord, to roughly 20.9x guided core funds from operations of $8.15–$8.20 against a 22–25x band. They fund from investment-grade balance sheets. The two operators fund from the market that just tightened.

So almost none of the four-to-tenfold difference in the fall is leasing. Applied Digital's rates are going up by its own account, and Digital Realty's second-quarter renewals repriced cash rents 25.4% higher. What repriced is the cost of capital spent before a tenant's first payment, plus, at Core Scientific, the standing of a single payer. The remaining question is political: investors now see the 3 November midterms as a channel for data-center backlash, through hearings and investigations that analysts doubt could materially stall construction.

"Put simply, our view is that every new restriction elsewhere makes what we already own harder to replicate and more valuable," Cummins said on 7 October. He is describing a moat that only pays once the megawatts are energized, and the next 600 of them have to be financed before a single invoice goes out.

Insulet took type-2 diabetics past 40% of its new American customers in the June quarter, and then cut the growth forecast for its home market because too many of them stopped using the product within three months. The population it could not hold on to is the same one paying Dexcom's sensor business, and on October 14 Dexcom's CONNECT trial was published in The New England Journal of Medicine — the evidence file it has handed to Medicare in support of covering those patients.

The halving of Insulet's shares over the past year has been read as proof that the type-2 opportunity both companies sell is smaller than advertised. The company's own numbers say something narrower. Omnipod revenue rose 24.6% to $795.9m, the global customer base grew 23%, gross margin held at 70.2% against 69.7% a year earlier, and net income more than quadrupled to $95.0m.

What broke was onboarding: one company, one country, one segment.

The first 90 days did the damage

Insulet, which sells the tubeless Omnipod insulin pump through the pharmacy channel, reduced full-year US Omnipod growth guidance to 17-19% from 20-22% on weaker type-2 retention and utilisation, concentrated in the first 90 days after a patient is set up. "As CEO, I take accountability for our reduced U.S. outlook," chief executive Ashley McEvoy told investors on the second-quarter call, adding that "once Type 2 customers remain on Omnipod for the first 90 days, retention rates stabilize, a trend that we also see in our Type 1 population." The fixes are operational: more customer-care staff for insurance navigation, sales compensation re-weighted to reward retention in the first 45 days, a refined sampling programme.

Group guidance moved to roughly $3.25-3.30bn from $3.28-3.33bn, under a 1% cut, with international Omnipod growth raised to 30-32% from 26-28%. At $141.90 the shares carry 21.8x this year's consensus earnings of $6.51; the same consensus against last October's price implies about 50x. Two things sit inside that compression which the income statement does not show: a March 12 voluntary Medical Device Correction and a second on May 26 covering Omnipod 5, DASH and Eros lots, with a securities class action alleging the first affected more pods than disclosed, and the collapse of drug-delivery revenue to $5.8m, down 78%.

Dexcom gets paid for the patient Insulet lost

For a sensor, the type-2 push is clean volume. CONNECT randomised 283 adults at 22 US primary-care sites over 26 weeks and cut A1C by 1.6 percentage points from a baseline of 8.8%, 0.9 points better than routine care. Dexcom's own survey presented at the EASD meeting finds 58% of type-2 patients taking GLP-1 drugs wearing a sensor against 27% of type-2 patients not on insulin, with use nearly quadrupling among non-insulin patients after a GLP-1 starts. Weight-loss drugs recruit sensor customers; they do not have to recruit pump customers.

The gate is reimbursement. Medicare today covers a sensor for any diabetic using insulin or with documented problematic hypoglycaemia; patients on oral drugs alone do not automatically qualify. Chief executive Jake Leach said Dexcom "submitted the CONNECT data for publication and provided the evidence to CMS in support of the non-insulin coverage expansion", a segment he said has "delivered some of the largest growth of our business over the last couple years".

That is a 2027 argument. What Dexcom has delivered now is margin: non-GAAP gross margin of 64.1% against 60.1% and operating income at 25.1% of revenue against 19.2%, on revenue up 13% to $1.308bn — down from 21.6% growth in the September 2025 quarter. Full-year revenue is guided to 11-13% after 15.6% in 2025, and the shares at 30.0x this year's consensus are about 17% dearer on that basis than a year ago.

Tandem's volume is its own installed base

Tandem Diabetes Care, which sells the tubed t:slim X2 and Tandem Mobi pumps, shipped more than 33,000 pumps in the quarter for 10% growth and lifted gross margin to 57%, up 460 basis points, narrowing operating loss to -$13.8m on revenue growth of 5.8%. But new pump starts were flat year over year and a few hundred units below plan, and management needs 12-13% growth in new starts in the second half to reach $1.065-1.085bn for the year. Its answer to Omnipod's form factor, a tubeless Mobi, is at the FDA and not expected in the US until 2027. At 1.95x trailing gross profit against Dexcom's 9.76x, it is priced as the loser of a pump race it also supplies: Tandem added Dexcom G7 15-day compatibility this quarter, so its lost starts are partly revenue-neutral for Dexcom.

Why the same patient splits the two businesses

The results account for almost none of Insulet's de-rating. A 23.5% revenue quarter with margins intact does not produce a 56% fall over twelve months; the recalls, the litigation and doubt about how much of the insulin-using type-2 market actually converts are doing that work, and the guidance cut is the smallest part of it. Dexcom's 25% twelve-month gain came with decelerating revenue and a wider multiple, which makes it a position on the coverage decision more than on current volume. Tandem has the margin recovery and neither the growth nor the device.

The mechanism underneath is the product. A sensor is a consumable any persuaded, reimbursed patient can wear, so prescribing patterns in diabetes drugs feed it directly. A pump asks the patient to keep wearing a device through a difficult first month, so the same expansion imports churn. From January 2028 CMS moves sensors and pumps to monthly rental payment with supplies bundled into the rate, a rule diabetes organisations are lobbying Congress to delay. Dexcom reports on October 29 and Tandem on November 5.

Insulet's quarter, due November 4, is the first that can show whether the type-2 patients it signed up over the summer are still wearing pods. The sensor only has to be prescribed; the pump has to be worn, and that is the harder sale to make twice.
