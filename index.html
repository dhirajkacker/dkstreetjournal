The three listed companies that sell the Ethernet fabric AI clusters run on all reported accelerating business, and all three have been marked down hard in a month. Only Cisco paid for its AI orders in margin.

The markdown rests on a specific argument: that the switch port is becoming a pass-through, that the cluster dollar gets spent but the scarcity rent stays upstream with the company that makes the silicon inside the box. The upstream half of that is not in dispute. Broadcom has guided fiscal 2026 AI semiconductor revenue to $58bn, up 186%, with AI networking revenue up more than 2.5 times year on year in its third quarter — an AI networking silicon business of roughly $19bn on those figures, larger than Arista's entire revenue guide for the year. The question is what happens downstream of it, and the three income statements disagree.

Cisco's order book came out of product margin

Cisco, which sells campus and data-center switching, routing, security and the Webex collaboration suite, booked $9.3bn of hyperscaler AI infrastructure orders in fiscal 2026, roughly 4.5 times the prior year and above the $5bn it first targeted. It recognized about $4bn of that as revenue. The cost is disclosed in its annual filing: non-GAAP product gross margin of 64.8% in the July quarter, down 270 basis points year on year on higher hardware mix and memory costs, with hyperscaler AI demand rising from under 2% of total revenue to approximately 6%. Full-year gross margin slipped to 64.5% from 64.9% while revenue growth accelerated to 11.8%. Management projects $7.5bn of hyperscaler AI revenue in fiscal 2027, which is the same mix, larger.

Arista raised the floor of its margin range

Arista, which sells high-speed Ethernet switches running its own EOS operating system to internet companies, telecom carriers and banks, did the opposite. September-quarter revenue grew 58.5% to $3.658bn, the fastest in its history as a public company, and operating income grew 67.9% — faster than revenue. Reported gross margin of 63.6% was about a point below last year but up from the 61.9% trough two quarters earlier, and management tightened the long-run range to 62-64% from 60-65%, lifting the floor. The fourth revenue raise of the year took fiscal 2026 to $13.3bn, with AI fabric revenue guided to double to roughly $7bn in 2027.

"These type of migrations are multiyear. And so we're very confident not only in our '27 guide, but a promising '28 ahead as well," chief executive Jayshree Ullal told investors on November 3.

What Arista spends to stay out of commodity territory is visible: research and development reached $408.5m in the quarter, 11.2% of revenue, against $278.1m in the prior one, on accelerator partnerships and liquid cooling. Two soft spots survive the quarter. Deferred revenue was flat sequentially at $6.8bn despite the growth, and Meta and Microsoft together are more than 40% of sales — the two customers best placed to have the same box built white-box, and the two whose accelerators Arista is co-engineering against.

Nearly half of HPE's profit is the networking it bought

Hewlett Packard Enterprise sits between the two, and the profit is not coming from the cluster. Networking revenue rose 74.9% to $2.9bn in the July quarter at a 22.0% operating margin, flat year on year — roughly $638m of segment profit on those disclosures, about 46% of group operating income on about a quarter of group revenue. Campus and branch gear is still half the segment. The AI-adjacent lines grow faster off a smaller base: data-center networking of $382m, routing of $788m, the latter up 270% behind a disclosed Oracle deployment of HPE Juniper switching and routing. Chief executive Antonio Neri said on September 2 that HPE is "at the core becoming a networking company." Meanwhile AI systems orders of $2.4bn lifted backlog to a record $6.8bn, and management guided the Cloud and AI operating margin down sequentially on AI systems mix and pricing.

The drawdown and the disclosures line up once

The three fell an average of 25% over the thirty sessions to November 6, against a twelve-month gain of 47% across the shelf they sit on. HPE's fall came in two legs, and the larger one predates the November repricing of data-center buildouts: it dropped 25.0% between October 9 and October 16, after Barclays downgraded it alongside Dell and NetApp, arguing that enterprise AI is about 5% of IT budgets with a quarter of that going to hardware, so recent hardware revenue reflects memory-cost inflation. That argument bears directly on HPE's reported gross margin expansion of nearly twelve points. Extreme Networks, which sells campus and branch Wi-Fi and switching to hospitals and schools, fell 1% over the same stretch.

None of the reported lines explains the move. Cisco's de-rating has a cause anyone can read in the filing, and the compression is modest — 7.9x forward gross profit against 9.1x trailing — because the dilution is real and continuing. Arista's does not: 39.8x forward earnings against roughly 41x in July, with the shares a touch below their May 1 level while the full-year guide went from about $10bn to $13.3bn, so a third of the multiple came off on revisions alone. HPE at 9.5x forward earnings, 11.7x trailing EV/EBITDA and a 7.0% free-cash-flow yield now sits below the bottom of a 10-13x forward anchor, with the caveat that the forward figure rests on a fiscal 2027 it has not reported against.

Cisco has not updated its AI order book since August 12. It reports again on November 12, and the open question is whether the margin it gave up was an entry fee or the new price of selling a port into an AI cluster.

The memory bill is bigger than the software gain

Contract prices for conventional memory chips are set to rise 10% to 15% in the fourth quarter as suppliers shift capacity toward server modules for artificial-intelligence workloads. That is the whole argument in two lines, and the arithmetic does not yet favor the better line. Software and services were $52m of the quarter; appliances were roughly $250m. On those bases the hardware hit costs about $5.5m of quarterly gross profit against roughly $1.8m gained on software — figures derived from the reported mix. The recurring dollar earns about 16.5 points more margin and is growing faster, 19% against 14%, but hardware is still about four-fifths of the invoice.

ASML's September quarter carried an admission capital-equipment vendors rarely make: it has sold more machines than it intends to build. The Dutch company, the only producer of extreme-ultraviolet lithography scanners, moved its order-book disclosure onto the balance sheet after discontinuing quarterly net bookings, and the substitute line moved violently — customer advances for tools due more than a year out rose €5.73bn in the quarter, reaching €9.30bn. "Given the dynamics of this quarter, we now anticipate that our current planned levels of output in 2027 and 2028 will not fully meet the demand of our customers," chief financial officer Roger J. Dassen told investors on October 14.

The thirteen listed suppliers of wafer-fabrication equipment fell between 20.6% and 48.9% in the month to 6 November. The drawdown has been read as a memory shortage failing to convert into tool orders: bits added by node migration and conversion inside cleanrooms that are already built consume far less equipment than new wafer starts, so record memory capital budgets need not become record tool sales. The last reported quarter at four of these vendors locates the constraint somewhere else entirely — in the cleanrooms, not in the willingness to order.

The prepayments arrive before the floor space does

ASML's record net sales of €11.7bn grew 55.2% year on year, with gross margin widening to 57.4% from 51.6% and operating income more than doubling. Of €8.4bn in system sales, €4.1bn was EUV. Low-NA EUV output is going to roughly 65 systems this year from 44 in 2025, with 30% growth planned for 2027 in both low-NA EUV and immersion deep-ultraviolet, and management is investigating going beyond that for 2028 because demand exceeds the plan. Prices on the most critical high-volume products rise from 2027, described as one-time rather than annual; existing backlog is not repriced.

Installed-base management revenue — upgrades and service paid on tools already running — was €3.3bn in the quarter and is guided to grow about 50% this year against roughly 36% for the group. That is the one piece of the bearish mechanism that holds: at ASML, the running fleet is earning faster than the new one. At 34.6x forward earnings, the shares are below the 40-48x forward recorded in May.

Lam capped its own December guide on parts

Lam Research, which sells the etch and deposition tools that cut and fill the high-aspect-ratio structures in 3D NAND and high-bandwidth memory stacks, grew revenue 54.4% to $8.219bn on a record 52.3% gross margin, its fifth consecutive revenue record. Deferred revenue rose $227m to $2.66bn. "Our preliminary 2027 WFE outlook is for more than $200 billion of spending, with percentage growth led by DRAM, followed by leading-edge foundry logic and NAND," chief financial officer Doug Bettinger said on October 21, a figure KLA independently corroborated alongside record quarterly revenue of $4.14bn and its first operating margin above 46%.

The shares fell as much as 11% on the day, because Bettinger said the $8.6bn December guide would have been higher but for near-term supply-chain constraints, with deferred shipments pushed into the first quarter of 2027. Lam also told the market that limited cleanroom space and parts shortages would stop the industry meeting demand into 2027. Service revenue of $2.5bn grew 40%, slower than the group. China rose to 35% of revenue from 26%. The forward earnings multiple is 20.8x against roughly 38x in May, while reported growth more than doubled.

What 100,000 wafer starts costs in implanters

Axcelis Technologies, which builds ion implanters, supplies the arithmetic the thesis needs. Roughly $150m to $200m of implant equipment serves 100,000 wafer starts a month, and its top three memory customers are adding more than 1m starts a month over four to five years. Memory is about 20% of system sales, nearly all DRAM and HBM. September-quarter bookings of $222m gave a 1.5x book-to-bill, the best since the fourth quarter of 2023, and chief executive Russell J. Low named the gate on November 5: "we are watching very, very carefully the availability of kind of greenfield clean room space because clearly, you know, today, our customers are often 4 wall constraints." Megafabs open "starting in 2027, but they are they are gonna be in phases." The stock trades at 24.2x forward earnings, with consensus 2026 earnings per share of $4.11 down 8.7%.

That capacity is being built. Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron are expected to spend $146bn in 2027, 3.4 times the $43bn of 2024, and SK Hynix has pulled completion of its Yongin cluster forward by twelve years. J.P. Morgan Global Research estimates DRAM prices will have risen more than 400% from the start of 2024 to the end of 2026.

Onto's leg is not reportable yet

Onto Innovation, which sells macro defect inspection and 3D bump metrology into advanced packaging, is the unverifiable case. Its June quarter grew 35.3% with operating income up 97.1%, off a 2025 that rose 1.8%. It raised its 2026 advanced-packaging growth outlook to roughly 80% from 50%, crossed $1bn of backlog, and closed a volume purchase agreement estimated above $240m with a leading HBM manufacturer. But 64.7x trailing earnings compresses to 21.4x forward only on consensus 2027 revenue of $1.44bn, up 43%, and the company guided the September quarter to $380-400m and reports between 9 and 12 November.

What the quarters explain, and what they do not

Nothing in these results explains the ordering of the selling. Onto, the purest HBM metrology name, fell 42.2%; Ultra Clean's gas-panel assembly fell 41.7%; ASML, the most lithography- and memory-levered name of all, fell least at 20.6%. The sequence tracks market value and liquidity. Roughly two-thirds of the damage was done in October, after Applied Materials declined to put a number on 2027 equipment spending at its 13 October investor day — Applied's own July quarter grew 24.8% and it now trades at 17.7x forward against 28.5x trailing — and the rest in the week to 6 November, when concern over tighter oversight of data-center construction hit the whole AI complex, a move Bank of America characterized as multiple compression rather than earnings cuts. Applied sits 54.0% below its 52-week high and Lam 53.7%. Of the names here, only ASML still holds the uptrend all thirteen held in May.

The bear case is not wholly empty. China's share of world equipment spending fell from 42.3% to 36.5% in 2025 with billings down about 11%, so the pull-forward is normalising at industry level even as it failed to appear sequentially at Lam or Axcelis. And a cycle whose gating item is construction phasing is a cycle whose revenue can slip a quarter at a time without any customer changing its mind.

Which is the live question for 2027, and it is a question about concrete and electrical capacity rather than about bits. The three memory makers' output for all of next year is already reported sold out. The tools to make more have been ordered and, at ASML, partly paid for. What nobody in this chain controls is when the doors open.

Appian raised its full-year guidance on 5 November after revenue growth accelerated in the September quarter, and its shares rose in a week when every large enterprise-software name around it fell. Between 4 and 6 November, ServiceNow lost 7.2%, Pegasystems 8.3%, Salesforce 5.9% and Snowflake 5.1%. Appian gained 3.7%.

The year-long mark-down of enterprise software rests on one mechanism: if an AI-assisted employee does the work of three, a per-seat invoice falls by up to two-thirds. Bain puts the cost of that repricing at roughly $2trn of market value over twelve months. Appian, which sells a low-code platform that generates workflows, forms, data models and interfaces instead of hand-coded applications to banks, insurers, life-sciences companies and government agencies, was read as an early casualty: a process-automation layer with no infrastructure underneath it, facing ServiceNow, Pegasystems and Microsoft's Power Platform. Over the year to 6 November, Pegasystems fell 45.0% and ServiceNow 40.6%. Appian rose 34.5%.

The upsell that reads as retention

Appian's AI money arrives as a price increase. Most of the quarter's uplift came from customers upgrading to the Advanced Tier, which carries a 25–35% premium to Standard, with AI usage up more than 50% from the prior quarter and consumption overages growing but not yet material. The effect lands in the company's disclosed retention meter: cloud net annual-recurring-revenue expansion hit 120%, against 115% in the prior quarter and 112% a year earlier, which management called the strongest in more than five years. Cloud subscription revenue of $136m grew 20%; total subscriptions, including self-managed software, grew 28% to $188m.

"Appian has claimed a distinctive and valuable place in the AI stack," chief executive Matthew Calkins told investors on 5 November. "We connect the world's most important technology to the world's most important work."

Four verticals — financial services, insurance, life sciences and the public sector — supply 80% of recurring revenue, and more than 70% of the business comes from customers spending over $1m a year, chief financial officer Serge Tanjga told the William Blair growth conference. That concentration is why one US Cabinet agency's seven-figure procurement deal moves a quarter.

Margin widened; the cash went the other way

GAAP operating income rose 62.4% to $21.3m, a 9.1% margin, and adjusted EBITDA of $43m beat a $30–33m guide. Full-year EBITDA guidance went to $116–119m, up about 53%, and revenue guidance to $873–877m. The base is low: 2025 was Appian's first positive GAAP operating year, at $0.6m on $726.9m of revenue.

Three things complicate the inflection. GAAP gross margin fell 2.9 points to 72.9%, while management described gross margin as 74% on a non-GAAP basis, flat year on year. Professional-services gross margin fell to 24% from 31% as implementations scale. And operating cash flow was minus $15m against positive $19m a year earlier, with cash and investments down to $89m from $187m at the end of 2025; $83m of that decline is cumulative buybacks under a $100m authorization. Management called the cash timing-driven and said October collections were among the strongest months on record.

The January retrial is not the engine

The shares have held an uptrend since 12 August, six sessions after the 6 August second-quarter beat-and-raise. The retrial of Appian's vacated $2.036bn trade-secrets award against Pegasystems opens on 11 January 2027, after the Supreme Court of Virginia threw out the 2022 verdict in January 2026; on 16 October the court let Appian present a reasonable-royalty damages theory it was barred from using at the original trial. Appian then rose 27.2% in a week. But Pegasystems rose 5.4% in the same window and ServiceNow 12.0%, so the likelier reading is a broad software rally that Appian led at roughly twice the move. Short interest of about 3.79m shares, some 5.3% of shares outstanding, is too small to be the cause.

Dearer than it was, cheaper than ServiceNow

Price-to-gross-profit has gone from 3.07x in early May to 6.05x, against gross profit that grew 20.8%: the price has roughly doubled relative to the profit it buys. On forward earnings, Appian's 47.7x is nearly twice ServiceNow's 25.8x.

Measured against forward gross profit the order inverts. Appian's 6.00x sits below ServiceNow's 8.92x, where 20.5% revenue growth came with GAAP operating income down 32.7%, and far below Snowflake's 22.97x, where 35.1% growth comes with a $263.0m quarterly operating loss.

Pegasystems, at 3.91x forward gross profit, is the cheapest of the four, and 3.1% revenue growth with 7% annual contract value growth says why. "Our technology is differentiated and very relevant to the times we are in. However, candidly, our execution this year has not kept up," founder-chief executive Alan Trefler said on 21 October. Salesforce, at 4.92x, grew 10.8%.

What the quarter can and cannot pay for

The operating results account for the direction and the timing of the advance, and for Appian being the one name in its neighborhood that gained in the week its peers gave up ground. They do not account for the full doubling of the valuation, because the acceleration is one quarter old. In the June quarter revenue growth slowed to 19.1%, GAAP gross margin compressed to 71.2%, and unsatisfied performance obligations of $625.3m grew 13%, slower than revenue. The raised fourth-quarter revenue guide of $233–237m implies about 16% growth, partly on a 2–3 point currency headwind, and consensus has 10.8% revenue growth in 2027 while earnings per share still rise 31%. What is being bought at 47.7x forward earnings is margin, and the sell side said so: William Blair upgraded to Outperform while DA Davidson lifted its target to $60 from $42 and TD Cowen to $58 from $50, both keeping their ratings.

Neither company's balance sheet carries the claim against Pegasystems, and nothing changes hands before a verdict. Until a jury meets in January, the invoice has to do the work on its own — and Tanjga, asked whether this quarter's expansion rate becomes the new baseline, declined to guide to the metric at all.

Rocket Lab reports third-quarter results on 9 November with the vehicle that justified its valuation no longer part of next year. The company has pushed the first flight of Neutron, its eight-tonne medium-lift rocket, into 2027 because of problems with the carbon-fiber parts used in its fuel tanks. What has to carry the price in the meantime is hardware: Space Systems billed $189.5m of a record $234.1m in June-quarter revenue, against $44.6m at Launch Services.

Record order books across listed space have been read as contracted revenue awaiting delivery, and Rocket Lab's is the largest among these names, reaching $2.36bn in June, up 137% year on year and weighted 58.5% to Space Systems. The past month has tested that reading. Buyers of these shares are paying for gross profit already banked rather than for signed backlog: on forward gross profit, five listed space names span roughly 10 times at York Space Systems to 89 times at Rocket Lab.

The leverage is real; the catalyst moved

Rocket Lab, the only listed company that both reaches orbit and builds the spacecraft and components that fly there, is converting. June-quarter gross profit rose 82.3%, faster than revenue, and gross margin reached 36.1% against 32.1% a year earlier. Annual gross margin has climbed from 9.0% in 2022 to 34.4% in 2025. The credentials behind that are specific: a prime contract worth $816m for 18 missile-warning satellites under the Space Development Agency's third tranche, plus solar cells, reaction wheels and star trackers sold into other primes' missions.

It still lost $57.5m at the operating line in the quarter and consumes cash, with a trailing free cash flow yield of -1.17%. Consensus does not have net income turning positive until 2028. Launch, the activity the equity story is named after, recognized about $7.4m per flight in the June quarter, a figure derived from segment revenue and the six Electrons flown; fifteen Electron missions flew in 2026 through 26 September. At 88.9 times forward gross profit the shares cost five times Planet Labs' 17.6 times.

Neutron was the asset meant to close that distance.

Planet turned cash-positive as its multiple fell by two-thirds

Planet Labs, which sells daily global imagery from a constellation of more than 150 satellites to governments, agriculture, finance and insurance customers, moved the opposite way. Revenue growth accelerated for four consecutive quarters to 58.1% in the July quarter, on gross margin of 56.6%, and the operating margin narrowed to -11.6% from -24.5%. The company posted adjusted EBITDA of $13.9m and guided fiscal 2027 revenue to $430m-$441m. Trailing sales now cost 11.3x, against 39.4x in early May.

The limit on that case is the order book. Backlog was $814.9m, up only 11% year on year, far behind revenue; one-time satellite handovers, led by the first Pelican delivered to the Swedish Armed Forces, were 12% of July-quarter revenue against 1% a year earlier, and existing customers expanded spend by 9%. The recurring imagery meter is growing more slowly than the headline.

Firefly's largest order is payable partly in a customer's shares

Firefly Aerospace, which sells the Alpha small launcher, the Blue Ghost lunar lander and defense software, rose 36.8% on 6 November on about five times its prior ten-session average volume after announcing a licensing and supply agreement with the satellite-to-phone operator AST SpaceMobile. It pays $800m upfront, half cash and half the buyer's Class A stock, $250m for 36 engines and $250m of engineering services over three years. The buyer's shares are down 50.5% over twelve months, so roughly $400m of that upfront moves with a counterparty's price, and the rocket the engines are for, Blacklift, is not targeted to fly until late 2028.

Chief executive Jason Kim called it a "bold transformation deal," saying "the engine is the hardest, highest-stakes decision they make." Firefly's own June quarter scaled revenue to $117.7m while gross margin compressed to 20.3% from 25.7%, and its trailing free cash flow yield of -9.39% is the worst of this group. It reports on 10 November, with analysts near $103m after a national-security customer pulled a hardware order into the prior quarter.

Karman Holdings, which builds payload fairings and solid-propulsion units for munitions and missile-defense programs, gained 42.1% the same session on a funded quarter: revenue of $202m, up 66%, with full-year guidance raised to $755m-$760m.

Redwire converted; York's buyer changed how it buys

Coinbase receives all of the reserve income on USDC held on its own platform and half the residual on USDC circulating elsewhere, under terms renewed through 2029. On its own October call Coinbase said it holds about 30% of USDC on-platform and captures roughly half the economics, describing the arrangement as "coopetition". Circle's distribution bill rises with its distributors' success.

That leg nonetheless held up. Net reserve margin rose 70 basis points year on year to 37.8%, slipping 80 points sequentially on the Hyperliquid arrangement, under which Coinbase passes about 90% of the reserve income onward. Management said the fourth quarter absorbs a full quarter of both Hyperliquid and a renewed Binance deal, and guided margins lower.

Combine the two disclosures and Circle kept roughly 1.3 cents a year on each dollar of USDC, against about 1.5 cents a year earlier, a figure derived from the reported rate and margin rather than disclosed. The rate did that, not the distribution terms.

The $182m that made the quarter a beat

Headline revenue of $864.9m was up 16.9% and earnings of $0.84 a share doubled the $0.42 consensus, but $182m of the $207m in other revenue was a one-time presale of tokens for Arc, Circle's new chain. Full-year other revenue is guided to $310–330m, of which core products account for $130–150m: about 4–5% of the $3.01bn consensus top line. Circle also disclosed a 5% workforce reduction, roughly 55 positions and $9.1m of restructuring expense, in a footnote rather than in its commentary. JP Morgan's Mike Colonnese cut his target to $100 from $104, Needham's John Todaro to $103 from $134.

Chief executive Jeremy Allaire was explicit that he is not defending revenue per dollar moved. "When this achieves scale, it's going to have enormous velocity and the unit costs are going to be much, much lower," he told investors on 4 November. "We are not shooting to let's go repeat the margin structure of the existing payments industry." On-chain USDC transaction volume grew 49% to $13.9 trillion in the quarter.

At $67.88 Circle trades at 58.5x this year's consensus earnings of $1.16, above its 36.5x trailing: consensus has reported earnings falling. On trailing gross profit it is at 24.2x against Coinbase's 8.1x, three times its closest listed peer after a 74% fall from its high.

Figure's take rate stayed inside the band it guided

Figure's third-quarter marketplace volume was $5.12bn, up 107% year on year and 20% sequentially, at the high end of its own $4.8–5.2bn range, before any contribution from Kiavi. The per-unit economics did not narrow as volume compounded: net take rate was 3.6% in the second quarter, inside the 3.5–4.0% guided range. What changed is the mix. Ecosystem and technology fees became the largest contributor to adjusted net revenue for the first time, and partner-branded gain-on-sale revenue grew 35.0% while partner-branded volume rose 151%, because partners are moving onto fee-based terms. Gross margin has fallen from 93.5% to 80.1% over four quarters on that shift, with revenue close to double.

The caveats are in the balance sheet. Figure bought Kiavi on 1 September for about $590m net of cash, funded with $600m of 8.5% senior notes, and trailing free cash flow runs deeply negative because loans held for sale consume cash before they are sold. Figure trades at 19.8x this year's consensus earnings against 25.8x trailing, with consensus earnings growth of 74%, and at 15.5x trailing enterprise value to EBITDA, the measure that now has to carry the notes.

Coin prices and a dead bill, not unit economics

Circle's results account for part of its mark-down: the core line is shrinking on a float that stopped growing, and the beat came from a token sale that does not recur. For Figure they account for none of it. The shares closed at an all-time low of $20.90 on 5 November, 69% below their high, with volume accelerating and results a week away.

The better explanation is the asset class and the statute book. Bitcoin fell about 6% to $75,700 and Ether to $2,247 on 5 November, triggering $1.09bn of liquidations, and the CLARITY Act, the market-structure bill meant to re-rate this layer, failed a Senate procedural vote 49 to 50 on 15 September. Over 30 days Circle fell 15.5% and Figure 18.8%, both less than Coinbase's 24.8%, and the levered coin-treasury vehicles in the same segment averaged 21.7%: the operating businesses were marked down at least as hard as the holding companies.

The rate input, meanwhile, has turned. Circle's 3.5% reserve rate is last year's cuts washing through a short book. Figure reports on 12 November, with Kiavi's first partial month and the interest on the notes that bought it both landing in the same statement. The line to read is the one Circle cannot set for itself.

The two companies that do most of the world's outsourced chip packaging and test reported the best quarters in their histories inside four days in late October. By the first week of November their shares had lost roughly a third of their value, and the explanation sits in election returns rather than in either income statement.

The shelf was supposed to work the other way. The standing belief about outsourced assembly and test is that the rent from the AI buildout's tightest physical step stays upstream with Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing's in-house chip-on-wafer-on-substrate lines, while the listed packagers get paid out of smartphone and automotive volumes at thin, mainstream rates. Both September quarters say otherwise. Gross profit per revenue dollar rose at each, the advanced work is now a disclosed dollar line, and the compression the theory predicts shows up in only one place: contract manufacturing.

The repricing arrived from the ballot box

The 3 November midterms produced a Democratic sweep of Congress, and voters in at least ten states backed restrictions on data-center expansion over electricity costs and land use. Two days later the Nasdaq Composite fell a record 1,421.52 points, or 5.52%, with the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index down more than 12% as the buildout became a political variable rather than an engineering one.

Almost half the month's decline landed in that single session: ASE Technology's American depositary receipts fell 20.1% and Amkor 14.5%, and both rebounded the next day. Over the 30 days to 6 November Amkor was down 38.5% and ASE 32.4%. The cleanest check that this was not a verdict on packaging is United Microelectronics, a mature-node foundry with roughly $300m of AI-related revenue in 2026 and no advanced-packaging socket at all: it fell 40.3% over the same stretch, more than either packager.

Amkor's phone book shrank as its computing book set a record

Amkor, which sells wafer bumping, flip-chip, fan-out and wirebond assembly and test to fabless designers and foundries from Tempe, Arizona, grew third-quarter revenue just 0.18% year-on-year to $1,990.6m, after 25.6% growth the quarter before. Gross profit rose 27.8% on that flat top line. Margin reached 18.26% against 14.32% a year earlier, a fourth consecutive quarterly expansion and above the 13.99% the company earned for all of 2025.

The mix did it. Computing — data center, infrastructure, storage — hit a record 26% of net sales against 19% a year earlier, while Communications fell to 37% from 51%. Advanced products, meaning flip chip, memory and wafer-level processing with related test, were $1,632m of the total, about 82%. The stalled top line is the phone side: AI data centers are absorbing memory, and IDC now expects global smartphone shipments to fall 16.7% in 2026.

"Customers are not only planning further ahead, but are also making long-term commitments and providing advance payments tied to future capacity requirements," chief executive Kevin Engel told investors on 26 October. Roughly $3bn of such prepayments have arrived since 2025, including $1.5bn from Nvidia for the Peoria, Arizona campus, which carries a finalized $407m CHIPS and Science Act award and begins volume production in early 2028. It earns nothing before then, and consensus has 2027 earnings per share at $2.76 against $2.53 this year — 9.1% growth, the depreciation eating the leverage. Amkor costs 13.1x trailing earnings and 6.58x trailing gross profit, against 10.8x gross profit on 21 August.

ASE put a number on AI packaging and raised it mid-call

ASE, the largest outsourced assembly-and-test provider, posted record consolidated revenue of TWD 244.03bn, up 44.8%, with growth accelerating through the year from 17.4% in the first quarter. Its assembly-and-test arm earned a 30.6% gross margin, eight points better than a year earlier, and delivered 91% of group operating profit on 62% of revenue. The electronic manufacturing services arm is where input costs bite: its margin slipped 0.6 points to 8.3% on component pass-throughs, for 9% of group operating profit.

"The so-called structural margin ceiling has now become the base for us," chief financial officer Joseph Tung said on 29 October. Management lifted 2026 leading-edge advanced packaging revenue guidance to over $4bn from $3.7bn during the call — roughly 15% of expected group sales — disclosed $300m of full-process chip-on-wafer-on-substrate work this year ramping above $1bn in 2027, and guided co-packaged optics past $0.5bn next year. Quotes for advanced packaging went up more than 20% in July.

The bill is cash. Capital spending was raised to $11.5bn, about 43% of expected revenue; net debt stands at 66% of equity and trailing free cash flow is negative 3.1% of market value. At 30.0x trailing earnings, down from roughly 42x in August, the cheap reading rests on 2027 consensus, which implies 60.5% net income growth.

Why the overflow reaches them at all

Neither company won these accounts from TSMC, which still earns 66.41% gross margin and says customers prefer its own flow. The packagers' share is set by TSMC's shortfall: non-TSMC players are estimated to add capacity equivalent to 110,000 advanced-packaging wafers a month by end-2028, and TSMC has transferred work to ASE and Amkor rather than see orders reach Samsung. Tool orders corroborate the ramp: ASMPT booked a record $1.13bn in the September quarter, up 138%, at a 1.69x book-to-bill, including thermocompression bonders ordered by packaging partners.

So the quarters account for none of the fall, and the two names were never one tape. Amkor is down 6.6% over twelve months and has been in a downtrend since 10 August, because 37% of its sales sit in a phone market losing units; ASE is up 105.8% and held an uptrend until 4 November, because its assembly arm is sold out and priced up. What the election changed is the terminal demand both are building into, and that is a question about permits and power, not about packaging yields.

Amkor's Arizona phase one completes late 2027 on a two-phase program management now sizes at $12bn. Every ton of that concrete was poured on the assumption that someone will be allowed to plug the finished chips in.

Chief financial officer Kenneth Gianella said demand is "continuing to see that demand rotate more towards REV8." Against Hesai and RoboSense, which supply geometric lidar data and which held, in Hesai's case, 44% of China's long-range advanced driver-assistance lidar market in June 2026, Ouster's durable edge is regulatory: compliance with the National Defense Authorization Act and Buy-American rules bars its Chinese rivals from federally funded US projects. Third-quarter wins ran through the Utah, Washington, Florida and Ontario departments of transportation, plus defense customers Forterra, Seneca and GOQ.

Margin upside is going into tooling

Management holds a long-term gross-margin target of 35-40%, says it is currently beating that band, and is spending the difference on retooled lines and precision automation under a Benchmark partnership meant to support more than 100,000 lidar units of annual capacity by the end of 2027. No volume commitment underwrites that. The adjusted EBITDA loss narrowed about 60% year on year to roughly -$3m, the reported operating loss was $19.3m, and cash stood at $421m including about $191m raised in a July equity offering. The shares carry 22.8x trailing price-to-gross-profit, more than twice Cognex's 10.2x.

Cognex earned more per dollar of sales and got marked down

Cognex, which sells barcode readers and integrated vision systems to logistics, electronics and automotive manufacturers, ran the reverse arithmetic. Revenue of $313.4m rose 13.2% to a company record, gross margin expanded 312 basis points to 70.8%, gross profit rose 18.4% and operating income rose 74.5% to $100.8m. Full-year guidance went up, to revenue of $1.14-1.16bn and adjusted earnings of $1.68-1.72 a share. "At the midpoint of our guidance, we expect approximately 93% flow-through of incremental revenue to adjusted EBITDA, compared with roughly 70% in 2025," chief financial officer Dennis Fehr said on November 5.

The stock fell 18.3% across the two sessions into that print. Contemporaneous coverage put the company-specific reaction at about -6% on a slight revenue miss; the remainder landed in the November 5 washout, when the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 12.2%, the session the paper has already described as repricing data-center demand expectations. Trailing price-to-gross-profit is now 10.2x against 13.4x in May, on a materially larger gross-profit base. The cautions are internal: automotive was flat year-to-date on European weakness, with Europe revenue down 15%, and management guided 2027 incremental flow-through down to about 60%.

A unit count has stopped predicting a gross-profit line

One quarter of results explains almost none of what happened to these shares, and the per-sensor mechanism that was supposed to bind them explains less. Ouster's dollars per unit fell because the marginal sensor is a camera rather than a lidar; Cognex does not disclose units at all and grew gross profit by price, mix and margin execution. Ceva, which licenses digital-signal-processor and connectivity designs and is paid a royalty on every device shipped, shows the same squeeze one layer down: licensee shipments of 567m units rose 16% in the June quarter while royalty revenue rose 1%, about 1.9 cents a device from 2.2 cents. It trades at 7.6x trailing gross profit, the cheapest of the three, and reports on November 9.

What survives is three separate businesses billing three different ways, all of them paid out of factory, logistics, infrastructure and defense budgets that have nothing to do with hyperscaler capital spending.

Ouster is building for a lidar volume nobody has promised it: lines sized for more than 100,000 units a year by the end of 2027. Until the orders arrive, the sensor it ships most often is the one that earns it least.

Between late October and the first week of November the four listed operators of outsourced customer contact and back-office work all reported, and the quarters pulled them apart. ExlService raised full-year guidance for the second time this year. Concentrix, which staffs and runs customer-contact operations plus front- and back-office automation for consumer-electronics, e-commerce, health-insurance and banking clients, guided its November quarter down. All four shares are higher over the month, each by double digits.

For two years these companies have been read as the clearest casualties of agentic artificial intelligence, and the month's advance was read as the turn: deflection arriving as revenue through contracts that charge for a resolved problem instead of an agent's hour. The filings carry the first half of that and almost none of the second. Work is moving between providers. The unit they bill has barely changed.

A seat at ExlService bills near twice a contact seat

ExlService, which sells insurance and healthcare analytics, claims processing, premium audit and payment accuracy, grew September-quarter revenue 19.7% to $634.0m, a third consecutive acceleration, with gross profit up 18.2%. Organic headcount rose 11% against 17% organic revenue growth; employee cost falling as a share of revenue is the mechanism management names. Divide the quarter's revenue by its 65,000 staff and annualize and the result is roughly $39,000 per employee, a derived figure, against about $21,600 on the same arithmetic at Concentrix and its payroll of 455,000.

"Data and AI-led revenue grew 36% on a reported basis and 31% organically in the third quarter, now representing almost 2/3 of EXL's total revenue," chairman and chief executive Rohit Kapoor said on the 28 October call. Digital Operations revenue fell 1.7% by design as AI was embedded in work the company already ran. Guidance went to $2.430bn-$2.440bn with adjusted earnings of $2.34-$2.36 a share. It is also the dearest name here, at 7.24x trailing gross profit and 6.95x forward, against 3.52x for Genpact, the nearest listed peer.

Concentrix's shrinkage was ordered by two clients

Concentrix's revenue declined 1.2%, or 0.5% in constant currency, after 5.4% growth in the February quarter. The November quarter is guided to $2.41bn-$2.46bn, a 3% to 5% constant-currency fall, because two hyperscaler clients are completing a reduction in support coverage, pulled forward from 2027. Beneath that sits a roughly three-point annual headwind from clients moving seats to cheaper countries. Gross margin held at 34.6%, and non-GAAP operating margin widened 30 basis points to 12.6%.

The mix story is real and small. "This quarter, we crossed the mark that 50% of our revenue now comes from businesses that we generated in the last 3 years," chief executive Christopher Caldwell told investors on 29 September, putting that pool's growth near 30% for the year. The software meter inside it, iX Suite licensing, carries about $120m of annual recurring revenue, some 1.2% of the $9.85bn consensus expects for fiscal 2026. Retention across the whole client base is 98% and the top 25 clients average more than 16 years, so capability is not the problem; price is. The company also acquired CastleHill Managed Risk Solutions for governance, third-party risk and AI-governance work.

Its shares rose 15.7% in the 22 October session, the largest single day on the list, with no earnings in the window; a dividend raise and that acquisition were the only datable news, so positioning is the likelier reading. At 0.57x trailing gross profit, a 31% trailing free-cash-flow yield and 2.74x forward earnings, the advance is closing a valuation gap. Trailing price-to-earnings is unusable: a $1.05bn non-cash goodwill impairment, triggered by the share price's own trading range, followed a $1.48bn write-down a year earlier.

TaskUs grew the line that costs most to deliver

TaskUs, which provides digital customer support, trust-and-safety moderation and data labelling for model training, grew revenue 5.4% to a record $314.7m while gross profit fell 9.2% to $108.9m, costing it 553 basis points of margin. "The current growth that we are seeing in AI services seems to be heavy on the onshore side, which comes at lower margins than what we make in our offshore business," chief financial officer Rishabh Khemka said on 4 November. AI services reached $75.0m, up 27.7%; trust and safety fell 15.8%. The largest client dropped to 17% of revenue from 27% on its own automation. Management's answer to the cost line is conventional price increases. The stock trades at 6.8x forward earnings.

ibex was handed the calls it automated

ibex, which runs customer care and technical support across voice, chat and SMS for banking, healthtech, retail and travel clients, is the clean case. "By having all contacts immediately reaching an AI agent, we were able to reduce abandoned calls by more than 60% without the need for our client to add additional headcount," chief executive Robert Dechant said on 5 November. The fibre provider in question then awarded ibex its traditional call-centre work. Revenue was a record $175.2m, up 16%, adjusted EBITDA rose 29%, and guidance went to $720m-$735m. Onshore revenue, the high-margin end, grew 37% and reached 29% of the mix. The shares cost 13.4x forward earnings against 13.5x trailing, which is no growth premium at all.

Only Genpact has moved off the headcount meter

Two of the four earned their re-ratings with results. ExlService because analytics and data engineering bill at nearly twice a contact seat, ibex because a six-week deployment bought it an incumbent's volume. TaskUs's advance rests on a guidance raise and a cheap multiple while the income statement deteriorates. Concentrix's rests on cash: $218m of adjusted free cash flow in the quarter and $550m or more of annual gross debt paydown targeted through 2027, against a top line consensus has flat to 2029.

The pricing migration the whole thesis needs has one listed example on the shelf's edge. "Non-FTE revenue exceeded 55% of total revenue in Q3, and our revenue growth continues to decouple from headcount," Genpact chief executive BK Kalra said on 5 November, with gross margin at 38.0% against 36.4% a year earlier. Everest Group describes the new billing units, per journey, per bot-hour, customer-experience-as-a-service, as beginning to emerge. Emerging is the right word: ExlService puts its own payment-integrity shift a couple of years out and calls the timing "unclear and unsettled".

So in this corner of services AI has become a question of which provider holds the account when volumes move, while the hour remains the thing counted on the invoice. The companies whose revenue per employee has pulled away are the ones that found something else to sell, and the next three quarters will show whether anyone can charge for a resolved problem at scale.
