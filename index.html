Dell's own figure for what a rack earns

Dell, the largest AI server original equipment manufacturer by volume, reported record Infrastructure Solutions Group revenue of $31.8bn in its July quarter, with AI-optimized servers at $16.4bn. Segment operating income of $4.781bn is a 15.0% margin, against 8.8% a year earlier, on a record $95bn AI backlog.

The dilution is real but smaller than assumed. Chief financial officer David Kennedy has said the AI server business runs in line with its target of a mid-single-digit operating margin, below the group rate, so each incremental AI dollar lowers the blend while raising the absolute profit. Dell reports its October quarter after the close today, guided to about $49bn of revenue and roughly $19bn of AI servers, with segment margin expected up slightly more than a point year on year and memory a headwind all year. At 12.9x forward earnings, the stock is well under the roughly 18x it carried in May.

Super Micro's strain is in the working capital

Super Micro, the San Jose builder of liquid-cooled rack-scale systems, tripled September-quarter revenue to $19.9bn and lifted gross margin to 12.74% from 9.31%, though sequentially it fell from 17.5%. The pressure point is elsewhere. One data-center customer was 44% of revenue, and operations consumed $434m of cash.

"The largest single driver was customer prepayment," chief financial officer Mark Long told investors on 3 November. "Deferred revenue increased approximately $3.1 billion in the quarter. Accounts receivable grew $3.6 billion, and inventory grew $2.5 billion to support the revenue ahead of us, and prepayments funded most of that build." A trailing free-cash-flow yield of minus 40% and a 3.8x forward multiple are describing the same disclosed facts.

Who actually pays for the memory

On 23 November the Financial Times reported that Dell, HPE, Lenovo and Supermicro are demanding price increases of 25% to 45% on AI equipment months after contracts were agreed, and none would confirm whether their contracts contained clauses against price volatility. Buyers are paying: memory-heavy processor nodes have doubled or tripled while TrendForce has server DRAM staying undersupplied with contract prices up 10-15% in the fourth quarter. HPE last year amended its quoting terms so a booked order can be repriced for component cost between quote and shipment.

The shares did not wait for the disclosures. Dell is down 23.0% over three months and HPE 10.1%, with Super Micro off 33.4% and down 22.9% over twelve months, having never participated in the gain its two peers made. The worst of it was one market-wide session on 20 November, when US equities lost roughly $3.2tn and Nvidia stood at about half its 6 October value; three sessions later Nvidia rose about 31% and carried Dell up 22.5%, HPE 17.5% and Super Micro 14.6% in a day.

So the derating and the disclosures point opposite ways for two of the three, and the reason is who holds the scarce good. Dell and HPE allocate constrained accelerators and memory into order books they can reprice, and both expanded segment margin while AI revenue grew fastest. Super Micro sells speed of deployment rather than contract leverage, and its 44% customer funds the inventory through prepayments, which is why the same selling reads as a priced risk there instead of a mistake.

HPE says the parts stay scarce into the second half of 2028. That is a long run of quarters in which these companies quote a price before they know what the memory will cost, and each one re-asks the question this week's reporting answered for only one of them.

The only US-listed pure play among the world's three makers of high-bandwidth memory has already agreed what it will be paid for most of next calendar year's output. "We have completed agreements for the vast majority of our calendar 2027 HBM bit supply with significant price increases year-over-year, narrowing the gross margin gap with conventional DRAM," chairman and chief executive Sanjay Mehrotra told investors in prepared remarks on 30 September. The fragility in Micron's earnings is real. It sits where a spot-price markdown cannot reach it.

What 26 take-or-pay deals fix, and for how long

Micron has signed 26 multi-year Strategic Customer Agreements carrying take-or-pay volume commitments that cover more than 35% of revenue through 2030, three-quarters of that revenue subject to defined pricing frameworks, alongside $32bn of customer financial commitments mostly in cash deposits. The deposits are visible in the accounts: $12.75bn received during fiscal 2026, with noncurrent customer contract liabilities at $12.90bn against $142m a year earlier. A second high-bandwidth memory assembly and test line in Taiwan opened in mid-October with all of its planned 2027 output contracted. Samsung, one of the other two suppliers, has also sold out its 2027 supply, so none of the three enters the year chasing spot volume.

Against that, fiscal fourth-quarter revenue rose 379% to $54.229bn, gross margin reached 86.8% from 44.7% a year earlier, and the first quarter is guided to a record $61.5bn plus or minus $1.5bn.

The shares went the other way. Micron closed at $703.74 on 23 November, 19.8% below a month earlier and about 42% under its 25 June record. The selling was not memory's: Nvidia fell 21.4% over the same month, Seagate 17.1% and Western Digital 12.3%. Micron dropped 13.89% on 20 November, the day Barron's called memory stocks "agonizingly cheap" at 3.8 times forward earnings, then gained 19.04% on 23 November when Nvidia's board reinstated Jensen Huang and the Nasdaq 100 rose more than 7%.

Where the contract book stops helping

Fiscal 2026 DRAM revenue rose 252%, of which roughly 180 percentage points came from average selling prices and only a mid-20% increase from bits shipped. Price, not volume, did the work, and price is the component a turn removes. Days of inventory closed the quarter at 129, nine days higher than the prior quarter. Capital spending was $27bn in fiscal 2026 and is tracking above $50bn in fiscal 2027; "We plan to increase our CAPEX in fiscal 2027 versus prior plans," the prepared remarks said.

Contracts cut in both directions. TrendForce expects conventional DRAM contract prices to rise 10-15% quarter on quarter in the fourth quarter of 2026, down from roughly 80% in the second and 40% in the third, attributing part of the deceleration to price ceilings in long-term agreements taking effect. Consensus already models the fade, decaying earnings growth from 138% in fiscal 2027 to 20% and then 3%. Micron trades at 4.0x forward earnings against 9.4x trailing, and its trailing price-to-gross-profit of 7.5x compares with 26.3x in May 2026. One unresolved item is labor: the union at Taoyuan voted 1,994 to 14 on 7 October to authorize a strike over profit-sharing, with no date set.

Nothing in that prices off DRAM. Third-quarter revenue was a record $20.648m, up 46.9%, split $16.9m of product and $3.7m of licensing, royalty and engineering revenue, the latter recognized largely against a $40m subcontract with a US defense contractor. The memory squeeze is a tailwind there. So the results explain almost none of Micron's markdown for the next several quarters and quite a lot of what comes after them: the contracted book insulates fiscal 2027 revenue and simultaneously caps what rising spot prices can add, while bit growth, inventory days and a near-doubling of capital spending are the variables left exposed. Everspin's decline belongs to a provision, a penalty and a dilution filing, and its claim on the AI memory tier arrives later still, with Aggarwal placing samples of the persistent-memory standard at the end of 2028 and early revenue in 2029.

The deposits sitting on Micron's balance sheet are returned only if customers buy what they committed to buy. If they do not, the cash stays and becomes revenue. It is the first point in this cycle where the weak case pays Micron something either way.

dLocal, the Montevideo-based processor that collects payments for streaming, ride-hailing, advertising and software merchants across emerging markets, reported on 12 November that the thing investors had been watching it lose finally stopped moving. Its net take rate, gross profit divided by payment volume, came in at 0.72% of volume, essentially unchanged from the prior quarter after a year of steady compression from 0.99%. Gross profit was a record $132.0m, up 27.9%, and gross margin widened sequentially to 33.2% from 31.8%.

The shares have fallen 16% over three months. The reading attached to that decline across the payments shelf has been disintermediation: stablecoin settlement and agent-driven checkout routing volume around the companies that process cards. dLocal's own disclosure says the opposite is happening to it. Crypto and stablecoins were described as a growing vertical with merchant wins in remittances and repatriation, and protocol-driven agentic commerce volume was characterized as still low. The company launched a product in April called Stablecoin Full, letting merchants accept and settle in stablecoins across more than 44 markets through one interface, which makes the rail a cheaper cost line and a sales argument.

The take rate held for the first time in a year

Volume was $18.3bn in the quarter, up 77% year over year and the eighth consecutive quarter above 50% growth. Revenue grew 40.9% to $398.0m, far slower, because 62% of that volume was local-to-local flows that settle inside one country and earn a thinner spread than cross-border collection. Payouts were another 25%. On the revenue line the implied take is about 2.18% of volume against roughly 2.72% a year earlier, a derived figure; the mix shift is most of it, with Brazil hit by a merchant shift toward the PIX instant-payment scheme and Boleto vouchers plus processing-cost catch-ups, and Mexico by slower volume at concentrated, lower-priced merchants.

Costs went the other way. Operating profit of $76.2m grew 37%, faster than gross profit, reaching 58% of gross profit as headcount fell 3% and operating expenses 11% sequentially. "The investment cycle is behind us, and now it's more about continued expansion of margins going forward even as we pursue growth," chief executive Pedro Arnt said on 12 November. Chief financial officer Guillermo Perez told investors the same day that the company expects "to grow gross profit above OpEx on an annual basis, but quarterly figures could be a bit more lumpy."

What trimmed the outlook was currency

dLocal reiterated volume growth toward the upper end of 60-70% and put full-year gross profit growth at around the midpoint of its 25-30% range, with operating profit at the lower end of 27.5-32.5%. The cause named was foreign exchange in Brazil, Mexico and Argentina, estimated at a six-percentage-point swing on year-on-year gross profit growth. Cash followed the margin: $99m of adjusted free cash flow in the quarter, a 146% conversion from net income, and $34m of stock repurchased.

The heaviest session was 17 November, when the shares fell 6.6% on roughly six times the prior week's average volume and crossed below their 200-day average. That day, General Atlantic's holding vehicle sold 12,813,179 Class A shares in open-market trades at an average $12.90, about $165m, its second large disposal in three months after 9.84m shares in August. The surrounding week was broadly risk-off, with the S&P 500 tracker down 8.6%.

Three businesses, three points in the flow

The companies grouped with dLocal do not earn money the same way, which is why their records diverge. Marqeta, an issuer-processor that runs card programs for platforms, keeps about 9.7 cents of gross profit per hundred dollars of processed volume, down from roughly 11.7 a year earlier; its gross profit growth has decelerated four quarters running to 6.6%, yet the stock is up 257% over twelve months and trades at 17.5x trailing earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, the dearest of the three on cash earnings. Shift4, the Allentown acquirer behind SkyTab point-of-sale terminals, reported $1,319m of gross revenue of which $671m was network fees it never retains, processed $61bn at a blended spread of 64 basis points that widened rather than compressed, and is down 41.5% over a year against 3.7x net leverage and a growth framework cut to mid-to-high single digits on restaurant and European weakness.

None of the three management teams quantified alternative rails as a threat. In the same November week the hardest-hit payment names were the stablecoin beneficiaries, Circle down 23.6% and Coinbase 20.1%, while Visa fell 5.6% and Mastercard 5.4%. On-chain AI-agent payment value has shrunk to about $1.59m a month from roughly $29m.

So the operating results account for none of dLocal's three-month decline and the guidance trim accounts for part of it: a six-point currency haircut on gross profit growth is real money, and management said compression has not bottomed even as the declines slow. The rest is a seller and a bad tape. At 15.5x forward earnings and 6.8x forward gross profit against 7.3x trailing, with a 14.0% trailing free-cash-flow yield, the market is paying for roughly 7% of gross profit growth from a business that just grew it 28% and holds 42 licences across 26 markets.

There is no scheduled disclosure from dLocal until its next annual results, estimated for late March. Whether General Atlantic has finished is not in the filings.

Veeva Systems sells cloud software to one industry — drug and device makers — and it sells almost all of it twelve months at a time. That matters because the markdown running through enterprise software this autumn has been sorted by contract structure: a committed multi-year book reads as protection from the possibility that artificial intelligence shrinks what buyers need. Veeva has no such book, and the market has not spared it. The durability here is regulatory switching cost rather than contract duration, which is a harder thing to observe and a harder thing to date.

The twelve-month order book

The filing shows where the money sits. Veeva recognized $992m of revenue out of beginning deferred-revenue balances in the six months to 31 July. What exists instead is a sunset with a date on it. Legacy Veeva CRM, which runs on Salesforce, reaches end of support on 31 December 2029, forcing life-sciences customers to move to Veeva's own Vault CRM or to a rival inside a roughly four-year window. As of 26 August, more than 180 customers were live on Vault CRM, including five of the top 20 biopharmas, with 12 of the top 20 committed; that is up from 140 live at the end of fiscal 2026.

The migration is contested. Salesforce's Life Sciences Cloud, co-developed with IQVIA, won Novartis and Pfizer over the stretch in which Veeva added Merck, Roche and Novo Nordisk commitments.

The business the filings describe

"Q2 was another strong quarter, delivering results ahead of our guidance," chief executive Peter Gassner said in prepared remarks on 26 August. "It was our best CRM quarter ever. We also are accelerating rapidly in AI overall, and especially with Veeva Falcon."

The encroachment is real and recent. On 20 October, OpenAI acquired Orchestra Bio, whose agents let biopharma teams delegate study monitoring and the tracking of development timelines, folding it into its Rosalind life-sciences suite — the clinical-operations work Vault's R&D applications serve.

Veeva fell 5.2% on 19 November, the eighth consecutive down session, a stretch worth about $7.1bn of market value. Ormat Technologies, the Reno geothermal group that owns and operates power plants, sells turbines to third-party developers and runs battery storage, and Fervo Energy, the Houston enhanced-geothermal developer that drills horizontal wells to make hot rock produce, both reported third-quarter results in the first half of November. Between the two sets of numbers, the price of a firm American geothermal megawatt-hour is now legible at both ends of the industry — from the already-drilled fleet and from the construction site — and the two are selling at close to the same rate.

The data-center bid for clean power that runs all day has been read as a re-rating event for geothermal. The quarters say the clearing price is already set near $100 and is sitting in one company's revenue line, while at the other it is a schedule. That changes what each stock is actually exposed to: at Ormat, the mix of segments; at Fervo, a financing that has not closed.

One megawatt-hour, two states of matter

Ormat's third-quarter filing shows electricity revenue of $514.5m on 5,476,168 megawatt-hours for the nine months to 30 September, a realized $93.9 per megawatt-hour. Backing the six-month figures out of the nine-month ones leaves a third quarter of $163.6m on 1,642,807 megawatt-hours, or $99.6 — the year's best quarterly rate, and a seasonal summer one. Against $92.6 in 2025 and $94.3 in 2024, the contract book has barely moved.

"PPA pricing today are well above $100 per megawatt hour. We do not see any trend of reducing this pricing," chief executive Doron Blachar told investors on 5 November. The catch is tenor: Ormat's plants sell substantially all output under contracts averaging about fourteen years remaining, and 190 MW running to 2031-2034 is priced at a weighted average near $86. Sixty megawatts have been repriced under blend-and-extend amendments, with roughly 200 MW of recontracting upside through 2034.

Fervo, meanwhile, guides 2027 revenue of $60-80m. Against roughly 100 MW of Cape Station Phase I at a 90% capacity factor — 788,400 megawatt-hours — that is $76 to $101 per megawatt-hour. Its $11.9bn binding book across 1,054 MW implies about $95 on a fifteen-year, 90%-availability assumption. The same price, on about 0.5% of Ormat's volume.

Batteries carried Ormat's quarter

The weakest line at Ormat is the geothermal one. Electricity revenue fell 2.1% in the quarter and its gross margin dropped to 22.2% from 25.4%, hurt by a hurricane-related outage at Puna that cost $5.1m of revenue; excluding Puna, margin would have been about 24.5%. Energy storage grew 145.2% to $49.9m at a 62.1% margin on strong PJM merchant pricing, producing roughly $31.0m of the $77.4m consolidated gross profit from 18% of revenue. The product segment shrank 1.2%, and its backlog fell to $183m from $202.8m.

Consolidated revenue still rose 10.2% to $275.1m and full-year guidance went up, to $1.17-1.2bn of revenue and $640-650m of adjusted EBITDA, with 1,358 MW of electricity capacity. "We expect to see an increase in margin already in Q4, and we expect to see a jump already in 2027," chief financial officer Assi Ginzburg said on the same call. Consensus does not share the optimism past then: 2027 revenue is modelled 3.5% lower than 2026, with earnings per share down 6.0%.

What $1.3bn buys at Fervo

Fervo's news is delivery. GeoBlock 1 at Cape Station reached commercial operation on 30 September, ahead of contract, 33 MW after 23 months of construction. The binding book grew 60% from the May listing, half of it hyperscalers and data centers; the Google agreement is 396 MW grid-connected on a fifteen-year take-or-pay with mid-2028 delivery. On 18 November Fervo added a 105 MW firm transmission agreement with Longroad Energy, taking contracted transmission to 505 MW from 2028.

Costs are falling where they must. Early Nevada pilot wells took 70 days to 11,220 feet; Phase I averaged 21 days to 14,483 feet, with the first four horizontal wells at Cape dropping from $9.4m to $4.8m apiece. "Our long-term target remains at $3,000 per kilowatt... we are positioned to achieve $5,500 per kilowatt of installed CapEx for GeoBlocks 4 through 11," chief executive Timothy Latimer said on 9 November, against about $7,000 for Phase I. Second-half capital spending guidance was cut to $700-750m from $850-900m while the 2028 schedule held.

What is not settled is the money. Fervo ended the quarter with $1.9bn of unrestricted cash against $301.6m of debt, facing $450-500m of fourth-quarter capex and about $2.2bn on Cape Google 400 through 2028, with project financing expected mid-2027 at roughly 70% loan-to-value. At a $3.18bn market capitalization and 1.14x book, the market is paying something near $1.3bn above the cash for the contracted megawatts.

How much the quarters explain

Fervo rose 35.5% over the month to 23 November, most of it on 9 November, when the results lifted it 26.1% in a session. That month is explained by company news, and it came against a power complex de-rating after the midterms on tighter data-center permitting odds, with SMR down 34.6% and Vistra down 10.5%. What the month does not explain is the year: Fervo remains 70% below its 13 May first close of $36.54, and neither it nor Ormat has broken the downtrend its moving averages have shown since the summer. On 20 November, as the S&P 500 tracker fell 3.6%, Fervo gave back 10.3% in one session.

Ormat went nowhere over the same thirty days and is down 21% over twelve months. Its trailing EV/EBITDA has compressed to 15.8x from 19.6x in late September and forward earnings to 31.1x from about 36x, still above the 10-12x typical of owners of contracted power, with trailing free cash flow negative. The group is growing; the geothermal fleet inside it is not, and the repricing that would change that arrives in 60 MW increments against a 190 MW block locked near $86 for another five years.

Blachar, asked about enhanced geothermal on 5 November, gave the test both companies now face: "The question that matters is not whether initial flow can be achieved. It is whether a project can sustain temperature, flow, water balance, and well integrity over many years with seismicity safely managed at the cost the PPA supports." Fervo's own pilot, three and a half years old, is already showing the thermal decline it was built to measure.

In the first week of November two companies that sit on the same radio-frequency shelf reported the largest quarters in their histories, and the growth came from the same narrow place: the silicon inside a high-speed optical module. MACOM Technology Solutions, which makes analog, microwave and photonic parts for optical networks, radar and base stations, booked $424.8m in its September quarter, up 62.7% on the year, with data center at $188.1m, or 44% of the total. SiTime, which sells silicon MEMS timing devices against incumbent quartz crystals, reported $298.7m, up 257%.

The shelf was marked down anyway, and the reason given was that radio chips were rolling over. That reading assumes these businesses still turn with handset and base-station builds. The November disclosures describe something else: a content-per-box cycle in which the bill of materials for an optical module grows every time the industry steps up a speed grade, so revenue rises without a single extra module shipping.

What a speed grade adds to a module

SiTime put numbers on the step. Moving from electrical modules to extended pluggable optics doubles timing content; near-package optics drives twice as many oscillators; co-packaged optics drives three times the content. Coherent modules need gigahertz-class precision, where parts sell for $6 to $8 each against $1 to $2 for megahertz devices, an uplift of up to five times. "More bandwidth requires more precision timing," chief executive Rajesh Vashist told investors on 4 November.

The mix is doing what that implies. SiTime's communications, enterprise and data-center line was $127.3m, up 202% year on year. Eight-hundred-gigabit revenue grew fourfold while management conceded it had held low share at 400G, so this is share capture layered on a unit ramp. The acquired Renesas timing division contributed $86.3m from 1 July; strip it out and core revenue still grew 154%, which settles whether the quarter was bought. What is not disclosed is units or distributor inventory weeks, and the binding constraint is wafer supply, which management expects to stay tight through 2027.

SiTime holds roughly 95% of the MEMS oscillator market and competes mostly against quartz suppliers, which still account for about 72% of a frequency-control market put at $6.84bn this year. The conversion, not the cycle, is the growth.

MACOM shipped 7% of its quarter from inside it

MACOM's evidence is its order book. The company ended fiscal 2026 with record backlog, a 1.4-to-1 book-to-bill in the fourth quarter and 1.5-to-1 for the year, its heaviest bookings year. Only 7% of quarterly revenue shipped from orders received in the same quarter, which management attributed to backlog depth and extended lead times. Gross margin rose every quarter of the year, from 54.6% to 59.9%, and the January quarter is guided to 62% to 63% on revenue of $450m to $460m.

"Being able to better utilize some of our existing manufacturing facilities has been a contributor," chief financial officer John Kober said on 5 November, naming volume, utilization and new products. The soft spot is the old business: "We see some softness in PON. We see some softness in some of our broadband business," chief executive Steve Daly said on the same call. Telecom is $72.1m and grew 1% sequentially. Industrial and defence, at $164.6m, includes follow-on shipborne radar and air-to-air missile orders and a low-Earth-orbit constellation win worth about $20m beginning in early 2028.

The names that were supposed to be deteriorating

The competitors held up the same way. Semtech reported record sales of $423.0m on 23 November, up 58%, with a record 33% adjusted operating margin and data-center revenue guided up about 160% year on year on 800G and 1.6-terabit ramps. MaxLinear's September quarter grew 72%, with infrastructure revenue near $125m, up 210%, and its optical data-center outlook raised to $250m to $270m. Both fell harder over 30 days than either protagonist.

One order book in this group is genuinely flat: indie Semiconductor's automotive strategic backlog held at $6.2bn year on year, with a $39.0m operating loss on $70.2m of revenue. It was the best 30-day performer of the five, and at 4.0x trailing gross profit it carries a sixth of MACOM's rating, so the market is discriminating by business quality even while the shelf moves together.

The markdown came from outside the order books

The timing points elsewhere. The Nasdaq had its worst week since 2020 in the week to 20 November on doubts about artificial-intelligence profitability, and when Nvidia's board reinstated Jensen Huang on 23 November, MACOM rose 16.8%, SiTime 18.8%, MaxLinear 22.6% and Semtech 17.5% in one session. These names traded as exposure to the artificial-intelligence complex, not as a radio order book.

What the drawdown did do is reset the rating against rising profits. MACOM's price to trailing gross profit stands at 23.1x, against roughly 50x on 22 May, a figure derived from that day's close and trailing gross profit, which has since grown 30%; its forward price-to-earnings ratio of 23.6x sits on consensus fiscal 2027 earnings of $9.80 a share, up 78%. Needham cut its target to $300 from $410 while keeping a Buy. SiTime remains the dearest of the five at 34.5x trailing gross profit, down from about 90x in May, and that figure is distorted upward by purchase accounting on the Renesas deal, which held reported gross margin to 51.6% against 70.7% on an adjusted basis.

So the business explains none of the three- and six-month declines at either company and the valuation reset explains all of it. What remains unresolved is supply: SiTime's wafers are short into next year, and MACOM is lifting capital spending to $100m-$110m with about 60% aimed at fab expansion. The content per module is rising faster than either company can currently build it, and that is a different problem from the one the shares were sold on.

TTM Technologies, which fabricates the printed circuit boards that sit underneath other companies' AI servers and inside American weapons programs, committed an additional $400m of capital spending in October on top of $1.3bn already planned through 2029. The money goes into four plants in China and the 800,000-square-foot Penang plant in Malaysia, built brownfield for rapid ramp, with revenue expected around 2028.

Since late June the printed-circuit and contract-assembly shelf has been marked as though the AI order book were being withdrawn. TTM's own disclosures run the other way. Revenue growth accelerated through every quarter of 2026, from 30% year on year in the first to 60% in the third; the 90-day backlog stood at $1.3bn at the October call, up 111%; and full-year guidance was raised to $4.47–4.49bn. What has been revised down is the price on those earnings, not the earnings.

The shares closed at $69.20 on 23 November against $210.57 on 25 June, a 67% fall that leaves them up only 7% over twelve months. A quarter of that came on 22 October, earnings day, when the stock fell 25% close to close and took the shelf with it: Celestica dropped 11.5%, Flex 12.4%, Sanmina 9.8%. Garmin, which shares the vendor industry label and nothing else, rose.

What the October call actually disclosed

Data center and networking was 41% of third-quarter sales and grew 115%. Inside it sits the product that explains the capital spending: N+M asymmetrical-layer boards, roughly four times the complexity of conventional ones and carrying gross margins well above the corporate average. "It is currently about 30% of the data center and networking activities, and it will grow next year to about 50% of that group," chief executive Edwin Roks told investors on 22 October.

Defense is the other half of the business and the harder one to displace. Aerospace and defense was 34% of third-quarter sales, grew 21%, and carries a record $2.0bn program backlog against $1.5bn a year earlier, on a book-to-bill of 1.6x that gives management 65% visibility into 2027 revenue. TTM describes itself as America's largest domestic manufacturer of advanced printed circuit boards, with content on more than 480 defense programs under ITAR-compliant operations. The Syracuse, New York plant serving that demand began its ultra-HDI ramp in the third quarter and targets full capacity in 2028, with no disclosed slip.

One program did slip. A single large N+M job moved on the customer's schedule, pushing fourth-quarter data-center growth guidance to about 150% year on year where Needham had modeled 195%; the firm cut its target to $150 from $175 and kept its Buy. Copper-clad laminate and T-glass lead times are running 12 to 24 weeks, a constraint on panels built rather than panels wanted.

The negatives are on the balance sheet

TTM entered October having spent $1.1bn in cash on Epiq Solutions and then announced a $650m all-cash purchase of Ophir Optics, an infrared and laser-optics maker, three days before the print. Net leverage was 2.8x at the end of September, and trailing free-cash-flow yield is 0.20% — the plants are consuming the cash the income statement generates. "This will be the end of the big stuff for a while," chief financial officer Daniel Boehle said on the same call, promising to get leverage back down.

That is the case against the stock, and it is a financing case. On earnings, the market is paying 8.80x trailing gross profit against roughly 35x in late June, a de-rating of about three-quarters over five months in which trailing gross profit grew 30%. Forward earnings are 13.9x on consensus of $4.96 a share, against consensus net income growth above 100%.

Plexus is marked for an exposure it does not have

Plexus, a fee-for-service manufacturer in Neenah, Wisconsin that builds medical, industrial and defense hardware for other companies' brands, fell 9.0% on 20 November after Ming-Chi Kuo of TF International reported that Google had cut planned 2027 shipments of Broadcom-designed accelerators by about a million units. Plexus does not build those. Its data-center work is an emerging funnel of conservatively about $0.5bn in power and thermal management, with two announced programs. Its revenue came from industrial, up 27.4% in fiscal 2026, defense up 23.0% and healthcare up 18.6%. The manufacturing funnel set a record at $4.9bn, up 33%, and the cash conversion cycle fell to 62 days. Yet at 10.75x trailing gross profit Plexus is dearer than TTM on the same measure, with forward earnings at 18.8x against roughly 20% growth and a trailing free-cash-flow yield of minus 0.54%.

How much the results explain

Almost none of TTM's two-thirds decline is explained by orders, and the part that is explained is a timing miss on one program worth about 10% of a single quarter's data-center line. What the results do not settle is whether a company at 2.8x leverage, generating no free cash, can fund $1.7bn of capacity and close three acquisitions without returning to the debt market — the one thing a 47% pre-guided revenue increase for 2027 depends on. Plexus's markdown has a simpler cause: it was sold by association on a report about silicon it never touches, which is why its drawdown is shallower and its de-rating milder.

The constraint TTM keeps describing is laminate and glass, measured in weeks of lead time. Nothing in the October disclosures suggests anyone has stopped asking for the panels.

The constraint on Microchip Technology's December quarter is not an order book that stopped growing. It is assembly and test capacity that data-center chipmakers rented out from underneath it.

That matters because the whole analog shelf has been traded since May as an automotive and industrial restocking cycle that had run its course, with the rollover in share prices read as evidence that distributors had finished refilling and orders would follow them down. The disclosures from Microchip, NXP Semiconductors and Analog Devices say restocking never arrived in the first place, and that the binding problem now is on the supply side of mature-node silicon, where little capacity has been added for years.

Lead times quadrupled at the top end

Microchip, the Chandler, Arizona maker of microcontrollers, analog and timing parts for industrial, automotive, computing and aerospace customers, reported 25 days of inventory in its distribution channel at 30 September, down from 26 days in March. Over the same stretch its lead times went from the 4–8 weeks that had prevailed to 6–32 weeks. Its fiscal 2027 risk disclosure states plainly that certain suppliers have notified plans to discontinue legacy manufacturing processes as industry capacity is increasingly directed toward data centers, with no assurance that necessary foundry capacity will be available on acceptable terms.

Chief executive Steve Sanghi told investors on 5 November that the company has "substantial unsupported backlog on high-margin products that our supply chain cannot fulfill", and that capacity at major outsourced assembly and test suppliers had been displaced by AI companies. Customers are not hoarding against it. "We can hardly get them to place orders with a new lead time," Sanghi said on the same call.

NXP, the Eindhoven supplier of microcontrollers, connectivity and processors to carmakers and industrial customers, ended its September quarter with distribution channel inventory at 11 weeks, its stated long-term target, while 80% of its portfolio now carries lead times beyond 16 weeks against 25% a year earlier. Its channel sell-in grew slower than its direct shipments: distributor revenue rose 8.3% sequentially to $2,244m against 8.9% for direct customers. Chief financial officer William Betts said on 27 October that "the book to bill remains well above 1 across all end markets and regions," and chief executive Rafael Sotomayor denied seeing broad-based restocking. Analog Devices' channel weeks ran below its 6-to-7-week target in the August quarter, with book-to-bill above 1 but "not in that sort of unusually high space".

Cars are the slow line, not the engine

The restocking framing also had the wrong end market. Analog Devices' industrial revenue grew 53% year on year to $1.97bn in the August quarter and communications 84%, while automotive grew 16%. NXP's Industrial and Internet of Things line rose 39% and communications infrastructure 54%, against 13% for automotive in its first $2bn automotive quarter. Microchip's industrial sales rose 50.3% and data center 55.7%; automotive, at 19.3%, was its slowest. Vehicle output is not the culprit: S&P Global Mobility's October forecast raised 2027 production for Europe by 68,000 units and Greater China by 258,000.

One order book, three prices

All three businesses are accelerating. Microchip has lifted year-on-year revenue growth for four straight quarters to 42.2%, gross margin by 10.0 points to 65.9% and operating margin from 12.8% to 28.6%. Its December guide of -3% to +1% sequentially, with gross margin stepping to 64–65%, is the cost of parts it cannot get and of $53m of one-time items that will not repeat; management said the March-quarter backlog is better than December's was at the same point.

The shares disagree. Over six months Microchip is down 37.3%, NXP 22.7% and Analog Devices 8.1%, and the week to 20 November took roughly 17% out of the PHLX Semiconductor Index before chip stocks rebounded on 23 November after Nvidia reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive.

Microchip's price-to-gross-profit has fallen from 20.1x in May to 9.4x now while gross profit grew 67.4%, and its 45.4x trailing earnings become 16.8x forward. NXP is cheaper still at 8.2x trailing gross profit, with a 5.7% free-cash-flow yield and a $6.3bn buyback authorization worth about a tenth of its market value; Microchip, at 2.36x net debt to EBITDA, cannot buy its own de-rated stock. Analog Devices is the one whose price tracks its results, and it has quietly got cheaper doing so, at 19.9x trailing gross profit against 25.7x in May.

So the split inside this shelf is about what investors will pay, not what the businesses are earning. What would break the thesis is visible and countable: distributor weeks climbing above target, or book-to-bill slipping under 1. What would confirm it is duller, and is already partly disclosed, which is backlog shifting from December into March as capacity frees up.

Microchip is absorbing the foundry and assembly price increases that start in January rather than passing them on. "We don't want to push the luck," Sanghi said. A company that is holding prices down while turning away high-margin orders is not describing a demand problem.

What one procedure pays turned out to be the whole argument in nerve and prostate devices this year. Procept BioRobotics, which places image-guided surgical robots in hospitals and sells a disposable handpiece for every Aquablation treatment of an enlarged prostate, now collects roughly $3,570 of handpiece revenue per case, up from about $3,200 a year earlier as its newer HYDROS platform displaced the older system and end-of-quarter bulk discounts were dropped.

Over the month to 23 November the first two rose 25% and 52%; Inspire fell 24%, and is down 36% over twelve months. The segment did not turn up together, and the reason is mechanical.

Procept's growth is coming out of installed robots, not new ones

Procept performed more than 13,400 aquablations in the third quarter, up 22% year over year, with handpiece revenue up 21% and the handpiece-to-procedure ratio at 106% against 98% the prior quarter as field inventory normalized. Revenue of $98.3m grew 18%, slower than the 37% of full-year 2025, but gross margin reached 66.3% against 60.6% in the fourth quarter of 2025.

Utilization, not capital placement, is doing the work. "In the third quarter, procedure volume at HYDROS accounts was more than 35% higher than legacy AQUABEAM accounts," chief executive Larry Wood told investors on 4 November, attributing it to surgeon migration between the two platforms. Capital pricing held at about $495,000 for a new system and $315,000 for a replacement, and management reaffirmed roughly 54,000 procedures for the year. Aquablation is still the challenger in its market: transurethral resection accounted for 53.7% of US procedures for benign prostatic hyperplasia in 2025 against Aquablation's 15.6%. The shares trade at 3.65x forward sales and 5.65x forward gross profit, against 4.09x and 6.33x trailing, and remain 74.8% below their December 2024 peak; Truist raised its target to $24 while keeping a Hold, and consensus sits essentially at spot.

Axogen's step-up was a change in what the product legally is

Axogen sells Avance, a processed human nerve allograft that bridges a severed nerve without harvesting a donor nerve from the patient. On 3 December 2025 the Food and Drug Administration approved its Biologics License Application, moving the graft from human-tissue classification to a licensed biologic. The payment followed: Medicare set 2026 facility reimbursement at $8,965 for hospital outpatient and $6,157 for ambulatory surgery centers, and commercial coverage reached about 96% of US lives after Elevance removed its nerve-gap-length criterion in August.

Third-quarter revenue of $75.8m grew 26.2%, guidance went to at least 29% growth and $290m, and breast reconstruction, now 64% of revenue, accelerated past 60% growth at roughly 11% penetration of eligible cases. Gross margin fell 180 basis points to 74.8% on costlier long-length production, the one line going the wrong way. The move itself is dated: shares jumped on the 28 October beat, with adjusted earnings of $0.21 against $0.13 expected, then climbed again after a 9 November presentation putting a $754m market behind prostate nerve reconstruction at about $7,000 per procedure, none of it in guidance. At 12.0x forward sales and 105x forward earnings, the price already discounts two more years near 30% growth.

Inspire sells the best gross margin and cannot get it billed

Inspire's hypoglossal-nerve stimulator for obstructive sleep apnea carries an 86% gross margin and is implanted once. From the first quarter of 2026 providers could no longer use the billing code previously applied to roughly 10,000 Medicare placements of Inspire V, costing about $30m of third-quarter revenue and $120m for the year. Revenue fell 7.9% and operating margin swung from positive 4.3% to minus 13.9%.

A dedicated permanent code exists on paper. "This dedicated Category I code is set to take effect on January 1, 2028, and stands to meaningfully simplify coding and reimbursement, thereby expanding access to Inspire therapy," chairman and chief executive Tim Herbert said on 2 November. The valuation process that sets the physician fee begins with a committee panel in January 2027, and management conceded the shorter Inspire V procedure could be valued lower. Chief financial officer Matt Osberg guided to "low single-digit year-over-year revenue growth for the full year 2027." The market has taken him at his word and then some: forward price-to-sales of 1.82x sits above the 1.76x trailing, which is a bet on another decline, even as the company holds $453.1m of cash, no debt and the cheapest gross-profit multiple of the three at 2.05x trailing. Barclays cut its target to $62 at underweight on 3 November and the shares have fallen every week since.

How much of this the numbers account for

For Procept and Axogen, most of it. Both moves rest on disclosed procedure counts and a raise, and in Axogen's case on a reimbursement schedule with dates attached. For Inspire the de-rating is also earned, by a coding gap that is arithmetic rather than demand, which is why its 86% margin and free cash flow sit awkwardly beside a price that assumes shrinkage through 2027. What the shelf does not have is one shared mechanism: the category average was also lifted by LivaNova and Integra LifeSciences on their own quarters, inside a November rotation out of artificial-intelligence equities into healthcare.

Axogen closes at the highest level in its record on a market it will not begin developing until 2027, and the next hard numbers for it land at a March 2027 investor day. Inspire's own fix arrives on a calendar nobody can accelerate, and the company has to fund a sales organization through more than a year of temporary billing to reach it.

The price of sending a text message in the United States went up three times this year, and the two companies best known for sending them did not get any of the increase.

Twilio and Bandwidth both reported the September quarter on 29 October, both raised full-year guidance on every metric, and both gave preliminary 2027 outlooks above their own prior frameworks. Since then Twilio has fallen 38.1% from the $301.27 it closed at on 1 October and Bandwidth 50.0% from its 9 July high of $78.44. The markdown reads as a verdict that AI agents will route around the messaging and voice rails before the rails get paid for them. The published rate cards say something narrower: the meter is intact, it is still being repriced upward, and the increases are accruing to the phone carriers.

Who gets paid on a Verizon text

Twilio lists a standard outbound American SMS segment at $0.0083 per 160 characters, the same across long codes, toll-free numbers and short codes, with carrier fees applied automatically on top. Since 1 October the terminating carrier's cut on Verizon has been $0.0050, an 11.1% rise. T-Mobile went from $0.0030 to $0.0045 on 19 January, a 50% increase, and AT&T to $0.0035 in April; on 1 November the surcharge stepped to $0.005 on six further networks. Add the two published rates and a Verizon-terminated message costs its buyer about $0.0133, of which roughly 38% never stops at Twilio. Twilio's own rate did not move.

Substitution changes the billed unit without removing it. A conversational Rich Communication Services session opens once a thread holds at least four rich messages with two genuine replies, and Verizon's session fee is $0.009, so since October a four-message exchange is cheaper billed as a session than as four messages.

Where the two part company is earned margin

Twilio's non-GAAP gross margin was 49.2% in the quarter, down 90 basis points, and chief financial officer Aidan Viggiano said it would have been up 80 without $53m of incremental US carrier fees. Messaging grew 24% as reported and 17% organically, with about seven of those points supplied by pass-through.

On that axis Bandwidth is the heavier case. Total revenue was a record $234m, up 22%, but cloud communications, its own measure of earned business, grew 12% to $158m, and messaging surcharge revenue rose $25m, or 51% out of a $42.1m total increase: 59% of the incremental dollars. GAAP gross margin fell 200 basis points to 36.2%, which management attributed solely to the surcharge line.

Strip it out and the comparison inverts. Bandwidth's non-GAAP gross margin rose 300 basis points to 60.6%, roughly 1,140 basis points above Twilio's by subtraction, on a network it owns and interconnects in about 70 countries. "SI is raising the value of that layer," chief executive David Morken said on the 29 October call of the network itself. "A legacy carrier connection cannot do that."

The unit that pays nine times more is barely metered

Twilio's ConversationRelay bills $0.07 per minute of active AI session on top of $0.0085 for an inbound local voice minute, so about $0.0785 against $0.0085 for a bare minute. That is the largest per-unit price step either company has. It is also almost empty. "The way that we characterize voice AI in our prepared remarks is that it's about 1% of what the voice interactions are so far," chief executive Khozema Shipchandler told analysts on 29 October, calling consumer AI "a fraction of that 1%." Neither company disclosed a dollar figure for AI voice revenue.

What the results account for, and what they do not

Some of the decline has fundamental content, all of it second-derivative. Twilio's organic messaging growth slipped from 18% to 17%, dollar-based net expansion from 116% to 114%, and fourth-quarter non-GAAP operating income is guided to $300–310m against $326m in the third, on higher hosting costs, which is what coverage blamed for a 19.2% two-day fall after a 12.2% earnings beat. HSBC's downgrade to Reduce, with a $211 target, argues the market already prices a generous share of future AI-agent volume into Twilio. None of that is a volume decline, a price cut or a named customer loss, and the surcharge steps this autumn ran the other way.

The rest is a rating change imposed from outside, arriving in a week when the S&P 500 confirmed a correction on 20 November on doubts about AI spending durability, and both names bounced with the complex on 23 November, Twilio 8.3% to $186.46 and Bandwidth 14.6% to $39.25. Twilio now trades at 9.8x forward gross profit against the 13.0x on the Desk's record in August, while gross profit grew 20%. Bandwidth is at 3.8x trailing gross profit against 6.5x in May, a 42% compression while GAAP gross profit grew 14.9%, and its balance-sheet risk has gone the wrong way for the bears: all but about $7m of the 2028 convertible notes is retired and net leverage is 1.3x.

The asymmetry is in who holds the pen on price. Twilio and Bandwidth set what a message and a minute earn them and have held those rates flat; the carriers reset their cut whenever they choose, and chose four times in eleven months. Whatever an AI agent ends up paying for access to a phone number, the surcharge on the text it sends will have moved again first.
