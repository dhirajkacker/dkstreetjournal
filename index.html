ServiceNow fell 27% on 29 October after guiding fourth-quarter growth in its current contracted backlog down to 18.5% from 21%, reviving the fear that AI will break seat-based software pricing. The operating line moved with it: a margin of 9.1% against -2.7% in the second quarter. The fear that the profit is borrowed from somewhere else fails on its own arithmetic, since net income of $12.3m sits below operating income of $21.3m, and the $2.036bn trade-secret award Appian won against Pegasystems was vacated with a new trial ordered, contributing nothing pending a retrial set for January 2027.

Cash has not yet followed. Appian disclosed roughly -$15m of cash flow against +$19m a year earlier, blamed on collection timing; an aggregator computes free cash flow of -$20.0m after capital spending. Two more drags sit outside the subscription book: a guided 2-3 percentage point currency headwind to fourth-quarter reported growth, and professional-services margin down to 24% from 31%. Growth paid for one advance, the multiple for the other

JFrog's price-to-gross-profit is 25.1x against 24.93x on 22 August, so the intervening gain was bought with gross-profit growth rather than enthusiasm. So the pricing mechanism survives the test and the valuations absorb most of its reward. Neither protagonist is cheap on the strength of that, and Figma's quarter is the standing evidence that the market is not currently paying for retention.

What neither company has shown is what customers do above the floor. JFrog's record backlog is contracted; the overage revenue sitting on top of it is the line the market stopped trusting this month, after Datadog told investors its largest customer had cut usage and guided the fourth quarter assuming that customer pays only its minimum.

Dell's October quarter landed after the close on 24 November, a day behind Hewlett Packard Enterprise's, and between them the two prints answer the question the enterprise server shelf has been marked down on: who pays for memory. Dell's Infrastructure Solutions Group, which sells its rack, blade and hyperscale servers alongside storage, earned an operating margin of 17% of segment revenue on $42bn of revenue, up more than four percentage points from a year earlier, disclosed in the 8-K filed that day.

Going in, the quarter was read as the reverse story. Dell, HPE, Lenovo and Super Micro book multi-quarter artificial-intelligence server orders at prices agreed before enterprise memory costs went vertical, and the shares have been priced as though that increase lands on the vendor. What the two American vendors disclosed this week is that it is largely landing on the buyer. Only one of them expects that to get harder next year.

Memory is now most of the box

Memory has passed half of a server's bill of materials, with cumulative memory cost inflation above 400% since early 2024. DigiTimes reported on 23 November that Dell, HPE, Lenovo and Supermicro are demanding 25% to 45% more at delivery than the prices written into signed contracts. The repricing is contested rather than contractual: XTX Markets sued Dell in Ireland in June over $62-70m of charges sought above an agreed $549m server order.

The supply reason is that the capacity which would make server memory modules is making stacks for accelerators instead. TrendForce puts high-bandwidth memory at 23% of all DRAM wafer capacity in 2026 and close to 30% in 2027, with conventional DRAM contract prices projected up 10-15% in the fourth quarter.

Dell repriced faster than its inputs rose

Dell's revenue doubled to $54.9bn and gross margin came in at 21.04%, a touch above the 20.71% of a year earlier and the recovery leg of a curve that bottomed at 17.75% in the April quarter. Operating income rose 251% on revenue up 103%. Backlog closed at a record $132.3bn, which chief financial officer David Kennedy told investors on 24 November "has been fully vetted through our rigorous due diligence process."

"Infrastructure demand continues to outpace supply," chief executive Jeffrey Clarke said on the same call. "As we look into next year, the gap between component supply and end-user demand continues to widen." Dell raised fiscal 2027 guidance to $202bn of revenue and $29.68 of non-GAAP earnings per share, with operating margin guided up roughly 350 basis points on an AI server mix three times the prior year's.

The 14% that moved HPE

HPE's fourth quarter, reported on 23 November, lifted gross margin to 38.0% from 30.4% and produced an 18.4% operating margin in Cloud & AI, the segment housing its ProLiant servers, on segment revenue up 69%. For fiscal 2027 management guided that segment to about 14%, citing this year's steep price increases moderating, a more dilutive AI mix weighted to the back half, and component costs still rising. Chief financial officer Marie Myers was reported as telling the call that costs were rising faster than pricing.

This is not the Juniper integration problem. Networking segment margin was guided to expand into the mid-to-high 20s, and the acquisition's annualized cost synergy target was raised to $800m from $450m.

Subscription revenue is not large enough to carry the mix either: annual recurring revenue reached $3.5bn, up 12%, against company revenue up 50% in the quarter. The warning is about parts, with memory and NAND shortages expected to restrict fulfilment until at least the second half of 2028 and lead times of 28 to 40-plus weeks.

What the prints explain, and what they don't

HPE fell 15.6% on 24 November. Dell fell about 12% during that session, before its own results, then recovered roughly 4.8% in after-hours trading once the backlog was out. Over thirty days Dell is down 35% and HPE 28% against 5% for the S&P 500 tracker, with Super Micro down 32% over the same stretch; both Dell and HPE remain well above where they traded a year ago.

One guided number does real work here, and it is HPE's. Four points of margin coming out of the segment that produces most of its revenue is a reason to pay less, and consensus still models group operating margin near 17.6% for fiscal 2027, above what management guided for Cloud & AI. Dell's de-rating has no such anchor in its own disclosure: every reported margin line expanded, guidance went up, and the board added $25bn to the repurchase authorization. Dell trades at 11.4x consensus fiscal 2027 earnings of $26.00 against 12.2x trailing, where trailing was 39x in August; HPE sits at 8.1x forward against the 10-13x it fetched in May. Antonio Neri, HPE's chief executive, put it plainly on 23 November: "I will argue that based on that profile we should deserve a higher multiple because I don't see us at a multiple that is 8 to 9x, makes no sense whatsoever."

What neither vendor controls is allocation. The wafers that would build server memory are building stacks for the accelerators that created the demand, and HPE expects the shortage to run into the second half of 2028 — the same window in which Dell has to deliver a record backlog at the margin it just printed.

The order book now has dates and purchase orders behind it

Management set out four steps on the 3 November call. Silicon carbide rated at 1.2 kilovolts, replacing silicon inside the alternating-to-direct-current power supplies that feed a rack, ramps this quarter and into the first half of 2027, where Navitas says it typically holds 30% to 40% of the power-supply content. Rack-level 400- and 800-volt silicon carbide designs have "large orders in place" for a first-half 2027 ramp. The wait costs $34m a quarter

Navitas has finished leaving the business that used to pay its bills: mobile and low-end consumer fell below 5% of revenue last quarter and management expects it to be insignificant by the December quarter, which leaves high-power markets at essentially the entire company. The cost of standing in that position is visible. The operating loss widened to $34.4m on $14.0m of revenue, worse than the $27.2m of the prior quarter, so the revenue turn has not reached the loss line. Pro forma cash is about $405m with no debt, which funds roughly three years of losses at the current rate. On 6 October Navitas completed its purchase of Claros for about $232.8m in cash and shares, buying integrated voltage regulator technology for the last inches of delivery to the processor.

That purchase walked it into Vicor's room. Vicor, which builds modular direct-current converters for computing, defense and test equipment, used its own October call to argue the opposite case: "Vicor proprietary dual FPA enables 10% to 15% reduction in AI processor power loss with over 40x the current gain of IVRs," sales head Philip Davies said on 20 October, meaning integrated regulators of the kind Navitas just bought cost power to insert.

Its December guide of 10%-plus product growth and a $43m royalty minimum implies total revenue falling to roughly $186m, because the catch-up does not repeat. Bel Fuse, which makes power supplies and magnetics, grew data solutions revenue 61% year over year and took $12.4m from one artificial-intelligence customer against $3.2m a year earlier.

So the transition is paying somebody now, on shipments, at 68.3% gross margin in Vicor's case and 40.9% in Bel Fuse's.

What the markdown accounts for

Navitas closed at $8.56 on the day of its results, up 7.94%, then $6.595 three weeks later on 24 November, 23% below the post-earnings close and 47% below late August, with no adverse company disclosure in between. Tower Semiconductor's customers are paying the Israeli foundry before any wafer ships. The company, which makes analog-intensive mixed-signal chips for telecom, automotive, industrial and medical customers from fabs in Migdal Haemek and abroad, reported customer prepayments and deferred revenue of about $249m in its third-quarter presentation, against $26m at the end of 2025, plus $92m of long-term customer advances against $2m. That matters because the November selloff in foundry shares was read as the market calling the top in chip-factory spending. Tower's own disclosures say the opposite about its order book. Revenue growth accelerated through 2026, from 15.5% year on year in the first quarter to 23.7% and then 33.0%, and gross margin expanded from 26.8% to 34.0% over the same three quarters. Gross profit rose 92% year on year.

Half the company is now light

Silicon photonics alone was about $240m of the $526m third quarter, roughly 46% of revenue, and Tower guided its annualized silicon-photonics run rate above $1.1bn on fourth-quarter group revenue of about $600m. Its 200mm fabs ran at 85–90% utilization and the 300mm fab ran above its 85% model. Management raised the long-term annual model to $3.6bn of revenue at a 45% gross margin, $760m higher than the February 2026 version.

The prepayments are the mechanism behind that confidence. "Customers will never sign up to being the end of '26 now, not '27, not just '28, but 2029, unless they have faith that, number one, you can ship wafers on time at the highest quality," chief executive Russell Ellwanger said on the same call. Tower's $920m capacity program was 65% paid through the third quarter, self-funded, with every long-term agreement securing that capacity signed. On 3 November it announced a collaboration with Innolight on near-package optics for AI infrastructure.

The fall was the rating, not the estimates

Shares fell 9.6% on 20 November, rose 18.4% on 23 November and fell 8.7% again on 24 November, leaving them 46.8% below the 24 September high. The same gap sits at the other two. What breaks in 2028

The credible risk is not mature-node commoditization. Tower has the thinnest valuation cushion of the three and the richest growth assumption inside it.

Until that capacity arrives, the distinguishing fact is whose customers pay early. A buyer who wires cash in 2026 to hold a 2029 wafer slot has made a statement that a price chart cannot revise.

Semtech, which sells analog chips for optical data-center links, long-range LoRa radio for industrial sensors, and legacy protection and power parts, beat on both revenue and earnings after the close on Monday 23 November and guided the current quarter above what analysts had modelled. By Tuesday's close its shares stood below where they began the week. Nothing in the release accounts for that.

The November markdown across radio-frequency and wireless silicon has been read as the end of a content cycle. The two names at the centre of it just reported the opposite, and the group itself sorted by exposure to artificial-intelligence data centers, not by radio content: Applied Optoelectronics fell 45.0% over the 30 sessions to 24 November, Semtech 36.3%, MaxLinear 34.8% and MACOM 17.8%, while Skyworks was down 0.4%, Silicon Labs 0.1% and Cirrus Logic up 2.9%.

A beat, a raise, and four lower targets

Semtech's quarter ended 25 October produced revenue of $423m, up 58% year on year against a consensus near $416m, with adjusted earnings of $1.17 a share. Data center was a record $145m, up 158% year on year, and the LoRa line grew to $68m. The two together were about half the company. Reported gross margin has gone from 51.9% to 58.6% in three quarters, operating margin from 10.6% to 27.0%, and net leverage from 1.1x to 0.7x.

The guide was the part that mattered: $475-485m of revenue against $447.8m modelled, with data center at roughly $180m. "We're going to be finishing the data center with revenue roughly about $520 million," chief executive Hong Q. Hou told investors on 23 November. "We are pretty confident based on these 3 factors I just talked about the backlog, the industry trend and the momentum, we're going to be double that in FY '28 to over $1 billion." The mechanism behind it is power rather than speed: Semtech's CopperEdge equalizer saves 90% of a retimer's power with no latency cost, and its FiberEdge amplifier share of the 800-gigabit market rose from about 18% two years ago to more than half.

Shares fell 9.4% in pre-market trading on 24 November, and four brokers cut targets while largely keeping ratings: Craig-Hallum to $160 from $205, Morgan Stanley's Joseph Moore to $157, Susquehanna's Christopher Rolland to $170 and Needham's N. Quinn Bolton to $185. The close-to-close fall was 16.4%.

MACOM fell 13% with nothing to announce

MACOM, which makes radio-frequency, microwave and lightwave semiconductors for radar, satellite ground stations and optical networking, dropped 13.4% the same day on no company news. Its fiscal fourth quarter, reported 5 November, grew 62.7% year on year to $424.8m, the fourth straight acceleration, with gross margin at 59.9%. Data center was $188.1m of it, industrial and defense $164.6m, telecom $72.1m. Book-to-bill was 1.4:1 in the quarter and 1.5:1 for the year on record bookings, with only 7% of the quarter shipped from orders taken inside it. The one soft patch chief executive Steve Daly named was telecom: "We see some softness in PON. We see some softness in some of our broadband business."

The two now compete for the same socket, MACOM having launched a 3.2-terabit chipset of amplifiers, photodetectors and modulator drivers against the FiberEdge line.

MACOM trades at 19.95x trailing price-to-gross-profit against 38.0x in August 2026, a multiple halved while gross profit grew 78.5%. Semtech is at 11.2x against 25.2x in August.

What the prints explain, and what they do not

Roughly nine of Semtech's sixteen points came before the opening bell, on its own news and the target cuts. The rest arrived with the sector, and the sector is the story: Nvidia fell 9.2% close-to-close that day while the S&P 500 tracker lost half a percent, the Huang comeback rally cooling one session after it had added 32.1% to Nvidia and lifted all of these names between 18% and 23%.

indie Semiconductor, which sells automotive radar, cabin connectivity and lighting chips to carmakers, shows what the sorting means. It has no data-center revenue, it fell 1.6% over the same 30 sessions, and its markdown already happened: down 60.1% over 90 days on facts that are genuinely worse. Revenue grew 30.9% to $70.2m, but gross margin compressed to 31.8% from 41.7%, the strategic design-win backlog is flat at $6.2bn excluding its Wuxi joint venture, and $144.3m of cash stands against $170.5m of 4.00% convertible notes due 2031. Its radar target is the clearest per-socket number of the three, 50-70m units a year at $5-6 each.

So the forward multiples flatter the two data-center names, and they do it conditionally. MACOM's 20.4x forward earnings rests on consensus fiscal 2027 revenue of $1.94bn, up 46.5%; Semtech's 23.6x rests on 37.1% growth the following year. Both numbers assume the capital-spending cycle the market spent November questioning keeps funding board redesigns and 1.6-terabit modules. The prints say the orders are funded today. They cannot say what hyperscalers approve in 2028.

The cheapest explanation of the week is still the dullest one: two companies with accelerating revenue and record backlogs were repriced because of what they sell into, by a market that stopped distinguishing between a shipped order and a forecast.

Fabrinet, which assembles optical transceivers and other photonic modules in Thailand to designs its customers own, no longer has a single customer worth a fifth of its revenue. Its fiscal 2026 annual report names Cisco Systems at 19.9%, Nvidia at 16.3%, Nokia at 10.7% and Amazon at 10.5%; Nvidia's share was 27.6% the prior year and 35.1% the year before that. Celestica, the Toronto manufacturer that increasingly designs the switches and racks it builds for hyperscale data-center operators, disclosed three customers at 35%, 17% and 14% of third-quarter revenue — 66% between them, all inside one segment — with its top ten at 85% against 80% a year earlier.

The assumption buried in this autumn's markdown of both names is that design ownership is the defensible end of the artificial-intelligence build and a per-module assembly fee the fragile one. Two quarters of disclosure point the other way. The pure assembly fee held its margin and spread its customer list; the design-led program book gave up margin and cash.

The assembly fee that did not compress

Fabrinet's September quarter brought revenue of $1,474.5m, up 51%, its fourteenth consecutive quarter of growth and its seventh of accelerating year-on-year growth. Gross margin was 11.79% against 11.90% a year earlier, inside the narrow band it has occupied for five quarters while data-center volume roughly doubled. Operating income grew 55%, faster than revenue. Data-center work reached $777m, 53% of the total and up 78%.

"Revenue of $1.474 billion grew 51% from a year ago for another quarter of accelerating revenue growth," chief financial officer Csaba Sverha told investors on 2 November. The December quarter is guided to $1.6-1.65bn. Free cash flow was negative $137m in September on $159m of capital spending that included the purchase of a Santa Clara campus, and capacity is scheduled to rise from roughly $5.8bn of annual revenue at the June year-end to about $10bn by January 2027.

Celestica grew faster and kept less of it

Celestica's quarter was the bigger number: revenue of $5.83bn, up 83%, a record 8.5% adjusted operating margin, and a 2027 outlook of $40bn at 9% operating margin. Underneath it, gross margin fell to 10.99% from 13.03%, so gross profit grew 54% against 83% revenue growth: the incremental business came in below the company average. Hardware Platform Solutions, the design-owning line meant to out-earn commodity assembly, reached $2.3bn and grew 70% — slower than the group.

The cash guidance moved with it. Celestica cut 2026 free cash flow to $460m from $600m to fund working capital, set 2027 capital spending at $1.5-2.0bn, and said 2027 would be a net use of cash with a return to positive free cash flow in 2028. It has $4bn of cash after a $3.45bn equity raise priced at $310 a share. "At 90% year-to-year growth, we feel high confidence that that number is supported," chief financial officer Todd Ankenmann said of the $40bn guide on 27 October. Consensus sits at $39.32bn, in line rather than above.

The sessions that did the damage

From 26 May to 24 November, Fabrinet fell 56.8% and Celestica 46.9%, alongside Applied Optoelectronics at -77.7%, Ciena -59.8%, Flex -53.1% and Nvidia -29.9%. Arista Networks, the switch brand that gains if optics migrate inside the box, rose 5.6% — which argues the selling concerns who funds and builds capacity rather than the architecture of the link.

Two dates carry most of it. Fabrinet fell 17.5% on 18 August, the session after a fourth-quarter report of record revenue above its own guidance range. Celestica fell 16% on 20 November, the day TF International Securities analyst Ming-Chi Kuo said his survey showed Google cutting planned 2027 shipments of its Broadcom-designed TPU 8i by about 1m units, roughly 20%, with volume shifting to a MediaTek design; Broadcom fell 11.4%. Kuo said the details were not final, and neither Google nor Broadcom has commented. The November leg began with the 3 November midterm result, which CNBC described as turning the buildout into a war of attrition.

Where the compression stops being arithmetic

Fabrinet now trades at 21.8x trailing and 14.9x forward earnings, against 58.5x trailing in May, and at 17.0x trailing gross profit against 47.7x then — a two-thirds compression through four quarters of accelerating growth and an unchanged gross margin. Nothing in the reported numbers accounts for it; the de-rating is in expectations, not in the order book.

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Two companies sell the wiring that carries bits between accelerators inside an artificial-intelligence rack, and since late October the market has marked them down together on a single stated worry: that copper links are about to be designed out in favor of optics. Only one of them had something happen to its business. Credo Technology, which builds retimed copper cables, the signal-processing chips inside them and the serializer-deserializer circuits it also licenses, lost a fifth of its market value across two sessions in October after a competitor told investors it intends to take a share of the market Credo almost entirely owns.

The read-across to Astera Labs, which sells retimers, smart cable modules and the Scorpio fabric switches that connect accelerators over PCI Express and newer scale-up fabrics, assumed one architectural clock governs both. Astera's own September quarter argues against it. Revenue grew 154% year on year to $584.9m, and operating margin reached 30.6% against 20.1% two quarters earlier. The shares fell 23% in the two sessions after that print.

The 93% MaxLinear wants a piece of

On MaxLinear's 22 October call, chief executive Kishore Seendripu put Credo's share of the active electrical cable market at an estimated 93% to 99% and said his company would sell silicon to cable manufacturers rather than build finished cables, targeting 20% to 30% of the 1.6-terabit segment, with first revenue in 2027 and volume in 2028. Credo fell 10.9% that day and 9.5% the next, on roughly double its normal volume. MaxLinear is not posturing from a standing start: its quarterly revenue reached $218.1m, up 72% year on year, with infrastructure up 210% on optical data-center products.

Credo's stated defense is integration. Asked at Goldman Sachs's Communacopia conference on 10 September whether new entrants were gaining traction, chief executive Bill Brennan said the company had "decided to own the entire system solution" and that "we're the single throat to choke when it comes to delivering the SKUs that our customer wants", calling that his biggest competitive moat.

The numbers around it are mixed. Sequential revenue growth has settled near 10%, from 51.9% in the January quarter. Gross margin on a reported basis fell to 64.5% in the August quarter from 68.2%, and operating income declined sequentially to $120.7m on higher revenue. But concentration loosened rather than tightened: the top four customers were 84% of revenue, with a new 10% customer added, against roughly 88% for the top three a year and a half earlier. The December-quarter guide of $525m to $535m beat consensus, the fiscal-2027 guide of more than 85% growth is intact, and management reiterated more than $600m of that year's revenue coming from optics, including silicon-photonics chips and optical signal processors.

Astera's risk sits in the customer file

Astera is the only one of the two that prices the rack in dollars. "Today, our content per XPU across fabric switches and copper signal conditioning can exceed $1,000 per XPU," chief executive Jitendra Mohan said on the 3 November call, adding that Scorpio "became our largest product family in Q3" at more than $1bn in annualized revenue. President and chief operating officer Sanjay Gajendra said "fully loaded Orion rack scale designs contain greater than $100,000 in Astera Labs content."

What the selloff can fairly point at is in the 10-Q, not the growth rate. Customer A was 53% of quarterly revenue against 23% a year earlier, and 58% of receivables. Inventory rose to $200.9m from $59.0m at the end of 2025. And Amazon's Trainium4 incorporates both NVLink Fusion and UALink, a move away from the PCI Express network that carried Scorpio in Trainium3.

The multiples run backwards to the growth

Credo trades at 17.0x forward earnings and 12.0x forward gross profit, against 50.5x trailing gross profit in May 2026; its market value has fallen from $36.6bn to $20.1bn while trailing gross profit more than doubled. Astera, after falling nearly as far, is still 43.5x forward earnings and 22.0x forward gross profit, roughly 1.8x Credo on both, from 52.3x trailing gross profit in May on a near-identical market value.

So the decelerating business carries the cheaper multiple and the accelerating one the dearer, which is the opposite of what a shared architecture scare would produce.

Most of the November leg was not about cables at all. Jensen Huang's resignation as Nvidia chief executive on 12 November, the 20 November session in which the Nasdaq Composite lost 5.28% with the volatility index at 35.89, and his reinstatement on 23 November moved both names in lockstep with the index; Marvell, down 36.7% over the same 30 days on its all-stock Lumentum deal, fell further than either. Within that, Credo's two-day October fall is the only company-specific repricing on the shelf, and it discounts a 2028 volume event. Astera's analyst cuts were to targets, not estimates. Meanwhile Nvidia's Rubin Ultra rack, due in the second half of 2027, connects its 144 accelerators over an all-copper scale-up network, and co-packaged optics arrives for scale-up in 2027 and 2028, possibly alongside copper first.

Credo reports its fiscal second quarter after the close on 1 December, the first disclosure since a rival named a number for its market. The useful line will not be the revenue beat but the customer table: the company that just added a fifth ten-percent buyer is the one being priced as though it is about to lose one.
