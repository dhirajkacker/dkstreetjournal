CoreWeave, which rents graphics-processor capacity to large enterprises and artificial-intelligence labs, told investors on 10 November that the price of its debt had come down. "At the end of Q3, our weighted average cost of debt across our entire business was 7.9%. Down 250 basis points year over year," chief financial officer Nitin Agrawal said. "Looking ahead, we will not use the unsecured debt market to fund our business at current yields. Nor do we need to." In the same week, banks including JPMorgan and MUFG were still working, after more than six months, to distribute $38bn of construction debt tied to Texas and Wisconsin data centers leased to Oracle, whose cost of default insurance hit a record.

That inversion matters because the whole case for owning the buyers of compute rather than the sellers rested on funding source: hyperscalers were said to pay for capacity out of operating cash flow, neoclouds to borrow against backlog owed by a handful of counterparties. Amazon's free cash flow turned negative for the first time since 2023 — a $42.4bn outflow for the twelve months to 30 September against a $14.8bn inflow a year earlier, with long-term debt doubling to $128.9bn — against roughly $220bn of 2026 cash capex and "meaningfully more" planned for 2027. Oracle's trailing free cash flow yield is -7.4%, more negative than Amazon's -1.6%. Everyone in this business borrows. What differs is the rate they are offered.

What a signed GPU-hour owes before it earns

CoreWeave's September quarter shows the full chain. Revenue was $3.661bn, up 168%, an acceleration from 112% in the June quarter, and operating income turned positive at $207m. The net loss still widened to $709m, because interest expense reached $846m — 23% of revenue and 4.1 times the quarter's operating income — on $46.9bn of total debt, with fourth-quarter interest guided to $1.05–1.15bn. Backlog reached about $133bn, up 138%, more than half of it on contracts where customer delivery is expected to begin by the end of 2026.

The charge against this model is that six-year depreciation on accelerators flatters the margin by assuming residual value that evaporates. CoreWeave's rebuttal is specific: 130,000 GPUs come off contract in 2027 and 170,000 in 2028, "all fully amortized," and Ampere and Hopper capacity has repriced 30–50% higher quarter on quarter at premiums to original contracts. At current pricing, management put 2027 renewals alone at roughly $4bn of incremental annualized revenue with no incremental capital. Jim Chanos, who is short the stock, counters that after stock compensation and on a ten-year asset life the business earns a 2–3% pre-tax return on invested capital. Concentration is the unsettled part: Microsoft was 45% of first-quarter revenue, and Meta committed in March to pay up to approximately $21bn through 2032.

Nebius borrows against prepayments, and costs four times as much

Nebius Group, the Amsterdam-based operator of an AI cloud platform plus data, education and autonomous-driving units, funds the same mechanism differently: it expects more than $9bn of customer prepayments in 2026, with 70% of June-quarter deals carrying prepayments covering 50–60% of the associated capital cost. Its GPU-backed debt is dated and collateralized: roughly $775m maturing 31 October 2030 at the secured overnight financing rate plus 2.5 percentage points, beside $5.0bn of convertibles. Its last reported quarter, to June, grew revenue 454% to $582m at a 77.1% gross margin and a -30.2% operating margin. Yet it is the dearest name here, at 14.9 times forward gross profit against CoreWeave's 3.9 — nearly four times the price on a smaller book, with third-quarter results due 12 November against reaffirmed full-year guidance of $3.0–3.4bn.

Cipher's tenants buy the chips

"We are not a neocloud," Cipher Mining chief executive Tyler Page said on 2 November. "Lenders and investors are placing a premium on predictable, contracted cash flows, and that is exactly what our model is designed to produce." The bitcoin miner turned data-center landlord owns no accelerators; its three contracted campuses carry 700 gross megawatts, $16.6bn of contracted revenue and about $864m of projected average annualized net operating income on a 17.3-year weighted-average lease term, against roughly $6.3bn of enterprise value. Its counterparties are investment-grade or credit-enhanced: Amazon Web Services leased about 300 gross megawatts at Black Pearl for 15 years, and the 168-megawatt Fluidstack agreement at Barber Lake is backstopped by Google. Project bonds priced 7.125%, then 6.125%, then 6.0% across three successive financings. The business itself is barely born: revenue fell 62.8% to $26.7m as mining is decommissioned, gross margin was -172%, and the quarter's $31.6m of GAAP income came from a $127m non-cash warrant revaluation. Rent starts hall by hall from the fourth quarter, and the 20-year Barber Lake tenant is disclosed only as "a leading AI lab".

The equity and the debt moved in opposite directions

All three were marked down hard before any of this reported: Cipher is off 45% over 30 days, Nebius 26% and CoreWeave 24%, with the trigger a delivery-schedule repricing rather than a demand cut — Microsoft's capex came in about $5bn under plan on 28 October because third-party capacity was late. CoreWeave then rose 24.7% on 11 November, its best session since April 2025.

The de-rating is multiple compression, not deteriorating disclosure. CoreWeave has fallen from 16.4 times trailing gross profit in early May to 9.4 in August to 5.1 now, while quarterly gross profit grew from $1.36bn to $2.48bn; at a $33.5bn market capitalization it is priced at about a quarter of its contracted book. The credit market, which has the first claim on those cash flows, repriced CoreWeave the other way. Spreads on ten-year hyperscaler debt have widened about half a percentage point this year against roughly a fifth of that for industrials with no AI exposure, and the widening was worst among the weakest borrowers — which this quarter did not mean the smallest ones.

Amazon told investors its owned and long-leased model carries roughly 35 percentage points of margin advantage over colocation and "neo" competitors, the clearest statement of why renting costs more than building. It signed a 15-year lease at Black Pearl anyway.

Cognex sells machine-vision systems — cameras, barcode readers and the software that interprets what they see — out of factory and warehouse capital budgets, and in the September quarter it sold a record $313.4m of them. Then American voters turned against data centers, and its shares fell 18.3% in two sessions.

Cognex, Ambarella and Ceva are traded as the listed way to own artificial intelligence running at the edge of the network rather than inside a server hall, and the belief inside that grouping is that edge inference is a derivative of the data-center buildout. Their own September-quarter disclosures describe a different relationship. Not one of the three books revenue from a data center being built. All three now pay for one.

A vote about power bills, priced as a chip story

The trigger was explicit. Chip and data-center stocks fell for a second day on 5 November as investors weighed whether Democratic midterm gains would slow construction, CNBC reported, after voters rebuffed data centers in every ballot measure except one and Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez demanded a national construction moratorium. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell about 9.75% that day, its steepest since March 2020, with over $2trn of value gone in the first hour. The S&P 500 tracking fund closed the two sessions down 0.15%. This was the chip complex, not the market.

Cognex's multiple halved while its price did not

Nothing in Cognex's quarter belongs in that selloff. Revenue grew 13.2% as reported and 19% excluding a prior-year one-time benefit from a medical lab automation partnership, the company said, with no meaningful currency effect. Operating margin reached 32.2% against 20.9%, free cash flow rose 21% to $104m, and full-year guidance went up to $1.14bn-$1.16bn with adjusted earnings of $1.68-$1.72 a share. "Q3 was a strong quarter for Cognex, with record revenue, a significant increase in profitability," chief executive Matt Moschner told investors on 5 November. Four of five end markets grew double digits; logistics did so for an eleventh straight quarter; automotive was flat on European weakness, with Europe down 15% while the Americas rose 42%.

Cognex's trailing price-to-earnings has fallen to 36.2x from 78.7x in early May, and its market value is roughly where it was, $8.77bn against $8.94bn. The entire de-rating came from earnings arriving, not from the price falling. Forward earnings of 30.6x rest on consensus of $1.70 a share for this year. Needham cut its target to $70 from $80, Stephens to $70 from $85 and HSBC moved to hold at $65, reading the print as positioning pressure around a quarter that met rather than beat.

Ceva is the only one that shows its invoice

Ceva licenses signal-processing and Bluetooth, Wi-Fi and cellular designs to chipmakers and collects a royalty when their parts ship, months later. Licensees shipped a record 623m devices last quarter for $12.7m of royalties, against 579m and $12.4m a year earlier — about 2.04 cents a unit now versus 2.14 cents then, on the two companies' figures. The mix explains it: Bluetooth shipments rose 9% to 329m while Wi-Fi volume fell 6%.

Revenue grew 2.4% and gross profit fell 0.7%, with margin down to 85.4% from 88.0% on smartphone, personal-computer and television memory pricing. Chief financial officer Yaniv Arieli guided to the opposite on 9 November: "We expect a fourth quarter strong fourth quarter with approximately 24% sequential revenue growth at the midpoint of our guidance. This would represent the highest quarterly revenues in CEVA's history." At 7.4x trailing gross profit it is the cheapest of the three; its trailing earnings multiple is negative and carries no information. Its artificial-intelligence cores, now licensed to more than 50 customers, do not pay a royalty until late this year at the earliest.

Ambarella reports no units at all

Ambarella's computer-vision chips go into driver-assistance systems, security cameras and robots, and edge AI was about 80% of last fiscal year's revenue. It discloses no quarterly shipments or average prices, only an installed base above 46m units and cumulative edge-AI revenue above $1bn. "Our edge AI revenue reached record levels in Q2," chief executive Fermi Wang said on 3 September. Growth has nonetheless slowed for five consecutive quarters, from 31.2% to 13.2%, and the quarter it reports on 24 November is guided to $115m-$124m, about 10.2% growth at the midpoint. The constraint is the same memory that Cognex offsets with price and Ceva absorbs in margin: device makers are receiving half to two-thirds of the memory volumes they order. Operating loss narrowed 63% to $8.1m on a flat 57.7% gross margin, but forward price-to-sales has barely moved, 6.27x against roughly 6.8x in July, because estimates fell with the price.

What the three quarters do and do not explain

Two of the three markdowns are doing rational work. Ambarella is guiding growth down into a loss-making cost base with no disclosed unit meter, and Ceva has just printed its first decline in gross profit. Cognex is the exception, and the reason is its own meter: it is paid by factory, semiconductor and warehouse capital spending, which has nothing to do with whether a data center gets permitted. The case against it is internal, not political — chief financial officer Dennis Fehr guided roughly 93% flow-through of incremental revenue to adjusted earnings this year against 60% indicated for next, logistics faces its hardest comparisons after eleven quarters, and rival Keyence has been growing faster in North America.

By 11 November the instrument and analog names had repaired the damage, Onto Innovation up 4.5% from 3 November and Nevamex up 14.0%, and Ambarella was back where it started. Cognex was still 8.8% lower and Ceva 11.6% lower. Over twelve months Cognex remains up 38.1%.

A moratorium on data-center construction would not remove a dollar of revenue from any of these three. It would, in time, loosen the memory market that is currently rationing Ambarella's customers and compressing Ceva's margin. Vertiv Holdings, which sells the uninterruptible power supplies, busway and liquid cooling installed inside data-center white space under the Liebert, Geist and Powerbar brands, told investors on 28 October that it will stop publishing how many orders it takes. The timing matters because the markdown of the power-equipment shelf has been read as demand deceleration along the electrical path between the utility interconnect and the rack. The companies' own October filings say the opposite about what is already sold. What separates them is where in that path each one gets paid, and for how long its revenue is already contracted.

The line Vertiv's order book left behind

Vertiv counts backlog narrowly. Albertazzi said on the same call that the company is "very strict in what [it] calls backlog" and books only "what is squarely a purchase order" — a capacity agreement does not qualify. The last disclosed figure, $15.0bn, is about one year of the $14,650m of 2026 sales the company now guides to at the midpoint, a raise accompanied by adjusted earnings guidance of $7.15 a share. September-quarter sales of $4,313m were 61% above a year earlier, 55% of that organic, and gross margin edged up to 38.1%.

The harder number is money received. Current deferred revenue stood at $4,120.5m at 30 September against $1,814.7m at 31 December 2025, cash paid at order and at design milestones to hold manufacturing priority. Vertiv's own filings note that orders can be cancelled or rescheduled, which is why the prepayment balance now does more work than the backlog line it effectively replaces.

Paid in 2026 for turbines delivered in 2031

GE Vernova, the gas-turbine and grid-equipment maker spun out in 2023, collects earlier than anyone else in the chain. A customer converts a reservation into a production slot by posting a non-refundable deposit of typically about 20% of the turbine price, years ahead of delivery. That mechanic produced a $5.3bn working-capital benefit inside $5.4bn of third-quarter free cash flow, and total backlog of $197bn, $104bn of it equipment — against revenue guidance of $46.5bn to $47.5bn, roughly 4.2 years of cover by our arithmetic. Power equipment pricing rose more than 20% in the quarter and segment EBITDA margin reached 21.4%.

One gauge in that disclosure points down. The slot-reservation queue fell from 63 GW to 58 GW while firm gas equipment backlog climbed from 53 GW to 64 GW: the funnel ahead of the contracted book thinned as the book filled.

Five months of cover, and a $105m tariff bill

nVent Electric, the smallest of the three at about $18bn of market value, sells enclosures, cabinets and liquid-cooling hardware largely through electrical wholesalers. Its backlog is $2.6bn, under half a year of the $5.79bn of 2026 revenue consensus carries, so costs land in the same quarters as the shipments they burden. Price and productivity more than covered $60m of quarterly inflation, including about $20m of tariffs against a full-year tariff estimate lifted to roughly $105m, but gross margin still fell to 35.84% from 37.45% a year earlier. nVent raised guidance hardest of the three. "At the midpoint, adjusted EPS is expected to grow 60% versus last year," chief financial officer Gary Corona said on 30 October. On that reading the de-rating prices the funnel, and GE Vernova's 58 GW of reservations is the only funnel number any of the three still reports.

Albertazzi told investors on 28 October that "the demand environment remains strong heading into 2027", naming power availability, permitting and skilled construction labor as the pacing items. Having retired the quarterly order print, Vertiv has made its deferred revenue line the thing shareholders must read it by — and has left itself one scheduled chance a year to prove the book is still growing.

America's big wireless carriers raised the fee they charge for delivering a text message repeatedly this year, and that money passes through the books of the companies that sell messaging and voice interfaces to software developers before it leaves again. It inflates revenue, deflates reported gross margin, and touches gross profit not at all. Both listed pure-plays reported on 29 October and both were sold hard afterwards, which raises a narrower question than the share prices suggest: whether the carrier is now taking the increment, or merely routing through it.

The market read the quarters as evidence that the meter is breaking — that volume is growing while the price of a delivered message falls toward the carrier's own cost. The profit lines say the opposite, and the split between the two companies is about what comes next, not about what happened.

What a message costs before anyone earns on it

The fee is set upstream. Across four pricing updates in 2026, six separate US SMS and MMS fee increases took effect: T-Mobile lifted its outbound pass-through for registered business messaging from $0.0030 to $0.0045 per segment in January, a 50% step, and Verizon raised outbound SMS on 1 May and again from $0.0045 to $0.0050 on 1 October. The blended fee went from roughly $0.0033 to about $0.0043 a segment in under a year. The increases were applied across registered campaigns, toll-free and short-code traffic alike, and Verizon's October change also lifted its rich-messaging fee.

Bandwidth's own meter is not the one that slowed

Bandwidth, which runs its own carrier network and sells voice and messaging interfaces plus session-initiation trunking to large enterprises and contact centers, reported revenue of $234.0m for the quarter ended 30 September. Cloud communications revenue — total less surcharges — rose 12% to $158.5m while surcharge revenue rose 51% to $75.6m. On the quarter's $33m of programmable messaging revenue, that is roughly $2.29 of remitted surcharge for every dollar the company keeps, on figures the desk has divided out.

Reported gross margin fell 222 basis points to 36.2%. Underneath it, GAAP gross profit grew 14.9%, an acceleration from 9.0% and 9.6% in the first two quarters; non-GAAP gross profit rose 18% to $96m and non-GAAP gross margin widened nearly 300 basis points to 60.6%, an 86% yield on the growth in cloud communications revenue. Adjusted EBITDA rose 43% to $35m and operating income turned positive at $1.8m. "We're raising the midpoint of our full year 2026 outlook across all of our guidance metrics," chief executive David Morken told investors on 29 October, adding that the company has "growing confidence in achieving double-digit cloud communications revenue growth in fiscal 2027." Bandwidth was also named the first certified bring-your-own-carrier partner for Salesforce's contact-center rollout across more than 25 countries, which management models at roughly a $20m annual run rate by end-2028.

Twilio's fee drag shrank while its expansion rate eased

Twilio, whose roughly 1,800 usage-billed interfaces let developers embed voice, messaging and email in their own applications, carried $53m of incremental US carrier pass-through fees in the quarter, below the $71m it absorbed in the second quarter. Non-GAAP gross margin of 49.2% was down 90 basis points but would have risen 80 without the fees; GAAP gross profit grew 20.3% against revenue growth of 19.9%, and free cash flow reached a record $370m. Full-year organic growth guidance went to 15% from 13–13.5%, and "we expect full year 2027 organic revenue growth of at least 10%, well above our 2025 Investor Day framework of 7% to 8%," chief financial officer Aidan Viggiano said on the same day.

The honest offsets sit in the demand data rather than the margin: dollar-based net expansion eased to 114% from 116%, organic messaging growth slipped to 17% from 18%, and the fourth quarter is guided to 11–12% organic. The AI voice business widely credited with re-pricing the category is barely visible. "The way that we characterize voice AI in our prepared remarks is that it's about 1% of what the voice interactions are so far," chief executive Khozema Shipchandler said, calling mainstream adoption something that "is going to take some time."

What the selling was actually about

Bandwidth has fallen 32.9% in the 30 days to 6 November and Twilio 27.2%, with both companies beating on revenue and earnings. Bandwidth dropped 13% because full-year guidance matched consensus rather than exceeding it, and Twilio fell on its fourth-quarter outlook and margin commentary rather than the quarter. A chunk is beta: on 5 November, the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.5% and Bandwidth lost 10.4%, Twilio 7.2%. Neighbours moved far less — RingCentral down 6.5% over the 30 days, Five9 down 15.9%.

So the quarters account for almost none of the drawdown and the forward guides account for most of it. Bandwidth's own full-year range implies fourth-quarter growth of 17–19%, against 22% in the third, and consensus has 2027 revenue growing 7.7% as the fee increases lap — the reported line deflates precisely because the pass-through stops compounding. One has de-rated past its own history on a 2027 that management says is already largely signed; the other has given back a re-rating it earned three months ago.

The fee increases are the one input neither company controls and both are indifferent to in profit terms. They will lap, the reported growth rate will halve, and the business underneath will look exactly as it does now.

Margin widened; the cash went the other way

GAAP operating income rose 62.4% to $21.3m, a 9.1% margin, and adjusted EBITDA of $43m beat a $30–33m guide. Full-year EBITDA guidance went to $116–119m, up about 53%, and revenue guidance to $873–877m. The base is low: 2025 was Appian's first positive GAAP operating year, at $0.6m on $726.9m of revenue.

Three things complicate the inflection. GAAP gross margin fell 2.9 points to 72.9%, while management described gross margin as 74% on a non-GAAP basis, flat year on year. Professional-services gross margin fell to 24% from 31% as implementations scale. And operating cash flow was minus $15m against positive $19m a year earlier, with cash and investments down to $89m from $187m at the end of 2025; $83m of that decline is cumulative buybacks under a $100m authorization. Management called the cash timing-driven and said October collections were among the strongest months on record.

The January retrial is not the engine

The shares have held an uptrend since 12 August, six sessions after the 6 August second-quarter beat-and-raise. The retrial of Appian's vacated $2.036bn trade-secrets award against Pegasystems opens on 11 January 2027, after the Supreme Court of Virginia threw out the 2022 verdict in January 2026; on 16 October the court let Appian present a reasonable-royalty damages theory it was barred from using at the original trial. Appian then rose 27.2% in a week. But Pegasystems rose 5.4% in the same window and ServiceNow 12.0%, so the likelier reading is a broad software rally that Appian led at roughly twice the move. Short interest of about 3.79m shares, some 5.3% of shares outstanding, is too small to be the cause.

Dearer than it was, cheaper than ServiceNow

Price-to-gross-profit has gone from 3.07x in early May to 6.05x, against gross profit that grew 20.8%: the price has roughly doubled relative to the profit it buys. On forward earnings, Appian's 47.7x is nearly twice ServiceNow's 25.8x.

Measured against forward gross profit the order inverts. Appian's 6.00x sits below ServiceNow's 8.92x, where 20.5% revenue growth came with GAAP operating income down 32.7%, and far below Snowflake's 22.97x, where 35.1% growth comes with a $263.0m quarterly operating loss.

Pegasystems, at 3.91x forward gross profit, is the cheapest of the four, and 3.1% revenue growth with 7% annual contract value growth says why. "Our technology is differentiated and very relevant to the times we are in. However, candidly, our execution this year has not kept up," founder-chief executive Alan Trefler said on 21 October. Salesforce, at 4.92x, grew 10.8%.

What the quarter can and cannot pay for

The operating results account for the direction and the timing of the advance, and for Appian being the one name in its neighborhood that gained in the week its peers gave up ground. They do not account for the full doubling of the valuation, because the acceleration is one quarter old. In the June quarter revenue growth slowed to 19.1%, GAAP gross margin compressed to 71.2%, and unsatisfied performance obligations of $625.3m grew 13%, slower than revenue. The raised fourth-quarter revenue guide of $233–237m implies about 16% growth, partly on a 2–3 point currency headwind, and consensus has 10.8% revenue growth in 2027 while earnings per share still rise 31%. What is being bought at 47.7x forward earnings is margin, and the sell side said so: William Blair upgraded to Outperform while DA Davidson lifted its target to $60 from $42 and TD Cowen to $58 from $50, both keeping their ratings.

Neither company's balance sheet carries the claim against Pegasystems, and nothing changes hands before a verdict. Until a jury meets in January, the invoice has to do the work on its own — and Tanjga, asked whether this quarter's expansion rate becomes the new baseline, declined to guide to the metric at all.
