Hecla Mining produced its silver last quarter for about $6 an ounce after credits for the zinc and lead that come out of the same rock, and the figure fell from the prior quarter because those credits got bigger — $6m bigger. The Coeur d'Alene, Idaho company's shares are down 18% over thirty sessions.

The drawdown runs across all four US-listed silver primaries, and it has been read as the hard-asset trade cracking at the cost line, where high-grade underground mines are supposed to be most exposed. First-half 2026 was the best half any of the four has reported: margins expanding at Hecla and First Majestic, raised guidance at First Majestic, record cash returns at Pan American Silver. What broke was the price of the metal and the cost of holding it. Silver slipped below $60 an ounce on 7 October, a first for 2026, and the 10-year Treasury yielded 5.24% after the Federal Reserve raised rates a quarter point on 16 September in a unanimous vote.

The zinc market sets the silver cost

Hecla does not sell silver. Greens Creek in Alaska and Lucky Friday in Idaho ship polymetallic concentrates to custom smelters, metal traders and third-party processors, and the company reports cash cost and all-in sustaining cost per silver ounce net of by-product credits. The price of zinc is therefore an input to the disclosed price of an ounce of silver. Zinc was $3,805 a tonne on 9 October, recovering from a six-week low as Western smelter capacity shrinks.

The wedge the smelter takes has inverted. Treatment charges on imported zinc concentrate have slumped to minus $50 a tonne, a record low in Fastmarkets data going back a decade, meaning smelters pay miners to secure feed; the 2026 benchmark Korea Zinc agreed with Teck was $85 a tonne, so the benchmark now understates how favorable concentrate terms have become. The $6.07 also excludes Keno Hill in the Yukon, which has not been determined to be in commercial production yet produced roughly 600,000 ounces and nearly $15m of free cash flow in the quarter, its fifth consecutive positive one.

Hecla's revenue fell on price while ounces rose

The metal lines show where the damage came from. Hecla's silver revenue dropped from $295.6m in the first quarter to $213.7m in the second, while zinc and lead together rose to $61.8m. Output went the other way, up 8% to 4.2 million ounces, with Lucky Friday setting a quarterly record on 31% higher milled grades. Gross margin reached 64.9% against 39.3% a year earlier, and the company ended the quarter with $483m of cash and about $472m net of debt, having closed the sale of the Casa Berardi gold mine to Orezone in March. "Hecla enters the third quarter of 2026 from a position of real strength," chief executive Robert Krcmarov said in the August results release.

Pan American is half a gold miner

Pan American, which mines in Canada, Mexico, Peru, Argentina and Bolivia, delivered attributable silver of 6.47 million ounces at the top of guidance and attributable gold of 165.9 thousand ounces below it, on shortfalls at Jacobina, Shahuindo, Timmins and El Peñón. Its silver segment reported $17.80 an ounce of all-in sustaining cost, its gold segment $1,984. Gross margin compressed from 52.7% to 39.3%. "Pan American delivered another quarter of strong financial results, generating $344 million in Attributable free cash flow," chief executive Michael Steinmann said on 11 August. The company repurchased about 4.4 million shares at an average $51.46, some 11% above the 9 October close.

It also guides 2026 taxes paid of $585m to $635m on an assumed $60 silver price, before Mexico's 7.5% duty on mining EBITDA and a 2.0% silver royalty to Royal Gold.

First Majestic, with four Mexican underground mines and Jerritt Canyon in Nevada, is the purest silver exposure: record revenue of $415.5m, up 57%, 60% of it silver, costs below guidance at $25.68 per payable silver-equivalent ounce, and full-year silver guidance raised to 14.6 million ounces, about a tenth above January. Endeavour Silver is the one still spending, with $56.7m of Terronera sustaining capital inside a $91m budget and trailing free cash flow still negative; its revenue is the only one rising sequentially, to $214.0m, as Terronera and the acquired Kolpa mine in Peru add volume faster than price falls.

What the half-year cannot explain

None of it accounts for the fall.

Three of the four were in uptrends in the first days of October and all four crossed into downtrends within seven sessions, Pan American on the 2nd and Hecla on the 9th, then all four rose between 2.6% and 3.7% on 9 October as the metal rebounded. They sit 33% to 46% below January and February peaks, above their July lows, and higher over twelve months.

Two things the results leave open. Forward consensus sits close to annualized first-half run-rate — Hecla at $1.42bn of 2026 revenue, Pan American at 38% earnings growth — but rests on a silver price that has just been breached on the downside. And the scarcity that drove the rally has been called finished: Deutsche Bank's October note projects an 18.5 million ounce surplus in 2027, with London vault stocks up about 70% in a year. "Peak silver scarcity is clearly in the rear-view mirror," the bank's head of metals research, Daniel Ghali, said. On that reading the market's split pricing is a judgment about jurisdiction and balance sheet: Pan American at 13.98x trailing earnings against roughly 26x at its February peak, Hecla still the premium at 34.1x with net cash and US ground.

The credits that make a $6 ounce possible are, in the end, a position in zinc smelting overcapacity. If treatment charges climb back toward the benchmark while the metal stays where it is, the cheapest ounce in the group gets dearer with nothing changing underground.

The day after Brazil's presidential first round, MercadoLibre's shares closed up 9.7% on 1.25m shares against the prior session's 390,000. Nothing in the marketplace had changed. Flávio Bolsonaro had taken 47.03% of valid votes to Lula's 45.16%, beating the polling average and forcing a 25 October runoff, and the session that followed was a repricing of Brazilian fiscal risk: the Bovespa closed at a record 206,911, up about 8%, the dollar fell more than 4% against the real, and Itaú Unibanco's US listing gained 15%.

MercadoLibre, which runs Latin America's largest online marketplace alongside the Mercado Pago payments and lending arm, belongs in that group for a reason its revenue line does not make obvious. Brazil generated $15.2bn of its $28.9bn of 2025 net revenues and financial income, about 52.6%. But the route from a Brazilian election to its earnings runs through a lender's funding cost, not through parcels shipped.

The slab that decides the quarter

Mercado Pago's credit portfolio reached $16.4bn in the June quarter, up 75% year on year, held on balance sheet with a provision against it. Net interest margin after losses was 21%, improved from 18% in the prior quarter, with 15-to-90-day arrears at 7.0% of the book and 4.6% on credit cards; management called those levels close to historical lows. The card book is run at minus 2.5% margin after losses by design, with cohorts reaching breakeven in twelve to eighteen months.

Funding that book is where Brazilian policy reaches the income statement. Copom cut the Selic to 14.00% on 5 August and to 13.75% in September, a fifth consecutive quarter-point reduction and 125 basis points below the 15.00% cycle peak, with minutes still insisting policy stay restrictive. An election outcome read as fiscal discipline is, for this company, a forecast about the cost of money it lends.

The commerce dollar meanwhile keeps getting thinner. Revenue growth accelerated for a fourth straight quarter, to 49.8% in the three months to June, and operating income fell 17.2% to $683m, with gross margin at 40.9% against 45.6% a year earlier as the company subsidises Brazilian shipping to hold volume against Shopee, Amazon, Temu and TikTok Shop.

Sea's bad month was written in Singapore

Sea, the Singapore owner of Shopee, the Monee lending arm and the Garena games business, has no Brazil mechanism, and it fell 12.6% over the same thirty days in which MercadoLibre rose 0.8%. The cause is dated: DBS analyst Sachin Mittal cut the stock to Hold on 1 October and his target to $105 from $148, writing that Shopee is "strengthening its moat, but at a cost to margins." The business disagrees with the shares: revenue grew 48.1% to $7.788bn in the June quarter, gross margin widened to 45.6%, operating income rose 28.4% to $626m, and Monee's $11.1bn loan book held its 90-day non-performing ratio at 1.0%. "With this solid momentum, we are optimistic that Shopee will achieve the milestone of $1 billion in adjusted EBITDA for the full year," chairman and chief executive Forrest Li told investors on 11 August. At 26.1x forward earnings against 35.1x trailing, it is the only one of the three whose multiple assumes profits rise.

Coupang's bounce is a Korean story

Coupang, the Korean retailer that owns the inventory it sells and delivers it on its own Rocket network, rose 14.8% over the same five sessions, off a 52-week low and with no Latin American revenue at all. Its de-rating is domestic: a $409m privacy fine booked in the June quarter took the operating loss to $556m, revenue growth has decelerated to 3.9%, gross profit shrank 2.6%, and Korea's Fair Trade Commission has opened proceedings over an obstructed on-site inspection while seeking a ₩140bn penalty on search rankings. "The vast majority of our customer spend never moved," founder and chief executive Bom Kim told investors on 4 August, putting margin recovery in Product Commerce out to next year. At 2.83x gross profit it is the cheapest of the three, on the only gross profit that is shrinking.

What has repriced is the multiple

None of these operating meters moved in the five sessions that produced the gains. The five-day move is multiple repair on a sovereign forecast rather than evidence that the credit book or the take rate inflected, and the forecast itself is unsettled: the first runoff poll, from Datafolha on 8 October, gave Flávio 52% to Lula's 48%, inside the margin of error, even as JPMorgan and Deutsche Bank upgraded Brazilian equities and XP had the best session in its listed history.

MercadoLibre reports third-quarter results after the close on 4 November, ten days after the runoff. The vintages originated through this year's book growth will not have seasoned by then, which is the uncomfortable part of owning a marketplace that also lends: the election result arrives in weeks, and the loan losses arrive whenever they arrive.

These shares spent the summer rising on the argument that capacity is finally leaving American trucking and that pricing is turning with it; between 23 September and 7 October all four crossed into sustained downtrends, down 25% to 31% over three months, after diesel reached a record $6.31 a gallon in mid-September and J.B. Hunt pre-announced a fuel hit of more than $10m. The June-quarter filings say the pricing argument holds at Werner Enterprises and is close to irrelevant at Heartland and Covenant Logistics, where the quarter turned on what a three-year-old tractor fetches.

Werner is paid by the truck-week, and the truck-week went up

Werner, the Omaha carrier running dry van, temperature-controlled and regional truckload fleets across the US and Mexico plus a brokerage arm, no longer sells much freight at spot. More than 70% of its truckload fleet sits under a dedicated agreement, fixed fleets parked at one shipper's door, about 7,400 trucks; the one-way fleet shrank from nearly 3,300 tractors in 2022 to under 2,400 at the end of 2025.

So the meter that matters is revenue per truck per week, and in the June quarter it rose 5.4% year over year net of fuel surcharge. The smaller one-way book did better still: revenue per total mile up 10.4%, miles per tractor per week up 15.7%. Werner raised dedicated pricing guidance from flat-to-up-3% to up 3-5% and lifted net capital spending guidance to $215m-$250m. "This tightness is being driven by intensifying regulatory pressure," chairman and chief executive Derek Leathers said on the July 28 earnings call, putting the industry in the "third inning" of capacity removal.

The reported line looks like the opposite. Operating income fell 74.5% to $16.9m, because the prior-year quarter carried a $45.7m liability reversal inside insurance and claims after a favorable decision on a 2018 adverse jury verdict; the company's 8-K attributes the decline to $53.6m of favorable reversals in total, including a contingent-consideration settlement. Adjusted operating income rose 67% to $27.6m. Revenue growth of 24% to $933.9m is partly bought: the January purchase of dedicated carrier First Fleet, reported as FirstFleet, added 2,110 trucks to the dedicated average fleet. At $32.70 the shares trade at 9.9x trailing EV/EBITDA, with consensus 2027 earnings of $2.24 implying 14.6x against 29.0x on this year's trough $1.13.

Heartland's quarter arrived on the resale lot

Heartland, a short-haul dry van and temperature-controlled carrier running the Heartland, Millis, Smith Transport and CFI brands on average hauls under 400 miles, is the one pure one-way book here, and the one with no quarterly call and no published pricing or utilisation metrics. What is disclosed: revenue of $184.1m, an operating ratio improved 1,490 basis points, average tractor age down to 2.3 years from 2.6, and $25m of acquisition debt repaid in the first half, taking acquisition debt and finance leases to $135m from $494m in 2022.

The fleet is funding the balance sheet. Forward earnings of $0.147 put the stock at 75.8x, trailing free cash flow is negative at 10.0% of market value, and only book value, at 1.14x, is cheap.

Covenant's profit came through a 49% leasing stake

Covenant, the Chattanooga group reporting in Expedited, Dedicated, Managed Freight and Warehousing segments, earned $8.8m of operating income on $332.9m of revenue, and says the largest drag was Managed Freight gross margin while higher earnings from its leasing investment offset it. That investment, a 49% stake in tractor and trailer lessor Transport Enterprise Leasing, contributed $5.3m pre-tax, or $0.16 a share, up from $4.3m, on higher equipment sale gains. Covenant is reducing its spot exposure too, moving about 15% of the Expedited fleet onto multi-year committed contracts as expedited freight revenue fell 11.4% and dedicated rose 4.3%. Net debt of $289.7m at roughly 2.2x adjusted leverage is the constraint; at 8.3x trailing EV/EBITDA the shares are the cheapest of the four on that measure.

Marten Transport, the refrigerated carrier with $104.0m of cash and no comparable leasing income, made $5.3m on $223.5m of revenue and watched its dedicated operating ratio deteriorate to 96.4% from 92.4%. At 46.6x forward earnings it is the weakest earner on the shelf.

Capacity is leaving on fuel and enforcement

The supply story the group was bought for is real, and freight demand is not what is driving it. September Class 8 net orders came in at 18,700 units, down 9.5%, which ACT Research tied to incomplete 2027 orderboards and uncertainty over the Environmental Protection Agency's 2027 emissions rule. Federal regulators restricted non-domiciled commercial licences from 16 March and made an English-proficiency failure an out-of-service violation. Dry van spot rates including fuel recovered to $3.53 a mile in early October, more than 10% above the late-August low, though much of the year-on-year gain is diesel itself; tender rejections eased to 13.78% on 1 October from 14.6% on 19 September.

The three-month drawdown is therefore a cost shock priced across the complex, with the rails off about 2% over thirty days against the truckload names' 9% to 15%, and the carrier results do not all point the same way underneath it. Werner's contract book repriced upward and its claims accrual improved against an industry where premiums reached about 10.6 cents a mile in 2025, while consensus still assumes a margin recovery the share price has stopped paying for. Heartland and Covenant depend instead on strong used-equipment values, which is the same tightness counted a second way: the enforcement pushing drivers out of service eventually puts their trucks on the lot.

Werner reports on 27 October and Covenant on 29 October. The number that decides the first is dollars per truck per week; at the second, it is how much of the profit again arrives from a business that owns trucks rather than drives them.

Gross profit on each new vehicle would land about 10% below the second quarter's, he said, with parts-and-service growth moderating as customers defer elective work and finance income softer; the company was "having to meet the customer where they are and give a little bit more on price." The next morning every listed franchised car dealer in America opened lower, and AutoNation closed below its prior 52-week low.

The warning was read as a verdict on car demand. What it described was the condition of three profit lines that have quietly become the business: the finance-and-insurance desk, the service drive, and the loan book the dealer now funds itself.

The new car is the small line

Lithia Motors, which runs 467 stores selling new and used vehicles plus financing, warranties, repair and parts under the Lithia and Driveway brands, shows the arithmetic plainly. In its June quarter, new-vehicle sales produced $4,829.2m of revenue and $284.0m of gross profit, a margin of 5.9%. The finance desk booked $366.4m of revenue, and because commissions on loans, warranties and insurance contracts carry essentially no cost of goods, all $366.4m of it was gross profit. Parts and service produced $633.0m, 2.2 times the new-vehicle line.

That shape holds across the industry, and it is moving the wrong way at the front. New-vehicle gross profit per unit at the public groups fell 15.8% year over year to $2,764 in the second quarter, while used slipped only 2.0%. Finance and insurance has been the defended dollar, at $2,569 per vehicle retailed, more than 40% above 2019's $1,798. Service is no longer outrunning inflation: same-store fixed-operations gross profit grew 2.3% against June consumer prices up 3.5%.

Inventory is not the culprit. Cox Automotive put new-vehicle days' supply at 76 in late September, down 1% from a year earlier, with incentives at 6.5% of average transaction price and the full-year sales forecast raised to 16.1 million units.

Three weeks aimed at the back end

Two days before AutoNation spoke, the Federal Trade Commission published price-transparency FAQs for auto dealers: the advertised price must be what any consumer can walk in and pay, excluding only charges government requires, so a $40,000 vehicle carrying a dealer document fee of $85 must be advertised at $40,085. The agency had already sent warning letters to 97 dealership groups in March.

On 16 September the Federal Open Market Committee raised its target range a quarter point to 3.75%-4.00%, the first increase since 2023. Floor-plan borrowing, the credit line that carries unsold inventory, reprices with it: Lithia's floor-plan interest expense rose $14.7m in the June quarter, while Penske's same-store equivalent fell 9.8%.

Then on 5 October Morgan Stanley's Daniela Haigian cut AutoNation to Equal-weight with a $175 target, from $250, and Group 1 Automotive to Underweight, trimming implied multiples across the franchise dealers by 1.2 turns. Her argument was the back end again: fee disclosure plus AI shopping agents that make cross-retailer price comparison frictionless, with Carvana scored as the best-positioned name in auto retail after a record quarter of roughly 197,000 retail units.

From 9 September to 9 October the six franchise groups fell an equal-weighted 19%, AutoNation worst at 24% and Penske least at 10%. Four of the six now sit in established downtrends; only Lithia and Penske still hold mild uptrends.

One guide-down, one record quarter

AutoNation fell on its own numbers. Revenue has declined year over year for three straight quarters, operating margin has gone from 7.50% in 2022 to 4.74% in 2025, and 2025 net income fell 6.2% while earnings per share rose, because diluted shares have dropped from 75.0m in 2021 to 33.8m. Its captive lender, at a $2.7bn portfolio, earned $20.1m in the first half against a $39.6m provision for credit losses. At 7.4x forward earnings against 7.1x trailing, the multiple prices a flat-to-down year.

Lithia's quarter went the other way. Revenue rose 2.2% to $9.8bn, new-vehicle gross per unit held at $2,718 for a third consecutive quarter, used retail gross per unit gained $339 sequentially, and Driveway Finance originated a record $884m. "Financing Operations delivered 80% income growth, with expanding margins and increasing penetration," chief executive Bryan DeBoer told investors on 29 July. The cost of that engine sits two filings down: in the March quarter, $21.0m of the credit provision was net charge-offs against $5.4m from portfolio growth, and funding the loan book leaves trailing free cash flow at minus 7.9% of market value even on a 9.6x trailing multiple. Forward, Lithia trades at 8.1x.

Penske held up because a quarter of it is not a car dealership. Premium brands were 71% of retail automotive franchised revenue in the first half, and equity earnings of $57.4m from Penske Transportation Solutions exceeded the entire commercial-truck segment's $47.2m of pretax income and equalled 22% of group net income. At 14.7x forward against 14.4x trailing, Penske carries the highest multiple of the three and the flattest consensus earnings path.

What the quarters have to settle

The selloff is a repricing of the fee, finance and service dollar, and only one of the three protagonists has reported deterioration in it. AutoNation guided its own front end down and has the contracting revenue line to match. Lithia's per-unit economics stopped falling two quarters ago, which leaves its 20% monthly decline resting on what a regulator's guidance and a downgrade say about lines that are still earning. Penske's defense is brand mix and a freight stake, neither of which a car buyer explains.

Lithia reports on 21 October, AutoNation on 22 October and Penske on 28 October. Three figures in those releases do the work the revenue line cannot: gross profit per unit by line, the captive lenders' charge-offs, and what the service bays grew in a quarter when customers are being told to defer.

A dealer can live with a thin margin on the metal. What it has never had to price is a customer who sees the whole number before walking through the door.

A supplier whose distributors are down to 25 days of stock, with orders running above shipments, has spent the last three months being priced as though the destocking were still ahead of it. Microchip Technology, which sells microcontrollers and analog parts to automotive, industrial and computing customers through authorized distributors, told investors in August that the correction in its sales channel was finished. The $38.5m of idle plant sitting inside the margin

An analog maker that owns its fabs reports under-loaded capacity inside cost of goods sold, so the margin recovers as the plant fills. June-quarter non-GAAP gross margin of 63.8% still carried $38.5m of capacity under-utilisation charges, worth about 260 basis points, down roughly $8m from the prior quarter and guided down by a similar amount again. Priced under both comparisons on gross profit

Trailing earnings are useless here. Revenue fell 42% in a single year, from $7.63bn in fiscal 2024 to $4.40bn in fiscal 2025, so the 104.7x trailing price-to-earnings ratio is struck on trough profits. The yield shock all six of them shared

Nothing in Microchip's reported results accounts for its de-rating. A channel that has stopped shrinking is not yet a channel that has started ordering. That is the distinction the next print has to settle, and it is the one thing neither the margin arithmetic nor the bond market can settle for it.

What a reserved slot is worth before anyone orders anything

A gas-turbine dollar arrives in three separable forms. Prudential Financial announced on 5 October that Prismic Life Reinsurance, the Bermuda company it launched with Warburg Pincus in 2023 and took a 20% interest in, will reinsure about $5bn of reserves backing dollar-denominated Japanese whole-life policies written by its Japanese affiliates. That takes the liabilities Prismic has agreed to carry for Prudential past $22bn. Four days earlier, Manulife closed the cession of biometric risk on C$3.2bn of John Hancock long-term-care reserves to Munich American Reassurance.

The June quarter at these companies was read as the higher-yield story finally arriving: assets bought near 5% standing behind promises priced years ago. The disclosures describe something else doing much of the work. Liabilities are leaving the ceding company's books, the capital behind them is released and spent on shares, and adjusted earnings are growing several times faster than the equity they are earned on.

Twenty percent on earnings, three percent on the book

MetLife, which sells group life, dental and disability cover to employers alongside annuities, funding agreements and pension-risk-transfer buyouts, reported adjusted earnings up 15% to $1.6bn and adjusted earnings per share up 20% to $2.43, with adjusted return on equity of 17% for a second consecutive quarter, the top of its target range. Adjusted book value per share, the measure that strips out the mark on its bond portfolio, rose 3% to $57.71. Part of that gap is retired shares; part is the hedging and derivative marks that adjusted earnings exclude and book value absorbs.

Behind the buybacks sits Chariot Re, the Bermuda vehicle MetLife started with General Atlantic, whose inaugural $10bn transaction took on structured settlement annuities and group annuity contracts from pension risk transfers MetLife had originated, and which MetLife expected to raise available capital by roughly $1bn. At 30 June the arrangement carried an $11.1bn funds-withheld liability and a $10.9bn receivable. On 5 August the board authorized $3bn of fresh repurchases.

The spread dollar itself wobbled

The owned-asset half of the model had a poor quarter. Variable investment income, the private-equity and real-estate fund line reported on a one-quarter lag, came in at $231m against the roughly $400m a quarter implied by full-year guidance of about $1.6bn. "Total investment spread was 97 basis points in the second quarter, below our guidance range of 100 to 120 basis points, driven by weaker private equity returns within VII," chief financial officer John McCallion said on the second-quarter call.

That did not persist. Around 24 September MetLife pre-announced preliminary third-quarter variable investment income of at least $600m ahead of its 4 November release. The origination end was the softer one: US pension-risk-transfer buyout and buy-in sales totalled about $3bn in the second quarter, down 26% from a year earlier, on 176 contracts with no single deal above $250m.

Prudential's fee half has not cured its discount

Prudential's asset manager PGIM earned $294m of adjusted operating income against $229m a year earlier on $1.491 trillion of assets, with $4.6bn of third-party net inflows offset by $3.0bn of affiliated outflows. Group after-tax adjusted operating income reached $4.08 a share, up 14%, while adjusted book value per share grew 4.7% to $100.91.

Prudential is the cheapest of the three at 7.8x forward earnings against 10.1x trailing, roughly 1.1x that adjusted book. MetLife, on the same disclosed figures, trades near 1.7x. The likelier cause is the forward path rather than the printed quarter: consensus has Prudential earning $14.45 a share this year, a shade below last year's $14.50, and $14.91 next, while MetLife's is set to rise about 17% to $10.15.

Manulife's cession is the defensive version of the same trade. Core earnings rose 12% to C$1.923bn with Asia annualized premium equivalent sales up 21%, and the Munich Re deal was largely capital-neutral, worth about C$30m a year, while cutting cumulative long-term-care morbidity sensitivity by 24%. Its last annual actuarial assumption review was favourable, reducing pre-tax fulfilment cash flows by $605m; the next one is implemented in third-quarter results. The shares trade at 9.5x forward against 16.3x trailing.

What the twelve months do and do not explain

Over twelve months to 9 October, Manulife gained 33%, MetLife 20% and Prudential 10%, with Prudential down 4% over the trailing thirty days and its trend softening a notch on 17 September, Manulife's on 2 October.

The operating record covers the gains. It does not cover Prudential's discount, which tracks a flat earnings path and an unsettled question about what stands behind ceded reserves. US life insurers ceded $2.4 trillion of reserves in 2024, more than $1.1 trillion of it offshore, and state regulators are exploring capital charges for recapture risk and for weaker reinsurers' credit; Cayman-based reinsurers were contesting the proposals as of 7 October. The fee income meant to replace spread is still tiny: MetLife's newly separated investment-management segment earned $57m in the quarter, against $1.6bn for the group.

The capital freed offshore has already been converted into retired shares. The reserves it was holding sit with reinsurers whose required capital is still being argued over in front of the people who write the rules, and each of these three now reports its earnings on a balance sheet a little smaller than the business it sold.
