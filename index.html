SpaceX agreed on 9 October to buy a nationwide portfolio of 800 MHz licences from the investment firm Grain Management, and the market promptly paid three landlords for a customer that has signed nothing. Crown Castle, which leases vertical space on more than 40,000 US cellular towers to wireless carriers, closed the session up 16.8% on 14.17m shares against roughly 3m on typical early-October days. American Tower rose 10.4% and SBA Communications 9.1%.

The same session took T-Mobile US down 12.3%, AT&T down 9.7% and Verizon down 9.2%. The physics explains the split. The 800 MHz band is low-band coverage that works through walls and ceilings, which satellites cannot do, so it is hard to use from orbit and easy to use from a tower. One headline therefore read as a prospective fourth tenant for the steel and a fourth national competitor for the carriers. The belief being bought is that a new network means new leases on existing towers.

The buildout SpaceX has actually described

The deal was valued at about $8bn by the Wall Street Journal, covers up to 14 MHz of paired licences, and needs approval from the Federal Communications Commission; the parties did not disclose terms. Grain bought the portfolio from T-Mobile in August 2026, so the spectrum arming a possible fourth carrier was sold by the carrier that fell hardest on the news.

SpaceX's own stated architecture is the problem with the rally's logic. The company has described housing small cellular radios alongside its existing Starlink broadband dishes on a limited number of host rooftops rather than building new macro towers. President Gwynne Shotwell has told investors the EchoStar spectrum "does have terrestrial components, so we definitely intend to build out the terrestrial component." Intent to transmit from the ground is not intent to rent a lattice. As Wireless Estimator put it the same day, "investors are betting on a new tower tenant that hasn't signed a lease yet."

The rent already signed is the rent in bankruptcy court

A tower dollar is earned under long-dated master lease agreements whose escalators run near 3% a year whatever traffic does, and it is lost through contractual churn. Crown Castle's second-quarter organic contribution to site rental billings was $38m, growth of 3.9%, but only "excluding an unfavorable $49 million and $5 million impact from DISH Terminations and Sprint Cancellations, respectively". The escalator delivered its 3-4%; one defaulted tenant took back more than it gave. American Tower shows the same gap: 1.9% reported organic tenant billings growth, about 4% before DISH.

That loss is a forward event, not a post-mortem. DISH declared its leases excused after the FCC forced parent EchoStar to sell spectrum to AT&T and SpaceX, and the three towercos are pressing roughly $7.6bn of disputed claims, Crown Castle alone about $3.5bn, in a case whose plan confirmation now slips to no earlier than 2 December. The Brattle Group put DISH at 5.1% to 7.0% of annual rental revenues across all three. The regulator that armed the hypothetical tenant is the defence for cancelling the real one's rent.

Three landlords, three different books

Crown Castle's reported revenue fell 4.9% year on year in the second quarter to $1.008bn, mostly the fiber and small-cell businesses sold in May. Its AFFO of $1.13 a share rose 10%, but the company said the increase came "primarily as a result of a decrease in interest expense and an increase in interest income" from the proceeds of that sale. It raised full-year AFFO guidance by $5m to $4.59 a share at the midpoint, inside a $4.53-$4.65 range, and ended the quarter at 6.3x net leverage. The cost side is a ground lease under every site: about 57% of its ground is leased, and the buyout programme plus efficiencies is expected to cut $15m of annual cost.

American Tower grew revenue 4.7% to $2.749bn and has a second meter inside the ticker. CoreSite, its US data-centre arm, had its full-year revenue growth guidance lifted to roughly 15% from 13%, against roughly 4% for the global tower book; European escalators are uncapped and indexed to inflation rather than fixed. SBA Communications is the shrinking domestic control: site-leasing revenue there fell 3.7% to $452.5m on Sprint and EchoStar consolidation, international rose 30.5%, and AFFO per share fell 3.8% to $3.03. Barclays downgraded American Tower and Crown Castle to Equal Weight on collection risk and kept SBA at Overweight for having less of it.

What the single session actually repriced

The business record does not account for Crown Castle's move. Its shares closed 9 October at $79.64 against $79.66 ninety days earlier and $96.62 a year before, and have traded below their moving-average trend every day since 17 August, including the day of the jump. After the pop it carries the highest trailing EV/EBITDA of the three at 21.0x, against American Tower's 18.3x and SBA's 17.0x, on the weakest organic growth and the most leverage. American Tower's +8.1% over three months has a decelerating-but-growing book and an accelerating data-centre segment behind it; its 26.1x forward earnings sits below the 28.7x on the Desk's May record. SBA is the cheapest of the three and the only one whose domestic revenue is outright contracting. A 5.24% ten-year Treasury yield is the discount rate under all of them, but it cannot lift one name 16.8% while its customers fall by a tenth.

At the Goldman Sachs Communacopia conference on 9 September, chief executive Chris Hillabrant said "the larger opportunity depends on how quickly carriers move on new spectrum." On 9 October the market decided a company that owns no towers and runs no network had answered him.

Affirm Holdings, the point-of-sale instalment lender that lets shoppers in the US, UK and Canada spread a purchase over terms from one month to forty-eight, took in more money last fiscal year from interest on loans sitting on its own balance sheet than from the merchants who pay it to offer interest-free credit. Interest income was $2.05bn of $4.26bn of fiscal-2026 revenue; merchant network revenue, up 30%, was $1.15bn.

That matters because buy-now-pay-later shares are traded as a read on the shopper's health, and revenue is treated as the read. It cannot be. Four unlike dollars land on one transaction, and only one of them is priced off the shopper's credit at all.

Four unlike dollars, one purchase

The merchant discount rate is charged on interest-free volume, where the retailer and not the buyer pays for the credit: Pay-in-X was 16% of fiscal-2026 volume and 0% APR instalment loans another 14%. Consumer interest income comes off the longer interest-bearing loans Affirm keeps, which is why that line is roughly half of revenue. A third dollar, gain on sales of loans, was $596.6m — a credit decision recognised up front as a capital-markets spread when the loan is sold into a forward-flow agreement. The fourth is card interchange, $294.0m, from the Affirm Card, which is attached to 19% of active customers. "We need to make sure that the Affirm Card isn't just the best card in the world," founder and chief executive Max Levchin told investors on 27 August. "It's also the best way to use Affirm."

Stack those and revenue can grow in a quarter when the economics decay.

Where the credit charge actually lands

The honest meter is revenue less transaction costs measured against volume, because funding cost, the provision for credit losses and the subsidised share of volume all land there. It was $589m in the June quarter, 4.2% of gross merchandise volume, and $812m or 5.8% before the credit provision — the same disclosure read with and without the credit charge. Across the full year the measure rose to $2.08bn on $50.2bn of volume, about 4.14%, against roughly 4.03% a year earlier: a widening unit margin on volume up 37%.

Affirm has to borrow before it can lend, and the borrowing got cheaper. Aggregate funding debt of $9.8bn splits into $5.3bn of securitisation notes, $3.3bn of warehouse facilities and about $1.1bn of convertibles, and the average annualised funding cost in the June quarter fell 103 basis points to 5.8%. Thirty-day delinquency on monthly instalment loans improved to 2.5% from the 2.7%-2.8% range of the prior three quarters, with an allowance of $563m against $9.56bn of gross loans held for investment. Management guided fiscal-2027 revenue less transaction costs to 4.16% of volume, above its own prior 3.25%-4.0% range, crediting favourable debt capital markets. "We are in control of the credit reality," Levchin said on the same call. "You would see us slow down growth before you would see us have a real credit disturbance."

A re-rating with no filing behind it

None of that is what moved the shares. Affirm closed at $77.76 the session after the report, slid 12% to $68.15 by 9 September, then rose 18% to $80.70 by 9 October. Price to gross profit — the fitting measure, since a roughly $1.5bn deferred-tax release leaves a trailing price-to-earnings ratio of 14.1x against 40.5x forward — went from 8.4x trailing in mid-September to 9.5x on 9 October, 7.2x forward, entirely on price. The likelier reading is the cost of money: implied odds of an October Fed hike collapsed from near 69% to 16.5% in the first week of the month after New York Fed president John Williams cooled the talk, and a lender with $9.8bn of funding debt prices the policy path straight into its cost of goods sold. A DBS Bank upgrade on 1 October helped; TD Cowen cut its target to $110 from $124 on 6 October while keeping a buy rating.

The bank and the remitter

Klarna Group, the London-headquartered payments provider that also runs a digital retail bank, carries the same interest-free receivable from the opposite side of the balance sheet: about 90% of its book is funded by consumer deposits, roughly $11.7bn to $13bn of them at 30 June, with a capital ratio rather than a counterparty's appetite as the constraint. Its credit line is improving — provisions fell to 0.52% of volume, a third consecutive decline — and its shares are down 66% over twelve months, at 1.3x trailing sales, the lowest since the September 2025 listing and 2.1x trailing gross profit. The cause sits above credit: full-year volume guidance cut to $149bn-$151bn from more than $155bn on German retail weakness and some $600m of adverse currency, consensus growth of 17.9% for 2026 against 31.6% delivered in 2025, and a chief financial officer and chief marketing officer both departing in early 2027.

Remitly Global, the Seattle digital remittance business serving immigrants sending money to nearly 150 countries, is the contrast that defines the group: no loan book, a fraud loss line instead of a credit one, and a take rate of roughly 2.11% of send volume in the June quarter against about 2.23% a year earlier on our arithmetic — a compressing spread on volume up 27%, trading at 16.6x trailing and 17.3x forward earnings.

So two lenders' disclosed loss rates improved while the sector narrative worsened: LendingTree's 2025 survey found 41% of users had made a late payment, up from 34%. The companies' own books and the industry's survey data point opposite ways, and nothing filed closes the gap. What the August numbers establish is that Affirm's spread widened on cheaper funding with losses flat; what they do not establish is the October advance, which arrived on a rate expectation.

Affirm reports after the close on 5 November, covering the first quarter it has funded since the Fed began raising its policy rate again. Whatever that costs shows up in the one line its revenue cannot show.

In the first week of October the two lenders whose decisions matter most to Fair Isaac's mortgage royalty went opposite ways. Rocket Mortgage said it will make VantageScore 4.0 its preferred credit-scoring model on all eligible loans from the fourth quarter, after about four months of testing found more clients qualified and scoring costs fell. Two days later United Wholesale Mortgage, which lends through brokers, reaffirmed that it will keep using FICO Scores on every credit pull.

That reaffirmation is narrower than it reads. United Wholesale will now pull both scores and automatically use the strongest qualifying result at loan level. Fair Isaac, which licenses the FICO credit score and sells decisioning software to lenders, still collects its fee on the inquiry; what it no longer has is the certainty that its number is the one the loan is priced off. That distinction is the argument left after the 29% single-session fall on 29 September, which followed the housing regulator's move to a single pricing grid treating loans made with either score equally at Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac.

The quarter nobody is arguing with

There is no damage in the reported numbers. June-quarter Scores revenue was $458.9m against $324.3m a year earlier, the business-to-business half up 49% "primarily attributable to a higher mortgage origination scores unit price." Mortgage-origination score volumes rose low single digits while revenue from them climbed 97%. Group revenue grew 25.7% and operating margin reached 53.8% from 48.9%.

That is the arithmetic of a published price list: $0.60 rising to $4.95 over five years, then more than doubling to $10 a score for 2026, passed through by the credit bureaus and resellers who face the lender. Mortgage originations were 71% of business-to-business Scores revenue. The price is set by Fair Isaac; the units are set by borrowers.

Both legs of the toll now point down

TransUnion has extended standalone mortgage pricing on the rival score at 99 cents through December 2028 and bundles it free with a FICO purchase; more than 1,100 mortgage lenders used it between January and September. Fair Isaac's answer has been to restructure the toll rather than hold the headline price: its direct-licence program for resellers offers a $4.95 royalty per score plus a $33 fee per borrower when a FICO-scored loan funds, or the $10 per-score rate unchanged.

The base the royalty multiplies is shrinking too. The Mortgage Bankers Association now puts 2027 originations at $2.101trn, below the $2.123trn expected for 2026, with the 30-year fixed rate at 7.49%.

Chief executive Will Lansing was blunt about the regime on the 29 July call: "The rules of the road, the environment that we're operating in is a gaming environment. The FHFA has put it in place. The GSEs have accepted it. And so that's the world we have."

What $1.96bn of buyback cannot settle

Management spent $1.96bn repurchasing stock into the decline, and diluted shares fell from 23.958m to 22.703m in two quarters, a reduction of roughly 5% by that arithmetic and a real slice of per-share growth. The shares have recovered 11.4% from their $599.51 low and remain 63.8% below the 52-week high. Brokers including Wells Fargo and Goldman Sachs cut price targets after 29 September without cutting their ratings.

So the results account for none of the fall and the regulator accounts for most of it. At 19.2x trailing earnings against roughly 62x in January the compression is real, but the 13.4x forward figure rests on a fiscal 2027 consensus of $49.95 a share assembled before the pricing grid existed. Cheapness measured against an anchor Washington has invalidated is not cheapness.

Fair Isaac's fiscal year closed on 30 September. The next report is the first that can publish a 2027 price list and show how many scoring units stayed after Rocket's switch, and until it does, a franchise that has always been priced on what lenders had to buy is being valued on what they now choose to.

On 30 March 2026, Sysco, the Houston distributor that supplies restaurants, hospitals and schools in the US, Canada, the UK and France, agreed to pay about $29.1bn to the owners of Jetro Restaurant Depot, 167 large-format cash-and-carry warehouses in 35 states serving more than 725,000 independent restaurants and foodservice operators. Sysco shares sank 12% that day. What it agreed to buy is the only customer type in this industry where the mark-up on a case is worth having.

Broadline distributors get read through revenue, as though the top line tallied restaurant demand. It mostly tallies product cost, passed through to the operator. US Department of Agriculture forecasters expect beef and veal prices up 9.4% in 2026; those dollars inflate a distributor's sales line and carry no profit with them unless the mark-up rises too. The meters that work are gross profit per delivered case, the operating cost of delivering it, and the split between volume-guaranteed chain contracts and independent kitchens won one account at a time.

Where $8.64 a case comes from

US Foods, which runs roughly 70 broadline depots and 80 CHEF'STORE cash-and-carry sites out of Rosemont, Illinois, discloses the meter directly. In the quarter to 27 June 2026 its adjusted gross profit per case rose 5.0% to $8.64 while adjusted operating expense per case rose 3.7% to $5.93, leaving adjusted EBITDA per case up 8.3% to $2.73. Revenue grew 4.5% to $10.53bn; gross profit grew 8.0%.

The mix did that work. Total cases rose only 1.9%, independent restaurant cases 5.1% and chain cases fell 1.5%. The independent figure was the fifth consecutive quarter of acceleration and the strongest since the fourth quarter of 2023, achieved against falling industry foot traffic; chief executive Dave Flitman attributed it to "healthy new account growth and improved penetration with existing customers." Private-label penetration inside the independent book reached 53%. Not all of the per-case gain is structural: the company credits a $19m favorable LIFO adjustment, vendor-management timing and higher customer fuel surcharges as well.

Sysco's answer to 1.7%

Sysco's equivalent meter is local case volume, up 1.7% for fiscal 2026 and 2.6% in the fourth quarter. "In our USFS segment, we grew local cases 0.5% in the first half of the year, and we grew local cases 2.9% in the second half of the year," chief executive Kevin Hourican told investors on 4 August 2026 — real improvement, and still behind both rivals. Group revenue says nothing useful about it: International sales rose 7.6% but only 4.1% in constant currency, and SYGMA, the chain-logistics arm, turned $2.2bn of sales into $30m of operating income. Full-year revenue rose 3.9% to $84.55bn while net income fell 3.9%.

Restaurant Depot is reported to earn roughly 13% EBITDA margins against Sysco's 5.2% on a format with no truck, no route and no salesperson. The bill is $21.6bn of cash and 91.5m new shares, about 19% of the 480.6m diluted count, with pro forma net debt of $32.3bn, or 4.89x adjusted EBITDA. Moody's cut the senior unsecured rating to Baa2. The Independent Restaurant Coalition has asked the Federal Trade Commission to block the deal on the argument that Restaurant Depot is the one wholesale alternative letting independents avoid minimum orders and delivery fees, and Representatives Jerrold Nadler and Maxwell Frost have demanded an investigation.

The 11.9% that is cigarettes

Performance Food Group has the largest sales line of the three and the least informative. Foodservice was $36.56bn of $67.58bn in segment sales, Convenience $25.97bn; consolidated gross margin of 11.9%, against roughly 18% at both rivals, is the arithmetic of cigarettes and excise taxes crossing the top line. Convenience supplied 38% of revenue and 21% of fourth-quarter segment adjusted EBITDA. The foodservice arm, meanwhile, posted "nearly 6% independent case growth," chief executive Scott McPherson said on the August call — the best of the three — and fiscal 2027 adjusted EBITDA is guided to $2.125–2.225bn, 12.7% growth at the midpoint.

Three declines, two of them unexplained

All three are lower over three months: US Foods 2.1%, Sysco 4.7%, Performance Food Group 15.3%. The sector reason on offer arrived on 29 September, when JPMorgan trimmed its US Foods target to $98 from $108 and adjusted estimates across the group, citing a narrowing gap between restaurant and grocery prices and rising diesel. The gap is real — USDA now forecasts food away from home up 3.5% and groceries up 2.4% in 2026 — but it is a forecast about menu pricing, not evidence about mark-up per case, which rose.

On price against gross profit, the comparable measure when reported margins differ by more than six points, Performance Food Group is the cheapest of the three at 1.84x trailing, Sysco sits at 2.39x and US Foods at 3.05x. Two of those discounts are unexplained by the per-case record; one is not. Sysco's 15.4x forward earnings is computed before the dilution and the debt land, so the apparent cheapness is a measurement artifact rather than a judgment. US Foods' 20.4x forward against 29.5x trailing simply reflects its own guided 18–24% adjusted earnings-per-share growth.

The FTC blocked Sysco's attempt to buy US Foods in 2015. It is now being asked to block Sysco from buying the warehouses independent operators use to get around distributors like Sysco, with management expecting to close in early 2027. Whichever way that goes, the question it settles is narrow and expensive: whether the richest case in American foodservice can be bought in bulk, or has to be sold.

Vicor raised the revenue guidance for a single quarter twice inside nine days, and neither raise came from a module leaving a loading dock. The Andover, Massachusetts maker of brick-format DC-DC converters and custom power systems told investors on 30 September that third-quarter revenue would grow more than 30% sequentially, up from the "more than 20%" it had promised on 21 September, which in turn replaced nearly 10%. The market has treated the sequence as proof that Vicor's power-delivery hardware is winning sockets in artificial-intelligence racks. The company's own releases say something narrower: it is being paid for permission. The agreement behind both raises, signed on 16 September, licenses Vertical Power Delivery — the topology that pushes current up into the processor from below — to an unnamed artificial-intelligence equipment maker on a non-exclusive basis, and expressly permits the licensee to buy the covered modules from third-party suppliers Vicor does not supply.

The royalty is scheduled, the leverage is a docket

Royalties were $30.4m, or 21.2%, of June-quarter revenue, the company disclosed on 21 July, of which an earlier agreement contributed $15m, with $5m set for the third quarter and $10m in each of the four quarters after that. That is a payment calendar rather than an order book, and because royalties carry essentially no cost of sales they drop almost whole into operating income. Net income of $49.8m exceeded operating income of $34.9m in the June quarter, so roughly 30% of the bottom line was earned below the operating line.

What creates the calendar is enforcement. In February 2025 the U.S. International Trade Commission upheld two Vicor patents and barred importation of unlicensed computing systems containing infringing power modules, with cease-and-desist orders against Delta Electronics and Quanta Computer entities. "Since the first patent to VPD was only recently asserted, it is still possible to secure a license at an early stage of escalation with a low royalty rate," chief executive Patrizio Vinciarelli said in the 21 September release.

The docket also runs the other way. The same determination found two Foxconn affiliates licensed to one patent through boilerplate in purchase orders for Vicor components, a ruling Vicor is appealing, and a second investigation naming Luxshare, Hon Hai, Delta, Quanta, Monolithic Power, Infineon, Flex and Celestica carries a target completion date of 20 September 2027.

The hardware is not the problem; the price is the question

Product revenue of $112.9m grew 31.8% year on year, book-to-bill ran above one and one-year backlog rose 26% sequentially to $379.7m. Reported gross margin nonetheless fell from 65.3% to 58.0%, consistent with a larger royalty share a year earlier rather than with product-margin decay.

Shares rose 68.0% from $183.91 on 16 September to $308.90 on 2 October, then gave back 13.9% to $266.07 on 9 October alongside the 7 October selloff in artificial-intelligence names and 24-year-high long Treasury yields. At 83.4x trailing earnings the stock is dearer than almost anything in power components, yet cheaper on gross profit than it was in May 2026 at a similar price: 44.9x against 61.8x. Earnings grew into the multiple. A third of the forward growth consensus embeds is contracted royalty income.

The contrast on the same Nvidia 800-volt supplier list is what makes the licence remarkable. Navitas, a fabless designer of gallium-nitride and silicon-carbide power chips, reported $10.5m of revenue for the June quarter, down 27.3%, with cumulative negative gross profit across four quarters and 77.0x trailing sales to carry; its in-rack 800-volt content is dated to 2027 and later. Vicor is the only one of the three whose third-quarter number is already half-written, and it was written by lawyers. The print lands at 7:00am Eastern on 20 October, and the line investors will read first is not shipments.

Grand Canyon Education does not own a university. It sells admissions and financial-aid counselling, marketing, learning technology and back-office finance to higher-education institutions, chiefly Grand Canyon University, the Phoenix nonprofit it sold itself out of in 2018, and it is paid a contractual percentage of what that university bills. On 29 July 2026 the two sides rewrote the contract. The amended and restated agreement re-bases the fee to 60% of GCU's tuition and academic-related fees alone, hands the revenue from student housing, food service, athletic tickets, the arena, hotel, golf course and apparel operations wholly back to the university, runs an initial fifteen-year term to 30 June 2041, and deletes GCU's right to terminate for convenience.

Management put the cost of the re-basing at about $20m of annual service revenue and less than $1m a quarter of operating income, because the ancillary revenue surrendered carried almost all of its own cost. That trade is the whole argument about this corner of the market. The weakness in education shares has been read as the demographic cliff arriving early; what the disclosures describe is a company whose revenue is one counterparty's billed tuition times a percentage it cannot reset, sitting beside a company whose largest payer is the Department of Defense.

A fee that can only grow on bodies

GCU has extended its tuition freeze for an 18th consecutive year. The counterparty that sets the base of the fee has chosen not to raise price, which leaves volume as the only lever. Second-quarter service revenue of $264.0m was up 6.7% on enrollments up 7.5%, to 121,921 students, of whom 113,011 study online. Divide the one by the other and the fee earned per enrolled student was about $2,166 against $2,182 a year earlier — a derived figure, and a falling one.

The margin came from cost discipline. Marketing and communication ran at 22.7% of revenue against 22.6%, counselling services at 33.4% against 33.5%, and operating income rose 12.3% to $58.2m, lifting the operating margin to 22.0% from 20.9%.

The smaller hybrid-campus business, which supports healthcare programmes for university partners, is capped by permits rather than appetite. Chief executive Brian Mueller's team told investors on 30 July that enrollment grew 8.5% across 47 active locations to nearly 6,000 students, that 14 locations are at or near capacity and need further state approvals to expand, and that 22 locations will show no year-on-year growth in new autumn enrollments because eight of them already started the maximum number of students their states authorised a year earlier.

Kaplan is paid the same way

Graham Holdings is filed as a conglomerate, with television stations, car dealerships, burners and lumber, and its education arm is usually treated as a sideline. It is not one: Kaplan's $50.3m of second-quarter operating income was 60% of the group's $83.6m, on education revenue that was only 32% of the top line. And Kaplan collects on the identical architecture — a 30-year support agreement under which it takes a fee of 12.5% of Purdue University Global's revenue, 13% for the years to 30 June 2027. Graham's education revenue fell 4% last quarter while its education operating income rose 9%.

American Public Education's payer is an appropriation

American Public Education owns its institutions outright and therefore keeps every obligation the fee collectors contracted away. In 2025, students using Pentagon tuition assistance as their primary payment source accounted for 41% of American Public University System's revenue, veterans' benefits 26% and Title IV federal student aid 19%. Since the American Rescue Plan Act, military and veterans' money counts on the federal side of the 90/10 rule, and APUS's ratio stood at 89% — one point from the limit, with the company stating it cannot assure future compliance.

The operating news was good. Second-quarter revenue rose 5.5% to $171.7m, gross margin widened to 55.4% from 51.8%, operating income nearly doubled to $13.5m, and full-year guidance was raised to revenue of $690m–$698m and diluted earnings of $2.48–$2.79. On 4 August the company folded Rasmussen University and Hondros College of Nursing into APUS, lifting the last regulatory restriction on Rasmussen's total enrollment. "The foundation is built, the business is simplified, the balance sheet is strong, and now the institutional combination is complete," chief executive Angela Selden told investors on 10 August.

The quarter after it was the problem. Third-quarter revenue was guided to $164.5m–$167.0m against consensus of $169.9m, and earnings to $0.18–$0.29 against $0.56, implying military course registrations up 1.0%–3.0% and nursing enrollment up 2.5%.

What the de-rating got right

Shares of Grand Canyon Education fell 7.0% over the two sessions after the amendment and raised guidance were published, and at $159.80 remain 22.8% below the $207.00 of a year ago. Price against trailing gross profit has compressed from roughly 10.3x then to 6.85x while that gross profit grew 7.6%; the forward multiple of 15.6x earnings sits below the trailing 19.2x. American Public Education is down 17.2% over three months and trades at 2.41x trailing gross profit, back from 3.07x in July, having given back most of a year's re-rating.

Only one of those two moves has a business event under it, and it is the smaller one: a phasing problem inside a year the company raised. The larger move has no deterioration behind it at all. The genuine damage in this industry landed on the owners of Title IV-funded campuses — Lincoln Educational Services fell from $43.20 in early August to $23.49, after reporting that enrollment grew about 9% while student starts rose about 1% "as fewer enrolled students than expected attended the first day of class", with management pointing at the restart of federal loan repayments in May 2026. A fee computed on a tuition invoice does not feel a student-credit shock the way a school that must collect from the student does.

What it feels instead is policy. The Education Department's 2011 bundled-services exception is what permits an online program manager to be paid a share of tuition at all, and the department has been soliciting comment on that exception and on the incentive-compensation prohibition it carves out of. Projections of US high-school graduates have the count peaking in 2025 and falling about 13% through 2041, even as total fall 2025 enrollment came in roughly 1% above the year before. The pipeline is a slope, not a cliff edge, and it runs the length of the new agreement.

So Grand Canyon Education has fixed its share of one university's frozen tuition until 2041, and fixed it over exactly the span in which the supply of eighteen-year-olds is expected to thin. The contract settles who gets paid and for how long. It cannot settle how many people walk through the door.

The October cut named geography, not the checkout page

Wells Fargo cut its price target on Booking Holdings, which collects a commission on hotel rooms, flights and cars it does not own through Booking.com, Agoda, Priceline, KAYAK and OpenTable, to $190 from $220 on 7 October, keeping an Equal Weight rating. The reason the bank gave was not artificial intelligence. It cited prolonged Middle East conflict pressuring long-haul demand, weighing on Booking more than peers given its geographic footprint, and trimmed its third-quarter room-night growth estimate to 5.5%.

The preceding six weeks had been read as the disintermediation trade arriving at last. Meta launched Muse, a personal AI agent able to complete a booking by itself, in the United States on 8 September; Expedia agreed on 22 September to sell hotel rooms inside it while remaining the merchant collecting the payment, and the market read the arrangement as a loss for both names, Expedia closing 5.5% lower that session and Booking 4.2%. Booking's own finance chief had already sized that channel. Referral traffic from large language models is "under 1% of room nights" and "hasn't moved so much recently," Ewout Steenbergen said on the 4 August call.

What Booking's August guidance already contained

The company's framework for the year assumed seven of its twelve months carry Middle East disruption. "We are at a high single-digit level for gross bookings and revenues and at mid-teens level for EPS at the high end," Steenbergen told investors on 4 August. Consensus has since settled where that guidance pointed: revenue up 9.6% for 2026 and earnings per share up 15.0%.

The June quarter is the base. Revenue of $7.352bn grew 8.1% year on year, half the 16.2% of the March quarter, on gross bookings of $51.0bn — a 14.4% commission take. Room nights rose 5% while marketing spend rose 11%, to $2.37bn, which management attributed on the same call to "changes in traffic mix, incremental investments in paid marketing at attractive ROIs, and a shift of merchandising spend to performance marketing." Operating margin still widened, to 34.0% from 33.1%, so the deceleration is a volume and price story rather than a cost one.

The route around it by acquisition is closed. On 9 September the EU General Court upheld the prohibition of Booking's eTraveli deal, the flight-booking purchase that would have put the air ticket next to the room night.

Meanwhile the rooms themselves are firming. CoStar and Tourism Economics raised their 2026 US revenue-per-available-room forecast to 4.4% from 2.8%. It is the flights that thinned: the International Air Transport Association cut 2026 passenger traffic growth to 2.1% from 4.9% and expects Middle East traffic to contract 11.4% in a region that handles more than 40% of global connecting traffic, on jet fuel averaging $152 a barrel against roughly $90 in 2025.

Booking closed at $160.34 on 9 October, down 24.8% from its 25 August close and 26.0% over twelve months. The shares now change hands at 15.3x forward earnings against 17.7x trailing and a ten-year average CNBC puts at 22.3x, with a 7.7% trailing free-cash-flow yield.

Expedia's year went the other way

Expedia Group, which runs Brand Expedia, Hotels.com and Vrbo alongside a wholesale arm selling travel inventory into other companies' checkout pages, has grown revenue faster for four straight quarters, reaching 14.0% in the June quarter, with operating margin at 23.9% against 14.0% a year earlier. Its business-to-business gross bookings rose 21%, to $10.74bn, against 8% in consumer bookings, and the cash it collects before guests travel — deferred merchant bookings — reached $14.4bn at 30 June against $9.3bn at year-end. It trades at 13.1x forward earnings against 16.4x trailing and is up 22.5% over twelve months, the only one of the three still higher. Chief executive Ariane Gorin told the Skift Global Forum on 23 September: "I certainly personally don't believe that a large portion of bookings are going to be an end-to-end conversational experience." A Sherman Act class action filed in Washington federal court on 2 October alleges it imposed restrictive pricing terms on hotels.

The rupee does MakeMyTrip's talking

MakeMyTrip, the Indian platform behind makemytrip.com, goibibo.com and redbus.in, grew June-quarter gross bookings 19.9% in constant currency, to $2,854.7m, with hotels and packages outgrowing air ticketing. Reported revenue grew 6.2%; the rupee touched a record low of 96.775 per dollar on 9 October, down about 9% over twelve months. Net income fell 67.8%, to $8.35m, and the shares still carry 91.4x forward earnings after a 53.0% twelve-month decline.

So the industry label describes an accounting convention, not a shared exposure. Booking's markdown is part earned: growth genuinely halved and the traffic bill is rising faster than the volume it buys, which is a margin claim on every future booking. What the October evidence does not support is the version in which the checkout page was taken away, because the hotel market it distributes was revised up and the measured AI referral share is under a hundredth of room nights. MakeMyTrip's decline is translation plus a collapsed profit line at the group's dearest multiple. Expedia's fall of 18.8% since late August has no deterioration behind it at all.

Booking's last disclosed room-night growth was 5%. Its third-quarter results are due on 3 November and Expedia's on 4 November, and the line that settles the argument is not revenue but the count of nights actually stayed.

Federal energy forecasters told households on 6 October that heating with propane will cost less this winter, about 3% less than last. In the Northeast the saving is larger: the Energy Information Administration, the statistical arm of the US Department of Energy, expects both lower prices and decreasing consumption to pull propane-heating expenditures down 15% against a year earlier. For a distributor whose revenue is gallons multiplied by a price, that is a smaller top line arriving just as the season starts.

It is also close to irrelevant to what Suburban Propane Partners earns. The master limited partnership distributes propane, fuel oil and refined fuels to roughly 1 million residential, commercial, industrial and agricultural customers across 42 states from about 700 locations, and its profit is a unit margin: cents on each retail gallon, the spread between a wholesale cost it pays and a posted retail price it sets. Cheap, well-supplied propane is the friendly end of that arrangement. Inventories stood at 103m barrels in the week to 26 September, about 13m barrels above the previous five-year average, with Gulf Coast stocks some 21% above theirs, and the Mont Belvieu wholesale benchmark was quoted at $0.919 a gallon on 6 October.

The June quarter separated gallons from gross profit

Suburban's fiscal third quarter, ended 27 June, is the cleanest demonstration available. Retail propane gallons came to 70.6 million, down 1.8%, against average temperatures across its service territories 17% warmer than normal. Total gross margin was $160.3m, unchanged from a year earlier, and the company's release attributed the underlying $3.9m decline excluding mark-to-market effects "primarily due to lower propane volumes sold, as propane unit margins remained steady."

The weather did not take the margin; it took the volume, and the volume loss was smaller than the weather implied. Chief executive Michael Stivala said the quarter's volumes "benefited from continued strength and growth in our counter-seasonal customer base," with May and June running ahead of the prior year on agricultural, industrial and national-account business.

What did deteriorate was the earnings line below the margin. Adjusted EBITDA fell to $18.0m from $27.0m on higher operating costs alongside the warmth, and revenue inched up 0.5% to $261.4m.

The socket count is the slow problem

Neither protagonist can buy its way out of attrition. US retail propane distribution is a roughly $15-20bn market growing between zero and 1%, or slightly negative, losing an estimated 0.7% of home heating-oil and propane accounts a year to natural gas and electric conversions, and there were no tracked retail propane acquisitions by the national operators in the 2025-26 year. AmeriGas was a net seller, having divested its Hawaii operations. Against that, roughly 2% net attrition at both Suburban and AmeriGas compares with the 4.2% base-business attrition Star Group reported for the twelve months to March 2026.

Inside UGI, propane is one dollar of four — and none of them sets the price

UGI Corporation owns AmeriGas, which serves about 1.4 million propane customers from 1,600 distribution points, and reports it beside a Pennsylvania utility delivering gas to roughly 672,000 customers, a midstream and marketing book, and a European liquefied petroleum gas arm. The propane leg had the worse quarter by far: retail gallons fell 10% to 124 million and AmeriGas segment earnings before interest and taxes dropped $25m, with April temperatures 16% warmer than the prior year. Underneath, net debt at AmeriGas came down roughly $270m in the quarter, with new paper issued at 6.875% retiring notes carrying coupons up to 9.375%. "We're very confident the leverage is going to be sub 4," chief financial officer Sean O'Brien told investors on 6 August. "We think by the end of the year, we have a shot to be sub 4."

Group guidance of $2.75-$2.90 in adjusted earnings per share for fiscal 2026 implies a down year against $3.09 reported for fiscal 2025, and at 11.5x forward earnings against 11.9x trailing, UGI is the cheapest reading among the listed gas distributors. None of that is what moves the stock. KKR offered about $9bn, or $42.50 a share, in an approach first reported on 18 August, and the gap to that number has widened since early September, from 10.7% below the offer on 4 September to 12.7% below it on 9 October, with no board response or agreement discoverable.

How much of Suburban's low the business explains

Some of it, and not most. Suburban's units closed at a 52-week low of $16.39 on 1 October, 20.7% beneath their $20.68 high, then recovered 4.3% over the following six sessions. One soft quarter is real, and an adjusted loss of $0.27 a unit came in wider than the $0.19 consensus loss. But the two meters that would signal distress did not move: trailing distribution coverage was 2.07x and the total consolidated leverage ratio 4.35x, the same as a year earlier, with the quarterly distribution held at $0.325 a unit — a 7.6% yield at the 9 October close and an unbroken run of 31 years.

The rest looks like a sector repricing that Suburban happened to sit inside. Every listed gas distributor fell over the 30 days to 9 October, and Suburban's 2.0% decline was the mildest of the group, against 8.1% at ONE Gas and 5.2% at National Fuel Gas; the long-Treasury proxy was unchanged over both 30 and 90 days, so no fresh move in long yields arrived to explain the month. At 8.9x trailing EV/EBITDA and 8.3x forward earnings against 8.7x trailing, Suburban is priced for no growth at all, while the single-analyst consensus for fiscal 2026 looks for revenue down 3.3% to $1.40bn with EBITDA up 4.8% to $307.6m — the decoupling, drawn forward a year, on one forecaster's work.

Both companies close their books on 30 September and have historically reported the full year in mid-November; no date has been announced. Nothing in the October outlook tells either of them how cold January will be, and the gallon count remains the number the weather owns. The spread is the half they set themselves, and an overstocked market has handed them the easier half.

Planet Fitness Grew Revenue on an Ad Levy and Equipment Resales, Not on Busier Gyms

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The markdown came from guidance, with same-club sales cut to roughly 1%. The two names filed alongside it went their own ways: Life Time raised dues on nearly flat membership, and Peloton is harvesting a shrinking subscription base.

Planet Fitness reported rising profit through a year in which its shares lost roughly half their value, and the gap sits in what its revenue line actually measures. Of the $16.1m increase in franchise-segment revenue in the second quarter, $10.1m came from raising franchisees' national advertising fund contribution to 3% of sales from 2%, money that passes through to media buying. Busier clubs contributed $1.7m.

The shares are held as a claim on 21.5 million gym memberships paying monthly dues. What the company is paid is narrower: a royalty on franchisees' dues, an advertising levy it is obliged to spend, the takings of the clubs it owns outright, and the exercise machinery it resells to its own franchisees. That last line came to $85.6m in the quarter, or 23.4% of group revenue, and what it tracks is franchisee borrowing.

Eighty-five percent of it was replacement equipment for clubs already open, bought by franchisees borrowing at Small Business Administration 7(a) rates that ran from 9.75% to 13.25% variable in May.

The franchise segment itself, the royalty annuity most owners of the stock think they are buying, came to $135.8m, or 37.2% of revenue. Royalties within it rose $4.7m, and $2.5m of that was simply clubs opened since April 2025 entering the base, with another $0.5m from higher royalties on annual fees. The 1.7% gain in franchise same-club sales produced $1.7m.

The markdown landed on dues the company has not collected yet

The quarter was good. Revenue rose 7.1% to $365.2m, operating income 20.9% to $123.8m, and diluted earnings per share reached $0.87 against $0.69 a year earlier. System membership hit 21.5 million, up 3.6%, with Black Card penetration around 68%, up 210 basis points. Part of the earnings gain was bought: diluted shares fell 8.4% year on year after about $200m of stock was retired in the quarter, funded partly by a $75m note drawdown that adds $4m of annual interest.

What the market marked down was the future dues line. On May 7 the shares fell 31.19% in a session, from $63.96 to $44.01, after the company cut same-club sales guidance to roughly 1% from 4-5% and adjusted net income to a decline of about 2% from growth of 4-5%, having added just over 700,000 net members in the first quarter against roughly a million a year before. Management blamed messaging that missed its audiences, competition in select markets, weather and the macro backdrop; shelving a national Black Card price increase cost about 150 basis points of the comp outlook by itself. A securities fraud class action followed the drop.

The answer was price. From August 31 through September 10 the company ran a $1-down, $10-a-month Classic Card offer, with that rate protected for the member's lifetime.

"As we evaluate any pricing decisions in our architecture, whether they're tiers, regional nuance, or something with Black Card, we're going to evaluate the price elasticity in conjunction with join mix, as well as total sustainable net member growth, and impact on churn," chief executive Colleen Keating told investors on August 6.

On October 9 the shares rose 10.5% to $47.98, their best day of the year, after UBS reiterated a Buy rating with a target of $80, citing September and early-October membership strength and the $10 promotion. The same day RBC Capital cut its target to $60 from $65 and Baird to $60 from $72, both keeping Outperform.

The rival bid for the same ten-dollar member is better funded now

Planet Fitness accounts for roughly 27% of all US gym memberships, and its four nearest low-price rivals together hold about 5m members. The pressure comes through amenities: Crunch Fitness, with about 3m members across some 516 clubs, and EoS Fitness, with about 1m across 127, are opening large-format clubs with infrared saunas and heated group classes. Crunch grew its club count 17% in 2024 and took a majority investment from Leonard Green & Partners in April 2025.

Life Time charges $245 a month and barely adds members

Life Time, which builds and runs resort-style athletic clubs with pools, courts, cafes and childcare, collects its dues directly and is really a property developer. Revenue rose 13.7% to $866.0m in the second quarter and operating income 38.0% to $149.7m, on average monthly dues of $245, up about 12.3%, with centre memberships up 1.2% to roughly 860,000. Adjusted EBITDA margin widened 80 basis points to 28.5% and net leverage fell to 1.4x. The buildings pay for the buildings: about $400m of sale-leasebacks closed in 2026, first-quarter rent rose 10.7% to $89.9m, and $33m to $35m of interest was expected to be capitalized this year. "Even after $400 million of sale-leasebacks each year, we will continue to grow our owned real estate portfolio as well as grow positive free cash flow," founder and chief executive Bahram Akradi said. Equity holders pay 14.1x trailing EV/EBITDA for that, a third more than Planet Fitness's 10.6x, on a trailing free-cash-flow yield of minus 1.7%.

Peloton, which sells connected bikes and treadmills with class subscriptions, operates no clubs. Subscription revenue rose 7% to $437m in its fiscal fourth quarter while hardware fell 14% to $171m, lifting recurring revenue to 72% of the total; subscription adjusted gross margin reached 73.6% against 13.4% on the machines. Subscriptions themselves fell 8.8% to 2.553m and churn rose to 2.2%. Fiscal 2026 brought $378m of free cash flow against a $2.14bn market value, and chief executive Peter Stern attributed guided revenue weakness to "churn created by subscription price hikes implemented last year."

One shelf, three different payers

Filed in one classification, these three share nothing on the demand side. Over twelve months to October 9 Life Time gained 57.5%, Peloton lost 30.3% and Planet Fitness lost 49.1%, a spread of 107 points under one interest-rate backdrop. Since January only one session saw two of them move more than 9% on the same day, August 6, and that was two separate earnings reports landing the same morning.

The split runs on income. US gym membership reached a record 81 million in 2025 with low-price brands taking above-average growth, yet members earning over $75,000 a year hold just over half of all memberships and the heaviest users earn more than $150,000. Life Time can price families up 12% a year on that base. Planet Fitness went to $10 to defend volume.

So the halving is not explained by what Planet Fitness reported, and only partly by what it will report. Consensus now has 2026 EBITDA falling 2.1% to $611.1m, the first forecast contraction in the series, which makes part of the de-rating from roughly 40.8x trailing earnings in January to 16.3x today rational work. The rest rides on whether a $10 join price buys back the member growth the dues architecture was supposed to deliver.

Meanwhile the revenue line will keep misleading. Both of the company's 2026 cadences, system openings and equipment placements, are weighted to the fourth quarter, so the year's closing revenue print will be decided largely by how much franchisees are willing to borrow at double-digit rates. It will be read as a verdict on how many people joined a gym.
