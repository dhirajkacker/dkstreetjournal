The companies that make the switchgear, transformers and enclosures an AI data center needs before it can be energized lost between a sixth and a quarter of their value in the two weeks after 3 November. Only one of the three had already told investors its orders were cooling. nVent Electric, which sells electrical enclosures, server-rack infrastructure and heat tracing under the HOFFMAN, SCHROFF and RAYCHEM brands, disclosed in late October that organic orders grew only low double digits in the September quarter, against roughly 40% in the March quarter and about 65% a year earlier.

The markdown treated all three as one bet on AI power demand, after restrictions won 46 of 47 data-center ballot measures on 3 November and SoftBank on 10 November wrote down $3.7bn of data-center projects, including $2.85bn of non-refundable advances already paid to equipment companies. What a single bet obscures is that these three books convert at completely different speeds, and the contested capacity sits in 2028: Nomura's post-midterm read is that 2027 construction schedules are largely locked and that moratoria push back 2028 starts.

The five-month book is the one that moved

nVent's backlog of $2.6bn is about five months of shipments at the September quarter's $1.515bn of revenue, so its orders are a present-tense reading. That quarter was strong by every reported measure: revenue up 44% organically, return on sales of 22.1%, and full-year adjusted earnings guidance raised to $5.36-$5.39 a share from $5.00-$5.10. Underneath it, gross margin fell 161 basis points to 35.8% while operating margin rose 286 basis points, which is scale offsetting price and mix, not pricing power widening. Infrastructure is now 60% of year-to-date sales against 45% in 2025. "We expect the infrastructure vertical to deliver record double-digit organic growth this year with data center expansion, and the need for power," chief executive Beth Wozniak said on 30 October. At 21.6x this year's consensus earnings, against roughly 30x forward in May, the de-rating is doing rational work.

Powell books the price, then waits two years

Powell Industries, a Houston maker of custom switchgear and modular E-House substations, recognizes engineered-to-order work over time at prices fixed when the order was signed. Its backlog reached $2.4bn at 30 June, up 69%, on bookings of $934.2m against $311.7m of revenue. Chairman and chief executive Brett Cope said the quarter "elevated our backlog to over $2 billion for the first time in our 79-year history." Gross margin was 30.57%, versus 30.70% a year earlier. That is the mechanism in one line: a record order book and no incremental margin, because the margin was set at signing. The $2.4bn is roughly 2.1 years of revenue at the trailing rate. Powell trades at 20.8x fiscal 2027 consensus earnings and 27.2x trailing, against the 35x forward and 58x trailing flagged in May.

Vintage, not volume, at GE Vernova

GE Vernova's third-quarter orders rose 96% organically to $29bn and backlog reached $197bn, with Electrification equipment backlog at $42bn, up 60%, and segment margin up 470 basis points to 19.1%. On 2027 consensus earnings the multiple is about 31.9x, against the roughly 50x Rajiv Jain called unsustainable in July.

The quarters account for one of the three declines. nVent's own disclosures show the deceleration its risk list anticipated, and its short book means a 2028 slowdown reaches it fast. The contract manufacturer that bought Advanced Micro Devices' server-assembly plants reported on 9 November that its heaviest quarter of rack output was also its richest. That is not how assembly work for the AI buildout is supposed to behave. Each rack shipped now carries a far larger bill of materials. The mechanism is real; it simply does not describe every company doing the work.

Fewer players, squeezed harder

Two of the three listed subcontractors that package and test other companies' chips published October revenue on 10 November, and both numbers accelerated. ChipMOS, the Hsinchu house that assembles and tests memory chips and the driver chips behind LCD and OLED panels, reported NT$2,850.6m for the month, up 30.9% from a year earlier, and said it continues to add capacity against customer forecasts and long-term supply agreements.

The shelf these names sit on was assembled as a spillover bet: contract packaging at TSMC is the bottleneck in accelerator output, so the merchant subcontractors that take its overflow were the cheap way to own the shortage. November tested that belief crudely, knocking 20% to 30% off all three inside a month. What the September-quarter and October disclosures show is that the member with no accelerator packaging whatsoever posted the widest margin gain of the three.

The AI content ChipMOS never packages

ChipMOS's September-quarter revenue was TWD 8.4008bn, up 36.7% year over year, with gross margin of 21.67% against 12.37% a year earlier — 930 basis points, the largest expansion on the shelf. Operating margin went to 19.73% from 6.02%, and net income more than tripled. Digitimes reported earnings of NT$1.89 a share, the company's highest quarterly profit since 2022.

That is a loading-and-pricing story, and the company said so in its own words, attributing record quarterly revenue to "sustained AI-related demand outpacing available memory supply." The likelier reading is that memory capacity pulled toward high-bandwidth parts tightens conventional DRAM and NAND, which fills back-end test floors and firms per-unit prices. The base was low: annual gross margin had fallen from 26.47% in 2021 to 10.83% in 2025. There is also headroom — the June quarter put overall utilisation at 78%, memory at 65%, with memory 51% of revenue, and no comparable September figure has been filed. ASE's margin ceiling became its floor

The one book that actually got worse

One session down, four back

The cycle's clearest beneficiary on this shelf has never packaged an accelerator and does not plan to. It tests the ordinary memory that everything else needs, and there is less of that than the world wants.

Three contract chipmakers spent the better part of three years watching what they charge for a finished wafer fall. In their September-quarter disclosures, all three said that direction has changed, and one of them has already told customers roughly what next year will cost.

A gauge of US semiconductor stocks plunged 12% on 5 November, its worst day since the onset of the pandemic in March 2020, on speculation that a Democratic sweep of Congress would bring tighter regulation of US data centers. The filings record something else: loading, wafer prices and prepaid capacity reservations all moved up in the quarter, at the leading edge and at mature nodes alike.

The same wafer, three price lists

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, which manufactures, packages and tests chips to other companies' designs, is paid an annually negotiated price per leading-edge wafer plus separately constrained advanced-packaging capacity. October revenue, filed on Form 6-K on 10 November, was NT$586.89bn, up 59.7% year on year. GlobalFoundries, the Malta, New York specialty foundry selling radio-frequency, power-management and embedded-memory processes, is paid partly for reserved capacity under long-term agreements with customer prepayments. What the six sessions actually priced

The decline was concentrated and uneven. CrowdStrike sells most of its new business through a pooled commitment called Falcon Flex: an enterprise signs up for a multi-year dollar amount, then draws modules against it later as it needs them, from endpoint and identity protection to cloud workload defense and log management. Earlier in this cycle the company told investors how large that committed pool had become. It no longer does.

The intuitive reading of a de-rating at a company that signs commitments before it recognizes revenue is that the commitments have stopped converting. The filings argue the reverse. Remaining performance obligations, the contracted revenue CrowdStrike has booked but not yet earned, reached $10.7bn at 31 July 2026 against $7.2bn a year earlier. Revenue over the same span grew 26%. About 46% of that balance, roughly $4.9bn, is due to be recognized within twelve months.

Four quarters of going faster, not slower

Quarterly revenue growth has accelerated in each of the last four periods, from 22.2% to 23.3%, then 25.6% and 25.8%, on revenue of $1.471bn in the July quarter. Annual recurring revenue reached $5.84bn, up 25%, with a record $332.8m of net new ARR added in the quarter, itself up 51%. The full-year outlook for net new ARR was raised by 630 basis points, to a $1.350bn-$1.359bn range implying about 34% growth. Gross margin widened to 74.6% from 73.5%.

"Q2 was the best quarter in CrowdStrike's history," founder and chief executive George Kurtz said in the 26 August 2026 release. "Delivering record Falcon Flex results, record net new ARR, and accelerating growth—the Falcon is soaring."

What a Flex customer's bill does after signing

The conversion ladder is disclosed as uplift rather than dollars. Moving a standard subscription onto Flex lifts that account's ending ARR by more than 40% on average; a first re-commitment adds a further 25%; accounts that have re-committed at least twice carry ARR 53% above where their first Flex began. By the company's September investor briefing about 630 accounts had re-committed, six times the prior year, with the first re-commitment typically arriving around eight months into a multi-year contract. More than 935 Flex accounts were added in the July quarter alone, more than the prior three quarters combined.

That consolidation pitch rests on a real position: third-party estimates put CrowdStrike at roughly 22.7% of endpoint protection, against Microsoft Defender's 15.6% and SentinelOne's 11.5%, with its strength concentrated in large cloud-first estates.

The numbers that stopped being published

Flex total deal value, the committed-dollars headline, was last quantified at more than $3.2bn in the April-2025 quarter, up more than sixfold. Its replacement is ending ARR from accounts that have adopted Flex, which passed $2.29bn at 31 July and grew 101%. That is a recognized measure, so the gap between dollars committed and dollars converted has left the public record. The five-or-more-module adoption rate, 67% as of 31 January 2025, is gone too; the disclosures now run 51% of subscription customers on six or more modules, 35% on seven and 26% on eight. A quarterly net retention percentage is no longer given, only the statement that gross and net retention both increased.

Cash is the one check that does not depend on management's framing, and it holds: free cash flow of $377m, 26% of revenue, with the full year guided to at least 30%. Stock compensation ran at about $377m in the same quarter, which is why GAAP net income was $5.3m and why sales and gross profit are the anchors that mean anything here.

A month of selling that came from outside the company

The worst session was 5 November, when the close fell 9.9% and Palantir, CrowdStrike and Okta all dropped about 10% as investors cut exposure to the data-center build-out after the Democratic congressional sweep. Three days earlier Deutsche Bank had upgraded the stock to Buy and raised its target to $300 from $240. On 29 October, Fortinet and ServiceNow both beat revenue estimates and were sold hard anyway. Over 30 days CrowdStrike's 12.9% decline was the third-smallest among the seven software and security names on this shelf, and the stock is still up 4.7% over three months.

So almost none of the fall traces to the business, and the compression has not undone much. At 57.8x trailing gross profit, CrowdStrike is dearer than the 48.4x it fetched in late July, and 51.9x forward. Palo Alto, which sells firewalls plus subscription services to large enterprises and governments, trades at 25.8x forward gross profit; Fortinet, whose FortiGate hardware business earned a 30.6% operating margin in the September quarter, at 14.4x; Zscaler, selling cloud-delivered secure access per user on 24.9% growth and a 76.7% gross margin, at 10.5x. CrowdStrike costs twice the nearest of them. The markdown repriced the growth; it did not find a flaw in the conversion.

What an owner is paying for, then, is a conversion engine whose best evidence is now a thickening order book and management's description of it. The next test of both arrives on 1 December, after the US close.

IDT Corporation closed its fiscal year on 31 July with the shrinking half of its business finally mattering less than the growing half. Group gross profit reached $496.8m, an increase of $50.6m, while the Traditional Communications segment, prepaid international calling and wholesale voice and still two-thirds of revenue, gave up about $6.8m of gross profit, a 4% decline per the annual report. That arithmetic is what the market bought on 29 September, the session after the print, when the stock gapped 12.7% to $76.20 on volume six times the prior day's. The quarter was read as proof that a melting telecom shell had become a payments and software group. It is proof of that. What it does not establish is the price: the shares have risen 59% in six months, and with trailing earnings per share up about 6% over that span, a derived split puts roughly 50 of those 59 points in multiple expansion, from near 16x trailing earnings in May to 24.0x now.

The terminal in the corner store is the whole engine

NRS sells point-of-sale terminals, card processing and in-store advertising to independent convenience, liquor, tobacco and gas-station retailers. It ended the year with 35,400 retailer locations, 2,700 more than a year earlier, and about 95% of its revenue recurring. Revenue rose 24% to $159.4m and segment gross profit was $146.6m on 12.3% of group revenue: the margin structure of a software business attached to a hardware socket.

Accenture closed its fiscal year on 1 October with the biggest order book in its history, and the term that matters inside it is not a growth rate but a pricing clause. Fixed-price and outcome-based work now exceeds 65% of new bookings, which means scope delivered in fewer hours than quoted leaves the saving in Accenture's own gross profit instead of in a smaller invoice.

For a year the market treated enterprise IT services as generative AI's clearest casualty: if the model writes the code, the firm that bills for the coder loses the hour. All three of the largest listed names have now reported inside five weeks. Their disclosures say the hour is not the variable that decides. Contract form is, and it splits them — Accenture keeps the productivity gain, while Infosys has told investors it is passing it through.

The half of the book where price is agreed first

Accenture's fiscal 2026 new bookings were an all-time high $84.54bn, up 5%, at a book-to-bill of 1.1. Inside the fourth quarter the mix is the finding: managed services, the outsourcing half where price is contracted in advance, took $12.77bn of bookings at a book-to-bill of 1.4, against consulting's $9.40bn at exactly 1.0. The firm signed 141 deals of $100m or more in the quarter against 129 a year earlier.

That shows up where it should. Fiscal 2026 revenue of $74.18bn rose 6.5% while operating income rose 11.5%, with gross margin at 32.05% against 31.91%. "We continue to believe the opportunities related to AI are greater than the impact of AI-related efficiencies in our business," chair and chief executive Julie Sweet told investors on 1 October.

The caution is in the guide. Accenture's fiscal 2027 outlook of 3-6% local-currency growth includes 2-2.5 points from acquisitions, leaving roughly 2-2.5% organic at the midpoint, a step down from fiscal 2026's 5%. Capability is being bought: $4.9bn spent on acquisitions in fiscal 2026, around $5bn more planned. At 17.3x forward earnings against 12.3x in mid-August, almost the whole three-month re-rating is the multiple rather than the estimates.

Cognizant's margin gain is not coming from delivery

Cognizant's adjusted operating margin reached 16.3% in the third quarter, a seventh consecutive year-on-year expansion, while GAAP operating margin of 15.4% fell 60 basis points. Below that headline, gross margin slipped to 33.77% from 33.87% and gross profit grew 4.3% against revenue's 4.6%. The engagement itself got no more profitable; the gain arrived from costs below the delivery line, where Project Leap restructuring is worth about $200m this year.

The pyramid got both busier and bigger: utilisation excluding trainees rose to 86% from 85% and headcount reached 359,900 at 30 September. Fixed-price and transaction work is now nearly 58% of revenue, up some 500 basis points since 2022. But third-quarter bookings fell 7% year on year and 2026 constant-currency guidance was cut to 4.0-4.4% from 4.0-5.5%. "The rebaselining of productivity has already happened to a large extent," chief executive Ravi Kumar said on 29 October. Cognizant is the cheapest of the three at 11.2x forward earnings and 4.11x trailing gross profit.

Infosys put a number on the giveback

Infosys disclosed AI revenue at 10.5% of the total in the September quarter, up from 8.2%. Applying those shares to reported revenue implies AI work grew by about $124m sequentially, to roughly $541m, while consolidated revenue rose only about $70m: the non-AI book shrank some $54m in the same three months. Group revenue of $5.152bn was up 1.5%.

The order intake is not the problem. "We won 19 large deals in Q2 with a TCV of $5.2 billion, which is second highest quarterly TCV in last five years. Of this, 62% was net new," chief financial officer Jayesh Sanghrajka said on 23 October. Price is. "Growth was below our expectations. Volume was softer, and price increases were lower than anticipated amidst high competitive intensity," he said the same day. Infosys cut fiscal 2027 growth guidance to 1-2% from 1.5-3%, and the company's own list of second-half margin pressures includes "continued sharing of AI driven productivity benefits." It walked away from work worth 1.8-2% of fiscal 2027 revenue rather than match the givebacks clients asked for.

Headcount rose 4,400 in the quarter and annualised revenue per employee, derived from reported revenue and disclosed staff numbers, was flat near $62,700. The growth came from heads. Infosys is also the only one of the three whose forward earnings multiple, 15.3x, sits above its trailing 15.2x, and at 8.07x trailing gross profit it is dearer per dollar of delivery profit than Accenture at 6.31x.

What the autumn bid actually bought

Accenture has risen 18.1% over 30 days to 11 November and 42.7% over three months, including a 15.9% session on its results, yet it is up only 4.6% on the year. Cognizant gained 8.0% over 30 days and remains 11.9% below its level a year ago. Infosys gained 13.6%, the best of the three, while sitting 27.7% below last November. The bid is shelf-wide — EPAM rose 40.5% over the same 30 days, Globant 24.2%, Genpact 20.4% — while Gartner, which sells research rather than delivery labor, fell 5.4%. On 5 November, when Nvidia fell 10.3%, all three services names closed higher, and Indian IT shares rallied the next day against an AI-linked selloff. The likelier reading for the Infosys leg is rotation out of AI hardware rather than anything Infosys disclosed.

So one of the three moves is earned on the meters that matter. Accenture's order book and its operating leverage both inflected, and the mechanism is legible: under a contract whose price was agreed before the work, fewer hours is margin. Cognizant's improvement is real but located in restructuring rather than in the engagement, with intake shrinking behind it. Infosys has the largest single deal of the quarter and no pricing power to go with it, which is why flat revenue per employee and a cut guide sit beside the shelf's best month.

Accenture reports its first quarter on 17 December and Infosys the December quarter on 13 January. Both prints answer the same question, and it is a contractual one: when a client asks for its share of the saving, which of them is able to say no.

Alphabet told investors on 28 October that advertising inside its AI-generated answers earns less than advertising beside a list of links. Search and other revenue grew 12% to $63.35bn in the September quarter, and Chief Business Officer Philipp Schindler named "lower monetization we were seeing in AI Mode" as one reason the line slowed, behind currency and the retail and media verticals. Management did not quantify the gap. The next session the shares fell 12.6%, the worst day in their history and about $500bn of market value, with press coverage tying the fall as much to September-quarter capital expenditure of $51.4bn, free cash flow of -$9.1bn and a halted buyback as to Search.

The belief that quarter was supposed to test is that AI answers punish whoever does not own the index. On that reading the exposed names are Opera, the Oslo browser company that hands its queries to a third-party engine for a contracted cut, and Baidu, which has spent two years converting its Chinese result page to generated answers. The month ran the other way. Over the 30 sessions to 11 November Alphabet fell 17.9%, Opera 12.4% and Baidu 4.7%, so the segment's one-month markdown is arithmetically Alphabet's.

One query, three meters

Alphabet is paid per click on inventory it controls end to end, and that meter is still rising. Paid clicks grew 8% in the September quarter against an 11% average across the first nine months of 2026, while average cost per click rose 4%. Everything around Search accelerated: group revenue rose 24.7% to $127.58bn, gross profit 24.9%, and Google Cloud doubled to $30.7bn on a backlog of $561bn. Management is expanding ads in AI Mode globally for the first time and says click-through rates on sponsored items there already match organic product listings.

Opera is not paid per query at all. It takes a contracted share of what its search partner earns, and Google is roughly 32% of Opera's revenue under an agreement that, for markets outside the United States, runs only to 31 December 2026. Advertising was $115.4m of June-quarter revenue against $62.1m of query revenue, with annualized revenue per user up 25% to $2.46. Opera fell 12.6% on 30 October, the session after Alphabet's drop, with no company news of its own. Four sessions later it pre-announced September-quarter revenue of $182.8m, up 20%, query revenue up 17%, advertising up 22% and adjusted EBITDA of $43.2m, above the guided high end, with desktop monthly active users reaching 100 million in October.

The one cannibalizing itself discloses it

Baidu's meter is the deteriorating one. Online marketing revenue fell 19% to RMB13.1bn in the June quarter while AI-powered new business reached RMB12.5bn, up 25% and now half of core revenue. The substitution is disclosed in the product itself: the share of result pages carrying AI-generated content rose from 11% in mid-May 2024 to roughly 70% of mobile searches by October 2025. The offset has not covered the hole. Group revenue fell 4.5%, gross margin compressed to 39.0% from 43.9%, net income fell 68%, and revenue has now declined year on year for four straight reported quarters with gross profit falling roughly three times as fast.

"While our online marketing business remains under pressure, the growing momentum in our core AI-powered business reaffirms Baidu's transition from an internet-centric company to an AI-first company," founder and chief executive Robin Li said on 18 August. Some of that pressure is the market's: China's digital ad spending is forecast to grow 7.5% in 2026, down from 8.7%, with budgets concentrating on closed-loop platforms.

Only one of the three markdowns is doing work

Baidu's is. Its trailing price-to-earnings multiple is unusable because trailing earnings are negative after the RMB11.2bn charge booked in the September-2025 quarter, leaving price-to-book of 0.66x as the one clean anchor: two-thirds of the carrying value of its own equity, for a business consensus expects to shrink again this year.

The other two fell for reasons their results do not carry. Alphabet trades at 12.4x trailing gross profit against 17.6x in early May, a compression of roughly 29% while that gross profit grew 24.9%; the quarter repriced the spending, not the franchise. Opera sits at 13.5x forward earnings and 7.7x trailing enterprise value to EBITDA, with a trailing free-cash-flow yield of 8.5% and a dividend of $0.80 a year, 5.0% at the current price, against consensus for FY2026 revenue up 23% and earnings per share up 39%. Neither company had reported a September quarter when the markdown happened: Baidu's lands on 16 November and Opera's full results on 17 November.

Opera's sharpest exposure sits in a calendar entry rather than a monetization rate. The contract that routes its non-US queries to Google and pays it a share of the proceeds expires at the end of December, and the company that decides what those queries are worth next year is the same one whose own bad session dragged Opera down with it.
