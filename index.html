Congress's stopgap funding law lets the Pentagon keep paying for work already underway and forbids it from beginning anything new. That one rule has done more to sort defense share prices over the past month than any change in what the armed services want to buy.

The companies that build airborne and ground sensing systems have fallen far enough to be read as proof that money is draining out of manned sensor platforms and into satellites. Their June quarters say something else. L3Harris Technologies, Leonardo DRS and Elbit Systems each reported faster revenue growth, wider operating margins and a record order book. The name marked down hardest in the group is the one whose entire business is sensing from space.

The rule inside the stopgap

The continuing resolution signed on 2 September 2026 runs to 11 December and holds the department at the extended fiscal 2026 baseline of $838.5bn against a $1.5 trillion request. It bars any project, program or activity that was not already funded and active in the prior year. Breaking Defense counts more than 150 fiscal 2027 initiatives blocked from starting, including $17.5bn of Golden Dome missile-defense work. A rule written that way penalizes a contractor waiting on a new start and leaves a contractor sitting on funded backlog untouched.

What L3Harris already has under contract

L3Harris, which integrates reconnaissance aircraft, space payloads, tactical radios and electronic warfare systems for governments, took $7.3bn of orders in the June quarter for a 1.2x book-to-bill, lifting backlog above $42bn. Revenue rose 8.4% to $5.88bn and operating margin widened to 11.1% from 10.8%. Its Space & Mission Systems unit grew 7% to about $3bn on higher volume in reconnaissance and missionized aircraft, classified space and F-35 control systems; the segment's 9.8% margin was 60 basis points lower only because a prior-year gain on a product-line sale did not repeat.

"We are actively negotiating more than $20 billion in new contracts, potentially tripling our backlog," chief executive Christopher Kubasik told investors on the 29 July 2026 call.

The shares are flat over thirty days, down 10.8% over ninety and 15.3% over a year. That leaves 24.9x trailing and 20.7x forward earnings against 38.0x trailing at the start of May, when the market capitalization was $60.7bn rather than today's $46.1bn, with consensus fiscal 2027 earnings per share of $11.94 still climbing at 12%. The propulsion franchise bought with Aerojet Rocketdyne in 2023 and the classified sensor portfolio are intact.

Leonardo DRS fell furthest and bought the software layer

DRS, which supplies infrared sensing, tactical radars and rugged computing to the US military and prime contractors, is down 25.5% over ninety days. Its bookings exceeded $1.1bn for a 1.2x book-to-bill, the 18th consecutive quarter at or above 1.0x, with record funded backlog of $5.1bn, up 17%. Operating margin widened 273 basis points to 11.2% on revenue up 10.1%. The Advanced Sensing and Computing segment that carries the reconnaissance franchise grew 8% to $587m with adjusted EBITDA margin up 110 basis points to 11.8%. "Organic revenue growth accelerated to 10% year-over-year," chief executive John Baylouny said on 30 July 2026.

Far from losing work to software-defined sensing, DRS agreed on 28 July to buy Raft, a McLean, Virginia mission-software firm, for $450m in cash. At 24.3x forward earnings it remains dearer than L3Harris, and consensus has its earnings growth decelerating from 27% this year to 6% next.

The space pure-play that should have won

If reconnaissance dollars were migrating to orbit, HawkEye 360 would be the beneficiary. It operates more than 30 satellites mapping radio-frequency emissions for government and allied customers, and consensus has revenue of $217m this year, up 85%, on a 70% gross margin. Instead it set an all-time low of $11.92 on 19 October, down 27.3% in thirty days from an IPO at $26 in May, and trades at 8.36x forward gross profit against 9.18x for DRS. The budget record points the same way: fiscal 2027 defense authorization drafts would dissolve the Space Development Agency into Space Force portfolio executives, after the prior request cut its transport layer by 20%.

Elbit's funding is European

Elbit, the Haifa prime that makes electro-optics, electronic warfare and rocket artillery, is up 52.2% over a year with a record $32.0bn backlog, 73% of it outside Israel and the quarter's increase mainly European. North America was 20% of June-quarter sales. Its largest award of the year was a roughly $1.4bn five-year modernization contract for a European customer in May. At 50.8x forward earnings and 8.1x book it is the most expensive name here, and no part of a bear case.

What the order books do and do not explain

None of the three order books explains the de-rating; neither drawdown traces to a dated catalyst, with no single session worse than about 3% for either L3Harris or DRS. The dividing line is whether a company's revenue has already been appropriated. Lockheed Martin and Northrop Grumman each rose about 2% over thirty days, while AeroVironment lost 28.5% and Kratos 20.8% — both dependent on programs the stopgap will not let the Pentagon begin. DRS sits between: funded backlog at a record, and a premium multiple that needed the new starts to justify it.

Both L3Harris and Leonardo DRS report third-quarter results on 29 October, six weeks before the stopgap expires. Until the appropriation passes, the safest thing a defense contractor can own is a contract somebody already signed.

Goldman Sachs's advisory revenues set an all-time quarterly record of $1.654bn on closing more deals in that same September quarter. Cabinets and kilowatts, billed monthly

Air Products' Americas business sold more gas in the June quarter and billed its customers less for the power that made it. Segment sales rose 5%, according to the company's quarterly filing: volumes added seven points, price was flat, and the energy cost passed through to customers subtracted two. The margin moved the other way, reaching 29.9% against 29.7% a year earlier, with about 50 basis points of that improvement coming from the same shrinking pass-through.

The top line of an industrial gas company is close to useless, then, as a gauge of whether the business got better. Most of the profit comes from air-separation units built inside a customer's fence under long-term take-or-pay contracts, where power and gas are rebilled at no margin and a fixed facility fee arrives whether the customer runs its plant or not. The meters that answer the question are operating margin, return on capital, and the money each company is committing to new plants. On those, Air Products and Linde have spent this year moving in opposite directions.

Two points of sales that were never profit

Linde, which supplies the same oxygen, nitrogen, argon and hydrogen and also builds the plants that make them, does the arithmetic in public. Its second-quarter 10-Q bridges 9% sales growth, to $9,289m, into six parts: two points of price, two of volume, two of currency, one of acquisitions, one from engineering, and one of "cost pass-through, representing the contractual billing of energy cost variances primarily to onsite customers." Half that growth carries no information about gas sold.

Air Products shows the same line running both ways inside one year. Over the nine months to 30 June, a higher pass-through added two points to Americas sales and cost the segment 50 basis points of margin, which fell to 29.0%.

Air Products got paid for retreating

On 26 June the company exited Casa Grande, Arizona and other small clean-energy distribution projects, citing slower-than-expected market development largely in hydrogen for mobility. Four days later it abandoned the Louisiana Clean Energy Complex, a low-carbon hydrogen and ammonia plant already under construction, on returns grounds. The pre-tax charge runs to $2.9bn, $2.2bn after tax, against cash costs capped at $925m. The NEOM green-hydrogen venture in Saudi Arabia survived; Air Products instead signed a marketing and distribution agreement with Yara for its renewable ammonia.

The shares rose 8.0% on announcement day, from $271.35 to $293.18. Capital spending guidance for fiscal 2026 came down to about $3.5bn from roughly $4.0bn, and the company is steering toward some $1.5bn a year of ordinary industrial-gas plants.

What the trading business did under that charge was improve. Adjusted operating income of $810m rose 9%, adjusted operating margin widened 110 basis points to 25.6%, adjusted earnings per share of $3.47 rose 12%, and return on capital reached 11.7%. Full-year adjusted earnings guidance went up to $13.39–$13.49. "Having taken additional decisions to further optimize our large project portfolio, we have a clear pathway to reduce capital expenditures and drive continued profitable growth through high-quality, traditional industrial gas projects," chief executive Eduardo Menezes said on 30 July.

Linde's margin gave way while its backlog hit a record

Linde's adjusted operating margin narrowed 60 basis points to 29.5% as cost inflation outran price and productivity. Chief executive Sanjiv Lamba, on 31 July, called it "another solid quarter, generating record sales and EPS while maintaining industry-leading profitability with 29.5% operating margin and 23.5% return on capital." That return was below the roughly 24% of the prior quarter. On the call Lamba put the roughly 30 basis points of decline excluding pass-through mainly on the US homecare business, and described the quarter's 2% price attainment as broad-based and generally tracking local inflation.

The order book did not crack. A long-term US electronics supply agreement lifted the contractual sale-of-gas backlog about $1bn to a record $8.1bn, within a total project pipeline near $11bn, and capital spending is guided to $5.5bn–$6.0bn. Linde fell 5.95% the day it reported.

Where the two de-ratings part company

Both stocks peaked on 2 July, Air Products at $314.19 and Linde at $546.64, and sit 6.0% and 9.4% below those marks; Air Products is still up 14.7% over twelve months.

Price to gross profit has eased to 16.3x trailing from 17.5x in early May while margin, return on capital and guidance all improved, and the forward multiple of earnings is 20.4x. One caveat sits against calling Air Products mispriced: the two now change hands at almost the same trailing enterprise value to cash earnings, 18.9x against Linde's 18.7x, while Air Products earns 11.7% on capital employed and Linde 23.5%. The likelier reading is a market pricing the return gap rather than missing the quarter. In a three-way oligopoly where Linde holds roughly 30% of the global market, Air Liquide about 24% and Air Products some 16%, the asset inside the fence is the moat, and the pressure point is renewal: bulk atmospheric gas contracts in North America and Western Europe get renegotiated as take-or-pay terms expire, which erodes a book without any volume falling.

The answer arrives in a cluster. Linde reports third-quarter results on 29 October and Air Liquide publishes revenue the same morning; Air Products holds its fiscal fourth-quarter call on 5 November. Whichever of them restates backlog or capital spending first will say more about the next decade of fenceline plants than any sales bridge can.

Two freight data sets published on 13 October took away the arithmetic the truckload trade had been running on. DAT's monthly release reported that spot and contract rates including fuel rose for all three equipment types in September, as the monthly national average on-highway diesel price passed $6 a gallon for the first time. Excluding fuel, van and flatbed rates declined in both the spot and the contract market. Only refrigerated pricing rose.

That distinction is the whole business. An asset-based carrier is paid revenue per mile net of fuel surcharge, multiplied by the miles each tractor runs in a week, multiplied by the number of trucks it can put a driver in. DAT estimates the surcharge reached a record 84 cents a mile for dry van in September, up 14 cents from August, inside an all-in spot rate of $3.01. September's rate print was widely read as the capacity squeeze finally reaching price. What reached price was diesel.

None of those 84 cents pays a driver or a depreciation charge.

The prop under Heartland's profit was the resale lot

The other leg under these shares was used-equipment value. ACT Research's September flash put the same-dealer average retail price of a used Class 8 truck down 1.5% from August to $61,000, softening more than seasonally expected, and retail sales fell 7% from August in a second consecutive monthly decline.

Heartland Express, the North Liberty, Iowa short-to-medium-haul dry-van carrier, is where that line does the heaviest lifting. Gains on the sale of revenue equipment ran at 13.6% of June-quarter revenue against a 2015-25 average of 4.1%, and without them the operating ratio would have been nearer 103% than the 91.0% reported. Net income of $10.6m sat above an operating loss of $8.5m. Heartland holds no quarterly earnings call and discloses no rate or utilisation metrics, so the resale line is the clearest window the company offers. The shares trade at 9.17x enterprise value to trailing earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation and 1.13x book, the cheapest book value of the three, while 75.3x on 2026 consensus earnings falls to roughly 21.8x on 2027 — a 245% per-share increase the price already pays for.

Werner is repricing truck-weeks by the year

Werner Enterprises, the Omaha full-truckload and logistics operator, took the other route out of the spot market. More than 70% of its truckload fleet, about 7,400 trucks, sits under dedicated agreements, and the one-way fleet shrank from nearly 3,300 tractors in 2022 to under 2,400 at the end of 2025. Dedicated revenue per tractor per week rose 5.4% net of fuel surcharge in the June quarter, and the company raised dedicated pricing guidance to up 3-5%. The pace is contractual. Renewals should bring "up low to mid-single digit type increases," senior vice-president of pricing Chris Neal said, and chief executive Derek Leathers added that "you need a couple of years of that to really get dedicated back to its traditional long-term run rate".

Reported earnings flatter and then punish. Revenue grew 24.0% to $933.9m in the June quarter, helped by a dedicated-carrier acquisition completed in January. Operating income fell 74.5% to $16.9m, against a prior-year quarter that carried a $45.7m insurance-and-claims reversal. At 9.93x trailing EV/EBITDA, Werner's forward path runs from 29.2x on 2026 consensus to about 14.8x on 2027, on a 97% jump in net income.

Marten's refrigerated rates helped; its dedicated book did not

Marten Transport hauls temperature-controlled food and consumer goods, and was the one name September's data favored: refrigerated spot rates rose 24 cents from August to $3.62 a mile. Its dedicated segment went the other way, the operating ratio deteriorating to 96.4% from 92.4% a year earlier. Revenue fell 2.8% to $223.5m and operating margin compressed to 2.35% from 4.23%. At 8.31x trailing EV/EBITDA, the cheapest of the three, 46.9x on 2026 consensus falls to roughly 19.7x on 2027.

What the de-rating already accounts for

Revenue trajectories improved at all three: Heartland's year-over-year decline narrowed from 26.1% to 12.5% over three quarters, Marten's from 8.8% to 2.8%. Margins compressed at every one of them. The two lines disagree because what improved was the part of the dollar nobody is paid on, and because the capacity story the shares were bought for has no revenue in it yet. "The truck market has certainly flipped this year, but recent tonnage levels confirm this is due to reduced capacity, not robust demand," American Trucking Associations chief economist Bob Costello said in the September tonnage release, which showed the index down 0.9% to 114.2.

Capacity exit sets a floor under future price; it does not fill a quarter. UBS cut Werner and Heartland to hold from buy and JP Morgan moved both to underweight, arguing a truckload rate recovery would be pushed out. With ex-fuel van pricing lower, used-truck values easing and the weekly national diesel average down from its $6.529 peak in the week of 21 September to $6.199 by 5 October, a markdown from an expensive multiple is doing rational work. The near-identical 8.3x-to-9.9x band across three structurally different fleets says the market repriced a shelf.

Diesel has turned back down from its late-September record, which takes the flattery out of the top line before it takes anything out of cost. Marten prints first, after the close on 22 October, and what it reports will carry less surcharge in it than September's rates did.

Fair Isaac, the Bozeman, Montana company whose FICO credit scores lenders buy through Equifax, Experian and TransUnion, committed on 1 October to eliminating about 15% of its positions, a plan carrying roughly $27.0m of pre-tax charges in the fiscal fourth quarter, with affected employees notified from the week of 5 October. Ten days later the Mortgage Bankers Association cut the forecast that governs the company's largest profit line, putting 2027 originations by loan count at 5.0 million against the 5.3 million expected this year, with the 30-year mortgage rate averaging 7.3% in the current quarter and holding above 7% through next year.

September's collapse in the shares was read as a fight over which score lenders are allowed to use. The count is the other half of the machine. The royalty is a price Fair Isaac publishes multiplied by the number of times a lender pulls a score, and the company controls only the first term. The cost cut defends the margin on the dollars that arrive; it does nothing to the number of pulls, and that number is now forecast lower.

The June quarter was a price increase

How much of the growth was price is not a matter of inference. Scores revenue reached $458.9m in the June quarter from $324.3m, up 41%, with the business-to-business half up 49% "primarily attributable to a higher mortgage origination scores unit price"; mortgage-origination revenue rose 97% while mortgage score volumes grew low single digits. Mortgage was 62% of total Scores revenue. The list price behind that: Fair Isaac took its wholesale mortgage royalty from $4.95 to $10.00 a score for 2026, after moving it from $0.60 to $4.95 over the prior five years. A roughly 102% price step produced a 97% revenue gain, so units and mix contributed close to nothing.

The other business bears no resemblance to it. Software revenue grew 2% to $215m, with Platform annual recurring revenue up 62% to $413m and net retention of 148% on Platform against 82% on the older non-Platform book that is shrinking beneath it. One consolidated revenue line cannot say which half moved.

The $10 is harder to collect than it was

On 1 October the Federal Housing Finance Agency moved Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac to a single loan-level price adjustment grid for Classic FICO and VantageScore 4.0, removing a 20-point adjustment that had made the rival model's pricing less favorable. The bureaus that distribute FICO jointly own that rival: TransUnion has cut VantageScore 4.0 to $0.99 a score and supplies it free with a FICO purchase, and Equifax has priced it at $4.50 through end-2027, also free alongside FICO. Fair Isaac's answer routes around them: a direct licence for tri-merge resellers at $4.95 a score plus a $33 funded-loan fee, or $10 flat, the first option described by the company as a 50% reduction in average per-score fees into resellers.

"We're not crazy about lenders choice as a policy. We think it's bad policy because it encourages gaming," chief executive William Lansing said on the 29 July earnings call, adding that volumes were not falling, "which suggests that they're pulling both scores." Pulling both is what United Wholesale Mortgage has said it will do; Rocket Mortgage said in early October it will make VantageScore 4.0 its preferred model on all eligible loans from this quarter.

The cheap number nobody has cut

Nothing in the reported results explains the share price. Revenue grew 25.7% in the June quarter, operating margin widened to 53.8% from 48.9%, and diluted earnings were $10.45 against $7.40. The stock fell 16.7% on 4 September and 29.2% on 29 September, closed at $719.63 on 14 October, 56.4% below a year earlier, then rose 9.2% on 12 October and 6.75% on 13 October as Needham cut its target to $1,100 from $1,650 and Raymond James to $1,230 from $1,750 without cutting ratings.

So the de-rating is about the price list rather than the business as reported, and that is also what makes the valuation conditional. At 20.7x trailing earnings against roughly 62x in January, the shares have lost two-thirds of their multiple; the 14.5x forward figure is computed against a fiscal-2027 consensus of $49.65 a share that embeds the $10 score and has not been revised. Senator Josh Hawley asked the Federal Trade Commission on 23 March to investigate the price increases, a second claim on the same lever.

The fiscal fourth-quarter and full-year report is scheduled for 11 November. It is the first occasion on which Fair Isaac can publish a price list for next year, and the first look at score units since the largest retail mortgage lender in the country said it would prefer somebody else's model.

A 195-Week Wait for Gas Engines Caps Archrock's Fleet and Fills Enerflex's Order Book

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Two gas compression lessors have been marked down hard through the autumn while every meter that sets a compression dollar improved. Kodiak Gas Services' average price reached $23.80 per revenue-generating horsepower per month, up 4.5%, on 98.2% fleet utilisation and record quarterly adjusted EBITDA of $216.8m; Archrock's contract operations revenue rose 3% at a 71% adjusted gross margin.

What soured sits outside the lease: Archrock's parts-and-overhaul book fell 35% year on year, and make-ready and lube-oil costs took $30m off the top of its EBITDA guidance. Enerflex, filed on the same industry shelf, is a manufacturer rather than a lessor — its Engineered Systems backlog went from $1.1bn to $1.5bn — and the engine shortage that caps the lessors' fleet additions is what is filling its factories.

Gas engines for new compressor packages now take about 195 weeks to arrive, and the orders going in are for 2029 delivery. Brad Childers, Archrock's president and chief executive, told investors as much on the company's August call: "For equipment required, lead times are now at 195 weeks with ordering for 2029." The cause sits outside the oilfield. Hyperscale data-centre demand has pushed engine manufacturers to full production, limiting engine availability for the gas compression industry, where waits traditionally ran 40 to 60 weeks.

That one constraint splits a group the market prices as a single shelf. Archrock and Kodiak Gas Services lease horsepower by the month and have been read as geared to the gas price and the drill bit; both inputs moved the wrong way for that reading. Henry Hub was $3.03 per million British thermal units on 6 October, down 8.7% from a year earlier, while the US rig count reached 603, some 56 rigs above last year, with the Permian at 274. Meanwhile the numbers that actually set a lease dollar went up.

A compression dollar is a monthly fee, and the fee rose

Revenue in contract compression is three numbers multiplied: horsepower on revenue-generating contracts, a monthly rate per horsepower, and utilisation. Kodiak, the large-horsepower operator concentrated in the Permian Basin, reported average pricing of $23.80 per revenue-generating horsepower per month in the June quarter, up 4.5% year on year, with compression fleet utilisation at 98.2% against 97.2% and compression adjusted gross margin 1.7 points wider at 70%. Adjusted EBITDA of $216.8m was a company record, up 22%, and Kodiak raised full-year EBITDA and discretionary cash flow guidance in the same release.

Archrock, which operates roughly 35% of the US outsourced compression market, showed the same shape. Contract operations revenue rose 3% to $329.3m, period-end utilisation was 94.4%, and adjusted gross margin was 71% against 70%; the operating fleet shrank from 4.7m horsepower to 4.5m because Archrock sold about 165,000 non-strategic horsepower. The same release disclosed an agreement with an existing strategic customer covering approximately 665,000 horsepower on an eight-year base term with a two-year extension. Compression demand follows the producing well count and the rising gas-to-oil ratio of ageing Permian wells, which calls for more horsepower per barrel even when the oil rig count flattens.

Archrock's shortfall sat in parts and overhauls

Where Archrock did disappoint, the mechanism lives away from the lease. Aftermarket services, the parts and maintenance book billed on customer-owned units, took $42.0m against $64.8m a year earlier, about 11% of group revenue; the company attributed the fall to the absence of non-recurring sales of overhauled engines and to customers skipping the usual mid-year maintenance uptick. Full-year aftermarket revenue is guided to $175m-$185m. The top of EBITDA guidance came down to $885m from $915m on four items: make-ready cost to put idle units back to work, second-half lube-oil pressure, the weaker aftermarket and higher long-term incentive compensation. Contract operations margin guidance held at 71.0%-71.5%. Childers called 2026 "a bit like the calm before the storm".

Enerflex sells what the lessors cannot get

Enerflex earns its money the other way: it manufactures compression and gas-processing packages, recognising Engineered Systems revenue as projects are built, and separately owns build-own-operate infrastructure carrying about $1.2bn of contracted revenue. Engineered Systems bookings were $488m in the June quarter against a trailing eight-quarter average of $363m, a 1.5x book-to-bill for the first half, with backlog up to $1.5bn from $1.1bn at the end of 2025. Then came the power awards: 450 MW of gas-fired generation for a North American data-centre developer on 1 October, and on 13 October more than a gigawatt, taking behind-the-meter backlog above 1.75 GW with deliveries through 2028. Enerflex also authorised roughly $85m of extra Engineered Systems facility investment, mostly for 2027 — a manufacturer adding factory capacity while the lessors stand in the engine queue.

The shares have moved accordingly. Over the three months to 14 October Archrock fell 22.7% and Kodiak 27.5%, while Enerflex rose 6.2% and has been in an uptrend since 7 October. Archrock's turn came at the earnings event itself, from $35.74 on 4 August to $33.08 on 7 August. Kodiak sits 34% below its twelve-month high and 29.5% below the $71.00 at which it sold 10.56m shares for about $758m in May, a raise that lifted diluted shares 10.6% in one quarter to 96.8m.

Cheaper than May, and one of them cheaper still

Archrock at $29.79 trades at 5.97x trailing gross profit, against 7.12x in May, on 9.4x trailing EV/EBITDA and a 5.6% free cash flow yield. Kodiak's forward price-to-earnings is 25.3x, against roughly 29x in May; the honest offset is a 0.10% trailing free cash flow yield and credit-agreement leverage of 3.2x, the cost of a fleet in growth mode.

Enerflex, up 137.5% over twelve months, is the cheapest of the three on price-to-gross-profit at 4.05x forward. That is the comparable measure because the margins differ by a factor of three: 23.9% gross margin at Enerflex against Archrock's disclosed 71% adjusted figure makes price-to-sales meaningless across them.

So the lessors' autumn is a change in what the market will pay for horsepower rather than a change in what horsepower earns. What the results do explain is narrower and real: Archrock's second, thinner dollar shrank by a third, and Kodiak's fastest-growing line is its least profitable, with Other Services revenue up 47% to $43.1m and guided at a 13%-16% adjusted gross margin against 70% on the lease, so mix alone can move reported margin. Kodiak's power optionality is also unsigned. Of the West Texas data-centre project, chief executive Mickey McKee said on the August call: "We've invoiced them for an initial deposit to reserve power equipment for the project while we negotiate a long-term contract." Neither lessor has reported since; Archrock's quarter is expected around 27 October and Kodiak's around 3 November.

Archrock says the industry needs roughly a million more horsepower to meet forecast gas demand growth by the end of the decade, and has set out growth capital predominantly for large-horsepower and electric-motor-drive units. The engines for it are already on order. Whoever holds a place in that queue sets the next lease rate.
