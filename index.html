The licensee

BHEL's heavy-duty gas capability is borrowed. Harbin Electric, which builds boilers, steam turbines, hydro sets and nuclear island components for Chinese power stations, sold RMB873m of gas-fired equipment last year. That was 1.9% of group revenue of RMB45.70bn, and it fell 55.3% from the prior year. Over the same period the shares behaved as though the company were levered to the global scramble for gas turbines: up more than double over twelve months, then down 34% in six.

The stake is what investors thought they owned. Artificial-intelligence data centers have made heavy-duty gas turbines the scarcest capital good in power, and anything with a turbine attached was marked up through 2025. In China the money is somewhere else entirely, and the sell-off since March has not distinguished between a company whose earnings are compounding on coal and nuclear and companies whose earnings have stalled.

What the Chinese order books actually contain

Harbin is the only one of the three that breaks gas out. Coal-fired equipment was RMB18.53bn of 2025 revenue, 40.5% of the group and up 16.2%; nuclear grew 3.5%, hydro fell 8.1%. The gas line had been RMB1.95bn in 2024, so 2025 more than halved something already small. New power-equipment order intake rose 26% to RMB44.0bn, with coal orders up 39.4% and nuclear up 81.6%.

That mix follows the country's construction schedule. China commissioned a record 22.4 GW of gas-fired capacity in 2025, a third of the world's total, yet gas is still only about 4.5% of the national fleet. Meanwhile China is due to commission 85 of the 104 coal units starting up worldwide in 2026, roughly 55 GW, after 30 GW came online in the first half alone.

Shanghai Electric's RMB172.8bn of 2025 new orders itemizes coal at RMB26.59bn, wind at RMB22.97bn, storage and nuclear — and no gas line at all. Elevators, motors and intelligent manufacturing took RMB44.48bn, a quarter of the book. Dongfang buries turbines inside an "energy equipment manufacturing" segment that was RMB58.0bn, or 73.8%, of 2025 revenue.

The technology is borrowed, and one route just closed

Dongfang has been a Mitsubishi licensee since 2003; the Japanese company marked cumulative orders of 150 units through Dongfang Turbine in October 2024 and runs a Guangzhou aftermarket joint venture with it. Its indigenous G50 is a 50MW F-class machine with about ten units booked. At the annual meeting in Chengdu on 29 May, Dongfang disclosed an export agreement signed 30 April with a North American customer for a first phase of ten G50s, and a target of 20 to 30 indigenous turbines a year by early 2028. Management attributed the group's thin margin to fierce wind-power competition and described gas turbines as a business where the long money is earned in service.

Scale the ambition. "We almost doubled the number of gas turbines sold globally, from 100 units in 2024 to 194 units in 2025," Siemens Energy chief executive Christian Bruch said of his own company. Shanghai Electric's window into advanced frames was its 40% stake in Ansaldo Energia, a former Siemens licensee; Ansaldo is now 99.6% owned by Italy's state investor CDP Equity.

Harbin's numbers went the other way from its share price: first-half revenue up 10.1% to RMB25.0bn and attributable profit up 63.5% to RMB1.718bn, gross margin 15.66% against 11.91%. What is actually in the profit pool

Doosan Enerbility still owns 46.06% of Doosan Bobcat and consolidates all of it — the 2024 plan to hive the stake off and fold Bobcat into Doosan Robotics collapsed in December that year after proxy adviser ISS told shareholders to oppose it. Bobcat reported first-half revenue of $3.16bn and gross profit of $777.9m at a 24.6% gross margin. Doosan Enerbility's consolidated first half was ₩8.99tn of revenue and ₩1.55tn of gross profit, a 17.3% blended margin. Converted at this year's dollar-won range of roughly ₩1,428 to ₩1,559, Bobcat is about half the revenue and roughly 70 to 74% of the gross profit — which also means the power businesses run a materially thinner margin than the loaders do.

The turbine business is real, and one part of the challenger case has already been overtaken. The businesses got better while the shares halved

First-half operating profit rose 32.4% to ₩547.8bn, orders rose 89.6% to ₩7.12tn and backlog reached a record ₩26.35tn. The stock fell 55.9% from its May 7 close of ₩136,400 to ₩60,200 on July 30, then rebounded 46.5%. Over the same slide GE Vernova lost 13.9%, Mitsubishi Heavy 17.4% and Siemens Energy 25.2%, and none bounced more than 11%. The cut was attributed to peer de-ratings, not to anything at Doosan.

The cause sits offshore of the turbine cycle. Korean stocks fell 5.98% on July 29 and triggered consecutive trading halts, and the index went from bear to bull market inside a month on the Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix artificial-intelligence trade. Leverage went out and came back.

Hanwha Aerospace was never the turbine bet

Hanwha Aerospace makes engine components for aircraft and marine vessels, and it traced the same 47.3% fall and 42.8% rebound. Its aero-engine division booked ₩760bn of second-quarter revenue and ₩37bn of operating profit, against ₩9,292.9bn and ₩1,365.5bn for the group; ground defense alone delivered ₩2,107.5bn of revenue and ₩533bn of profit on K9 howitzers, the Cheonho anti-aircraft gun and shipments to Poland, Egypt and the Middle East, with backlog at ₩38.3tn after an April contract with Finland. The independent gas-turbine service business PSM belongs to affiliate Hanwha Impact, which unified it with Hanwha Power Systems under the Hanwha Power brand in March; Hanwha Aerospace shareholders own none of it.

At 23.8x forward earnings and 9.4x forward gross profit it is the cheapest of these four names, on 21 to 22% return on equity. The reason is visible: Korea's defense export orders ran near $8bn by end-July against $15.4bn for all of 2025, the big four contractors' combined backlog slipped sequentially to ₩98.5tn, and consensus models a 42% earnings decline in 2029.

The verdict

Neither drawdown was earned by the operating businesses, both of which posted record results through it. But the recovery should not be read as vindication of a turbine thesis either. Doosan's first US frames do not ship until 2029, its 63.5x trailing enterprise value to EBITDA rests on a 0.5% net margin, and the gross profit supporting its multiple is mostly compact equipment sold to American contractors — a cyclical business with no connection to data-center power. Hanwha's cheapness is defense cheapness, priced against an export pipeline that has halved.

The useful test is narrow. If Doosan's power segments cannot lift their margin above the loaders', then the challenger premium is being paid on a subsidiary the company twice tried to give away.

Accenture's last disclosed new bookings were $19.3bn, down 2%, with revenue growth decelerating to 5.6% and headcount up 1%.

Cognizant has a sixth straight quarter of adjusted operating-margin expansion to show for it. Accenture has disclosed nothing at all since June 18.

Accenture agreed this week to buy two more consulting businesses: McCoy & Partners, a Dutch specialist in SAP transformation work, on August 25, and COMWARE, a Tokyo technology-services provider bringing more than 180 professionals, on August 27. Both go into Accenture Edge, the mid-market unit. The Dublin-headquartered firm, which bills large enterprises for cloud, cybersecurity, data and application-modernization programs largely by consultant time, spent the best week of its year buying billable heads.

That is worth noting because the re-rating those purchases landed inside — Accenture's shares are up 38% from their July 15 low, Cognizant's 48% — rests on a proposition neither company will now quantify. Accenture told investors that the first quarter of fiscal 2026 would be the last in which it broke out advanced-AI bookings, on the grounds that the technology is embedded in nearly everything it sells. The final figure was $2.2bn, double the prior year, atop roughly $11.5bn booked cumulatively against $4.8bn of cumulative advanced-AI revenue. Cognizant has never disclosed a generative-AI bookings number at all. The central question — is AI adding contract value faster than it removes billable hours — is no longer answerable from either firm's reporting.

The meter, not the order book

The available proxy is Cognizant's, and it splits. The Teaneck, New Jersey outsourcer, which serves financial services, healthcare, products and communications clients from an offshore-heavy delivery base, reported trailing-twelve-month bookings of $29.1bn, up 5%, a book-to-bill near 1.3 — while bookings signed in the June quarter itself fell 6%. The quarter carried seven deals with total contract value of $100m or more. Longer contracts, not more of them.

What is being sold inside those contracts is also changing shape. Fixed-price and transaction-based work has grown for three consecutive years, and some clients now ask for AI-infused rate cards that embed pre-training and inference costs. "Human effort, platforms, software, frontier services, all bundled for an output which is not effort-based, but outcome-based," chief executive Ravi Kumar S told investors on the July 29 call. He also supplied the demand check: one in four Global 2000 companies have paused AI deployments, and roughly two-thirds have not yet realized measurable productivity gains.

Infosys, the Bangalore-based offshore provider, names the mechanism outright. Management confirmed real deflation on large-deal renewals, with clients demanding productivity give-backs at renewal and increasingly mid-contract, and guided fiscal 2027 constant-currency revenue to 1.5–3% — roughly half a point organic once a 1.7% acquisition contribution is stripped out. Its AI-first revenue reached 8.2% of the total, up from 5.5% two quarters earlier. Real, scaling, and not enough: total revenue grew 2.9%. Infosys trades at 15.0x forward earnings against 14.3x trailing, the market pricing earnings down.

What Accenture has actually shown

Accenture's May-quarter revenue of $18.72bn grew 5.6%, decelerating from 8.3% in February. New bookings were $19.3bn, down 2%. Gross margin was flat near 32.8%. Headcount rose 1%, to 798,739. Fourth-quarter revenue is guided to $17.75bn–$18.4bn. The one place AI leverage shows is unit economics: Accenture's staff generated about $91,500 of revenue each over the past year, up 5.7% on near-flat headcount, against Cognizant's $60,700, up 1.8% on a workforce 12,900 larger than a year ago. Accenture bought that leverage with 11,000 job cuts under a restructuring charged at $865m, chief executive Julie Sweet saying the firm is "exiting on a compression timeline" people for whom reskilling is not a viable path.

The shares have moved on none of this. Accenture has published nothing since June 18; the trigger was the late-July rotation out of AI infrastructure names, already documented here. The sell-side arrived after: Citi lifted its Accenture target to $190 from $135 on August 24 and TD Cowen to $173 from $151 on August 26 — the first already spent at Friday's $189.61 close, the second below it. Accenture's forward multiple has expanded from roughly 10x in late July to 13.7x, against 15.0x trailing and a 10.8% free-cash-flow yield; Cognizant sits at 11.1x forward against a 14–15x reference and 13.7x trailing. Both are cheap against their own history. Neither is cheap because anything improved.

Cognizant earns part of its move: a sixth consecutive quarter of adjusted operating-margin expansion to 16.0%, full-year adjusted earnings guidance raised to $5.70–$5.82, financial-services revenue up 12%, headcount down 900 sequentially, and its trend measures crossed into positive territory on August 26. Accenture earns almost none of it, because it has said nothing. That the offshore ADRs sat out — Infosys up 10%, Wipro flat — argues this is a re-rating of two US-listed names rather than an industry demand recovery.

Accenture's fiscal year closed August 31. The fourth-quarter print lands in late September, date not yet confirmed; last year's equivalent came on September 25. It will be the firm's first word in three months, and the first full-year bookings number with no AI line in it to check.

Originating hypothesis

paired band bottoming labor billed software delivery · subject: ACN, CTSH

The labor-billed rung of enterprise software — the systems integrators paid per consultant-hour to actually deploy the cloud, SaaS and AI platforms this desk has spent the month writing about, and the one layer of the software economy it has never made a protagonist — is quietly bottoming at gradual intensity: Accenture has been marked strongly bearish → mildly bearish on the 30-, 90- and 365-day views while Cognizant has climbed strongly bearish → mildly bullish over 30 days and mildly bearish → mildly bullish in the last week, with neither name in any 1m/3m/6m/12m mover list and no violent session anywhere to explain it; the question worth investigating is the mechanism end to end — whether generative-AI work is genuinely being sold as new, incremental contract value at these firms, visible in disclosed GenAI bookings, total bookings and book-to-bill, or whether the same AI is deflating the pyramid it is billed from, so that headcount, revenue per employee and pricing show a shrinking meter dressed up as a growing order book.
