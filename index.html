The delivery book is no longer the thing breaking

The bill comes first. September-quarter capital spending was $464m, or 41% of revenue, against $433m of operating cash flow. TSMC collected roughly $10,500 of revenue on each twelve-inch-equivalent wafer it shipped in the September quarter. GlobalFoundries, which builds microcontrollers, radio-frequency modems and power-management chips on mature and specialty nodes out of Malta, New York, collected $2,935 on each 300mm-equivalent wafer. United Microelectronics, the Hsinchu pure-play that fabricates other companies' designs at 28nm and above, collected about $1,900 — its own disclosed blended average selling price.

November marked all three down almost as a single trade, on the belief that spending on artificial-intelligence capacity had peaked and that the companies owning the fabs would wear it. Their own quarters record the opposite sequence: revenue growth accelerated at all three through 2026, gross margins widened at all three, December quarters were guided higher and none of them trimmed capital spending. What separates them is not the demand they describe but who is paying for the capacity that serves it.

The ladder from $1,900 to $10,500 a wafer

TSMC's gap is mix. Nodes at 7nm and below were 79% of third-quarter wafer revenue, and high-performance computing rose to 68% of the total. Shipments grew 9.6% from a year earlier while revenue in New Taiwan dollars grew 50.9%, which works out to roughly 38% more revenue per wafer — the quarter was priced rather than shipped. At a 66.4% gross margin, about $7,000 of each wafer's revenue stayed as gross profit.

GlobalFoundries kept $882 a wafer on a 30.1% gross margin against 24.8% a year earlier, shipping 647,000 wafers with selling prices up 4.8%. "This marked the first quarter of year-over-year blended pricing improvement since 2023," chief financial officer Sam Franklin told investors on November 4. UMC kept about $655 a wafer, on a record 1.21m wafers and utilization of 92% against 85% in the June quarter; its blended price was flat quarter to quarter, with 22nm reaching 18.3% of wafer sales.

Cash flow, prepayments, balance sheet

TSMC pays for itself. Third-quarter operating cash flow of NT$919.8bn covered NT$443.2bn of capital expenditure 2.1 times, with full-year capex reiterated at $60-64bn and 2026-2028 spending described as significantly higher than the past three years. It is also raising price: customers have been told wafer prices go up 3-6% from January 2027, with 2nm and 3nm-class nodes taking the most. "The gap between demand and supply are still quite big, and that is why we put all our strengths to increase the capacity," chairman and chief executive C.C. Wei said on October 15.

GlobalFoundries shares the bill. "We now expect net CapEx investments of approximately $4 billion into our manufacturing footprint in 2027, while continuing to deliver our free cash generation targets," Franklin said; about a third of that is to be supported by customer prepayments for incremental capacity, alongside roughly $760m of CHIPS Act grant money and a 35% investment tax credit. Third-quarter free cash flow of $136m already included about $150m of prepayments and grants. Chief executive Tim Breen put the book behind it plainly on the same call: "We are absolutely booked out through 2027, increasingly booked out through 2028."

UMC carries its own. The $2.0bn 2026 cash capex budget sits on its balance sheet, as does a newly board-approved $3.2bn for silicon photonics, deep-trench capacitors and thin-film lithium niobate, with first installations late in 2028. "For gross margins for next two years at least, depreciation, along with the rising raw material cost, is going to put pressures on our overall financial gross margin," chief financial officer Chitung Liu said on October 28. Chief executive Jason Wang's answer is price: a blended increase of about 10% year over year beginning in the first quarter of 2027.

The markdown nobody's estimates followed

Through the thirty days to November 25, TSMC fell 19.3%, GlobalFoundries 19.6% and UMC 31.2%. Three-quarters of TSMC's decline landed in two sessions, November 5 and November 20, the latter the day Nvidia dropped about 14% as the artificial-intelligence trade broke down over returns on the data-center build-out. Nvidia itself was down 30.9% over the same window. The de-rating belonged to the whole complex.

What it left is three different price levels. TSMC trades at 17.3 times trailing gross profit, against 21.9x in August, and about 14.7 times FY2027 consensus earnings. GlobalFoundries is at 9.7 times trailing gross profit against 13.8x in August and 1.67x book, having touched a 52-week low near $34.05 on November 25 as brokers cut targets — Needham to $55 from $80, Wedbush to $45 from $72 — while consensus FY2027 earnings of $2.55 a share still imply 28% growth. UMC's headline 13.0 times trailing earnings is the least reliable number of the three: a NT$30.05bn investment gain in the June quarter, an equity-portfolio mark, accounts for roughly NT$11.9 of its NT$33.95 trailing earnings per share, which puts the core figure nearer 19-20x.

So the compression sits in the multiple, and the operating record under it is intact: TSMC's October revenue rose 59.7% year on year and UMC's 18.3%. The difference the per-wafer arithmetic exposes is protection rather than growth. TSMC sets price on capacity it funds from its own cash flow. GlobalFoundries' volume sits under long-term agreements carrying take-or-pay reservations and prepayments, including a five-year, $2bn silicon-interposer agreement with TSMC that ramps in 2028, so a capacity pause elsewhere lands partly on its customers' balance sheets.

UMC has no such cushion. Its relief arrives with the January price list. The depreciation on the fabs it is building out of its own cash arrives whether the demand does or not.

Corsair Gaming told investors that its memory business had just posted a record quarter and that the margin it earns on that business will be roughly half last year's. Logitech International, buying into the same component market, described the opposite arrangement: supply bought forward for the year and a price list raised in May. Both sell gaming hardware. Only one of them is reselling the shortage.

The pair has been read as a post-pandemic recovery finally turning up. The quarters say something narrower. At Corsair, the growth in the revenue line is the cost shock, arriving at the expense of everything the same customer used to buy alongside it; at Logitech, the shock is a decision about what to charge. The distinction decides which company's reported growth survives a turn in memory contract prices, which TrendForce projects rising 10-15% quarter over quarter in the fourth quarter as cloud buyers absorb capacity.

Nearly half of Corsair's revenue is now a price it does not set

Corsair, which sells peripherals and Elgato streaming gear alongside power supplies, cooling, cases, branded memory modules and pre-built PCs, reported third-quarter revenue of $367.0m, up 6.2%, with memory revenue of $167.3m. "Memory revenue was a record for a third quarter at $167.3 million, up 43% year-over-year," chief executive Thi La told investors on the third-quarter call. "Demand was strong, prices were higher, and we gained market share sequentially."

The share is gained inside a small residual: Kingston has held 62% of the global memory module market for a 23rd consecutive year, with Corsair, G.Skill, Crucial and ADATA splitting what is left behind it.

Memory is now 45.6% of group revenue against 33.8% a year ago, and the rest of the segment paid for it. Computed from the segment lines in the same report, components and systems excluding memory fell from roughly $117.1m to about $83.2m, a decline near 29%: memory inflation added some $50m of revenue while the hardware beside it lost about $34m. Management reads the weakness as deferred rather than lost, which is the same enthusiast declining a $550-plus 32GB kit and declining a case and a cooler with it.

Gross margin fell to 26.7% from 33.2% in the June quarter. Gross profit grew 5.1% against revenue growth of 6.2%, and the company recorded a GAAP operating loss of $1.6m on a quarter that beat its own revenue guidance by $17m. La raised the full-year revenue midpoint $36m. Chief financial officer Gordon Mattingly, on the same call, put the other half on the table: "Last year's fourth quarter was unusually high at about 35% due to average selling prices rising rapidly. We expect memory gross margin of approximately 18%-20% this year, which is still above our historical average."

Logitech answered with a price increase and a purchase order

Logitech's memory exposure sits in video-collaboration room systems, not in mice. Chief financial officer Matteo Anversa told the October 27 call the company had proactively secured supply, which is why inventory is elevated and turns have fallen to 4.8 from 5.2, and that memory components for video conferencing were "pretty much secured throughout the full fiscal year 2027". It raised video-collaboration prices in May; the line's 12% constant-currency growth last quarter split roughly 60% price, 40% volume.

That is the posture of a price-setter, and the share data supports the claim to be one: Logitech leads gaming peripherals at roughly 19% against Razer's 16%, with Corsair near 7-10%. Mouse share rose 320 basis points in the quarter on management's own count.

It did not spare the stock. Logitech reported September-quarter sales of $1.22bn, up 3%, with non-GAAP gross margin of 45.2% and a beat on earnings, then guided the December quarter to down 5% to flat with gross margin of 41-42%, about 200 basis points below last year. Roughly 150 of those points are inflation, split about half freight and half materials. The guide also absorbs $70-80m from a semiconductor supplier's manufacturing incident that closed a facility in late June, cut from an initial estimate of up to $200m through secondary sourcing and redesign. The shares shed about $2bn of market value in a single session, which Vontobel's Michael Foeth attributed to the margin guide and soft European demand.

The market paid for the pass-through and marked down the list price

Over twelve months Corsair has nearly doubled and Logitech has fallen about 10%, and the earnings do not carry much of either. Corsair's price-to-trailing-gross-profit is 2.79x against roughly 1.72x a year ago, so only about 19 percentage points of that advance came from gross-profit growth. Its 13.0x on fiscal 2026 consensus of $0.91 becomes 14.2x on the fiscal 2027 consensus of $0.83, which is 9% lower: the street already treats this year as the peak. Logitech trades at 17.9x trailing and 17.5x forward, cheaper than the 19.0x it closed its last fiscal year at, but consensus has fiscal 2028 earnings only about 3% above fiscal 2027, and the company returned $406m in the September quarter against a $14.4bn market value.

The cause of the split is the invoice. Corsair's growth is denominated in a price the chipmakers set; Logitech's is denominated in a list it publishes, which is why its gross profit grew 6.3% on 2.8% revenue growth while Corsair's did the reverse. Neither escapes the demand side: IDC cut its 2026 global PC shipment forecast to a decline of 11.3% while still expecting market value to rise to $274bn on 17% price growth — fewer units, dearer ones, which is precisely the shape Corsair reported.

A pass-through runs both ways. In late November the memory complex broke hard, with Micron down about 13% in a session as the same researcher trimmed its price outlook. Cheaper kits would release the builds that buy Corsair's cases and coolers, and deflate the line that now supplies nearly half its revenue, and mark down the memory it deliberately stockpiled for the holidays. Nothing in the company's disclosure says which leg lands first.

Texas Instruments told investors on October 21 that its industrial chip revenue had reached its third-quarter 2022 peak for the first time in four years, and that analog capacity outside China and Taiwan is tight because almost none has been added over that span. Back-end test and assembly tool lead times are extending on top of that. Three weeks later the shelf those parts sit on was being marked down as though the opposite were happening.

The markdown was read as a second analog inventory correction arriving: order books cooling, distributors stuffed, the 2024 trough repeating. Every channel figure the four largest Western analog and embedded-control makers disclosed this autumn points the other way. The binding problem is that high-margin orders already placed cannot be built, because the packaging, test and foundry capacity they need has been taken by artificial-intelligence customers paying for it first.

Nobody is building a buffer

Microchip Technology, which sells microcontrollers, analog and timing parts into automotive, industrial and data-center systems, reported on November 5 that its distributor inventory stood at 25 days, flat sequentially and at the low end of a ten-fiscal-year range of roughly 17 to 43 days. Its own lead times have stretched from a historical four to eight weeks out to six to 32, and high-margin orders it could not fulfil in the December quarter were pushed into March. Of the 184 days of inventory on its own balance sheet, 12 days are last-time buys of long-life-cycle parts whose manufacturing capacity its suppliers have discontinued.

Analog Devices, which sells data converters, amplifiers and power management into industrial, automotive, defense and communications sockets largely through distributors, said on November 24 that channel inventory had returned to six to seven weeks, back inside its target model after sitting below six, and that book-to-bill was "nicely above 1x" in almost all end markets. It attributed roughly 30% of revenue — aerospace and defense, automated test equipment, data center — running above consumption to better end demand, with no material restocking detected.

NXP Semiconductors makes the same claim from a different base. "The book to bill remains well above 1 across all end markets and regions. Our backlog continues to grow in dollars and extend further into 2027," chief financial officer William J. Betts told investors on October 27. Eighty percent of NXP's lead times now exceed 16 weeks, against 25% a year earlier, and chief executive Rafael Sotomayor said customers discuss safety stock but that it has yet to occur.

That is three independent confirmations that the channel between the fabs and real consumption is not refilling, against a fourth company reporting its industrial line at a four-year high.

The markdown sorted by cash, not by orders

In the 30 days to November 25, NXP fell 1.2% and Texas Instruments 9.4%, while Analog Devices fell 18.3% and Microchip 24.0%. The order books moved together; the prices did not.

What separated them is who has to spend. Analog Devices guided fiscal-2027 capital expenditure to $1.6bn-$1.9bn against $733m for clean-room and back-end test capacity whose first output arrives as early as fiscal first quarter 2028 — roughly 11% to 12% of a revenue base guided at about $4.4bn a quarter, derived from its own guidance. Trailing free cash flow yields 3.4% against a standing commitment to return all of it. Its shares fell 17.4% close-to-close on November 24, a day the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index dropped 7.88% after rising 14.03% the session before, and coverage attributed the fall to the spending plan and a flat sequential guide rather than to the quarter, in which revenue grew 42.2% year on year and operating margin reached 42.7% from 30.7%.

Microchip's constraint is its balance sheet rather than its budget. Net debt runs at 2.36 times earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization, buybacks are suspended pending further deleveraging and the dividend is held flat. Its margin recovery is pure absorption: non-GAAP gross margin climbed from 52% in the March 2025 quarter to 60.5% by December and 66.5% in September as under-utilization charges shrank with volume. The guide down to 64%-65% is $53m of one-time items booked at full margin not repeating, plus a seasonally lower-utilization quarter.

What the results account for, and what they do not

The reported businesses account for none of the markdown. Both protagonists grew revenue 42.2% year on year in their latest quarters, each the fourth or fifth consecutive acceleration, and neither cut on demand. What the results do not cover is the cash: a doubled fab budget at Analog Devices with no revenue attached until 2028, and a leveraged Microchip absorbing foundry and assembly price increases from January rather than passing them on again. On forward earnings Analog Devices trades at 19.5 times against 32.4 times trailing, and against roughly 58 times trailing in August. Microchip's 16.0 times forward sits against 43.2 times on trough earnings of $0.22 a share in fiscal 2026.

Chief executive Steve Sanghi was blunt about the demand everyone else is buying. "I have been saying it, that data center is in the bubble," he told analysts on November 5 — from a company where data center is 16.7% of sales and industrial grew 50.3%.

Both companies report again around early February. The test is narrow: whether the parts customers have been waiting four months for can be shipped before anyone decides they no longer need them.
