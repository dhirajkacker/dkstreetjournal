Dodging Apple and Google's Cut Is the Only Growth Left in Social Casino Games

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Two mobile casino operators lost players in the September quarter and each posted its best margin yet, because the money now arrives through their own web stores, where Apple and Google take no cut of roughly 30% of what a player spends. Playtika's direct-to-consumer sales reached 40.8% of revenue; DoubleDown collects 58% of its social-casino revenue that way, and its average payer now spends $216 a month against $272 a year earlier.

Revenue stopped growing at both. The payment shift explains the margins and leaves the share prices to dated events: DoubleDown's leap came when its Korean parent offered $16.25 in cash per American depositary share for the minority it does not own, and Playtika's came when its board ended a sale process and kept the company public.

The two listed companies that sell virtual chips redeemable for nothing both lost players this year, and both just reported the fattest margins in their history. The explanation sits on the checkout page. Playtika, the Israeli publisher of Slotomania and Bingo Blitz, took $275.1m of its September-quarter revenue through its own direct-to-consumer stores, 40.8% of the total and up 31.4% from a year earlier, while operating income rose 16.3% on revenue that did not grow.

Record profitability at a shrinking company reads as proof the games monetize better. The filings say something narrower: this is a plumbing dividend. A payment taken on an operator's own website avoids the roughly 30% fee Apple and Google charge inside an app, and both companies have already overshot where the research firm Eilers & Krejcik expects the whole category to be at the end of the decade. Its 2026 forecast has direct-to-consumer rising from about 18% of social-casino spending in 2024 to over 35% by 2028, while the market itself declines almost 7% this year toward $8.4bn of gross revenue.

Fewer players, squeezed harder

Playtika's average daily active users fell 13.4% to 7.1m and its average daily paying users 5.9% to 333,000, yet revenue per daily user rose to $1.03 from $0.89. Adjusted earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization reached a record $252.1m, a 37.4% margin against 32.2%. "This quarter, we delivered $673.8 million in revenue and a record of $252.1 million in adjusted EBITDA," Robert Antokol, the co-founder and chief executive, said on 5 November. Reported growth across 2026 ran 5.5%, then 5.0%, then -0.1%. "When we discussed DTC a year ago, we set a goal of reaching 40% on a run rate basis within two years," chief financial officer Tae Lee told investors on the same call. "We reached it well ahead of that timeline."

DoubleDown Interactive, the Seoul-based publisher of DoubleDown Casino and, through its SuprNation unit, licensed real-money gambling sites in Europe, is further down the same road. Direct payments were 58% of social-casino revenue in the quarter against 21% a year earlier, payer conversion improved to 9.5% from 7.8%, and the adjusted EBITDA margin reached 42.7% from 39.1%. Consolidated revenue still fell 1.6% to $94.3m, with social casino down 3.5% to $76.8m and the real-money arm up 7.4% to $17.4m, now about 18% of the group. Average monthly revenue per payer dropped to $216 from $272, which management puts down to the acquired WHOW Games audience converting more often for less.

"It is definitely true that we have fewer players today than we've had in the past," chief financial officer Joseph A. Sigrist said on 11 November, adding later that direct sales were below 5% of social-casino revenue in early 2024 and "now we're nearing 60%... I don't think we're finished."

No bargain in the install auction

The tempting companion story is that the state crackdown on sweepstakes casinos handed these two cheaper players. It is not in the numbers. Dedicated bans were in force in California, New York, New Jersey, Louisiana, Indiana, Maine, Tennessee and Oklahoma as of early November, yet DoubleDown's sales and marketing spend was roughly flat at 16.0% of revenue and rose from the June quarter, and Sigrist has said the main effect of the sweepstakes business on DoubleDown is upward pressure on cost per install and advertising. Playtika's own step-down, to 24.9% of revenue from about 30.6%, is a deliberate exit from low-retention channels: Bingo Blitz revenue fell 18.2% to $133m as a direct result.

So the margin story is real and shared, and it accounts for the direction of both share prices without accounting for their timing. Playtika's gain arrived in three sessions around the 5 November print, which paired the record margin with the board's decision, after a review relaunched in April, to continue as an independent public company. DoubleDown was slightly lower over the month until 9 November, when it rose 15.6% in one session on 1.9m shares against a norm nearer 100,000, because controlling shareholder DoubleU Games had agreed to an all-cash tender offer at $16.25 per American depositary share for the minority, conditioned on reaching 95% of common shares.

That offer is the part that matters for anyone still holding the stock. DoubleDown carried $582.1m of cash and short-term investments at the end of September, $545.3m net of parent borrowings, around 70% of its $779m market value, and the bid stands 3.4% above the 11 November close while capping the minority's claim on that pile. The shares change hands at 6.1x forward earnings and 0.77x book. Playtika's 11.9x forward earnings and 0.35x forward sales price in no growth at all, which is what consensus models, and the business carries roughly $1.78bn of net debt against a $980m equity, with a $1.805bn term loan due 2028 and $600m of notes due March 2029; the regular dividend was discontinued this year. Lee told investors the company expects more than $400m of free cash flow for the full year.

A calculation from the two disclosures puts about $674 a month of revenue behind each Playtika payer against DoubleDown's reported $216, a gap of game mix rather than of execution. Both operators are now doing the same thing to the same kind of customer: keeping the few who remain, and keeping more of what they spend.

The arithmetic has a ceiling. Direct sales cannot pass 100% of revenue, and at 58% DoubleDown is closer to it than to the industry's 2028 expectation. When the last payer has been moved off the app store, the shrinking audience becomes the whole of the story again.
