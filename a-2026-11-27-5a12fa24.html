Analog Chips Now Take Four Months to Deliver, and Buyers Are Not Stockpiling Them

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

November's markdown across analog silicon was read as the inventory correction starting over. The order books of the four largest Western makers say the constraint is the opposite one: they cannot build what has already been ordered.

Texas Instruments' industrial revenue reached its 2022 peak again in the September quarter. NXP says most of its lead times now run beyond 16 weeks against a quarter of them a year ago, and its chief financial officer puts book-to-bill above 1 in every end market. Analog Devices' distributors hold six to seven weeks of stock, back inside its target model; Microchip's hold 25 days, flat and near the bottom of a ten-year range.

What separated the share prices was capital, not demand. Analog Devices guided fiscal-2027 capital spending to $1.6bn-$1.9bn from $733m with no output until fiscal 2028 and fell hardest of the four; NXP, utilization in the mid-80s, barely moved.

Texas Instruments told investors on October 21 that its industrial chip revenue had reached its third-quarter 2022 peak for the first time in four years, and that analog capacity outside China and Taiwan is tight because almost none has been added over that span. Back-end test and assembly tool lead times are extending on top of that. Three weeks later the shelf those parts sit on was being marked down as though the opposite were happening.

The markdown was read as a second analog inventory correction arriving: order books cooling, distributors stuffed, the 2024 trough repeating. Every channel figure the four largest Western analog and embedded-control makers disclosed this autumn points the other way. The binding problem is that high-margin orders already placed cannot be built, because the packaging, test and foundry capacity they need has been taken by artificial-intelligence customers paying for it first.

Nobody is building a buffer

Microchip Technology, which sells microcontrollers, analog and timing parts into automotive, industrial and data-center systems, reported on November 5 that its distributor inventory stood at 25 days, flat sequentially and at the low end of a ten-fiscal-year range of roughly 17 to 43 days. Its own lead times have stretched from a historical four to eight weeks out to six to 32, and high-margin orders it could not fulfil in the December quarter were pushed into March. Of the 184 days of inventory on its own balance sheet, 12 days are last-time buys of long-life-cycle parts whose manufacturing capacity its suppliers have discontinued.

Analog Devices, which sells data converters, amplifiers and power management into industrial, automotive, defense and communications sockets largely through distributors, said on November 24 that channel inventory had returned to six to seven weeks, back inside its target model after sitting below six, and that book-to-bill was "nicely above 1x" in almost all end markets. It attributed roughly 30% of revenue — aerospace and defense, automated test equipment, data center — running above consumption to better end demand, with no material restocking detected.

NXP Semiconductors makes the same claim from a different base. "The book to bill remains well above 1 across all end markets and regions. Our backlog continues to grow in dollars and extend further into 2027," chief financial officer William J. Betts told investors on October 27. Eighty percent of NXP's lead times now exceed 16 weeks, against 25% a year earlier, and chief executive Rafael Sotomayor said customers discuss safety stock but that it has yet to occur.

That is three independent confirmations that the channel between the fabs and real consumption is not refilling, against a fourth company reporting its industrial line at a four-year high.

The markdown sorted by cash, not by orders

In the 30 days to November 25, NXP fell 1.2% and Texas Instruments 9.4%, while Analog Devices fell 18.3% and Microchip 24.0%. The order books moved together; the prices did not.

What separated them is who has to spend. Analog Devices guided fiscal-2027 capital expenditure to $1.6bn-$1.9bn against $733m for clean-room and back-end test capacity whose first output arrives as early as fiscal first quarter 2028 — roughly 11% to 12% of a revenue base guided at about $4.4bn a quarter, derived from its own guidance. Trailing free cash flow yields 3.4% against a standing commitment to return all of it. Its shares fell 17.4% close-to-close on November 24, a day the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index dropped 7.88% after rising 14.03% the session before, and coverage attributed the fall to the spending plan and a flat sequential guide rather than to the quarter, in which revenue grew 42.2% year on year and operating margin reached 42.7% from 30.7%.

Microchip's constraint is its balance sheet rather than its budget. Net debt runs at 2.36 times earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization, buybacks are suspended pending further deleveraging and the dividend is held flat. Its margin recovery is pure absorption: non-GAAP gross margin climbed from 52% in the March 2025 quarter to 60.5% by December and 66.5% in September as under-utilization charges shrank with volume. The guide down to 64%-65% is $53m of one-time items booked at full margin not repeating, plus a seasonally lower-utilization quarter.

What the results account for, and what they do not

The reported businesses account for none of the markdown. Both protagonists grew revenue 42.2% year on year in their latest quarters, each the fourth or fifth consecutive acceleration, and neither cut on demand. What the results do not cover is the cash: a doubled fab budget at Analog Devices with no revenue attached until 2028, and a leveraged Microchip absorbing foundry and assembly price increases from January rather than passing them on again. On forward earnings Analog Devices trades at 19.5 times against 32.4 times trailing, and against roughly 58 times trailing in August. Microchip's 16.0 times forward sits against 43.2 times on trough earnings of $0.22 a share in fiscal 2026.

Chief executive Steve Sanghi was blunt about the demand everyone else is buying. "I have been saying it, that data center is in the bubble," he told analysts on November 5 — from a company where data center is 16.7% of sales and industrial grew 50.3%.

Both companies report again around early February. The test is narrow: whether the parts customers have been waiting four months for can be shipped before anyone decides they no longer need them.
