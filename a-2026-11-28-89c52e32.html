Millicom Paid $99m to Settle With Four Ex-Guatemala Executives, Halving Quarterly Profit

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Millicom's third quarter reads as decay in exactly one line and as a record in every other, and the share price has sided with the one line. Profit attributable to shareholders fell to $98m from $195m, almost entirely because the company booked a $99m settlement with four former executives of its Guatemalan business and $20m of Colombian integration severance.

Underneath, adjusted earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization set a quarterly record of $1.1bn, up 7.7% organically, net leverage fell to 2.53x, and Colombia — now the group's largest market, bigger than Guatemala — posted its best-ever margin at 44.2%.

The operating record does not explain a fall of nearly a quarter from August. What might: América Móvil's Claro grew faster than Tigo in both core markets, and on 6.87x enterprise value to EBITDA against Claro's parent at 5.36x, Millicom is not the cheap one.

Millicom International Cellular, which sells mobile service, fixed broadband and mobile money under the Tigo brand in ten Latin American markets, halved its third-quarter profit by settling with four men who used to run its Guatemalan business. Profit attributable to shareholders came in at $98m against $195m a year earlier, and earnings of $0.58 a share missed what analysts expected by more than 40%. Nearly every operating line in the same quarter set a record.

The stock has been held as a Central American dollar annuity carrying a risky Colombian acquisition. The filings describe the reverse arrangement. Colombia, bought from Telefónica this year, is now the largest market in the group at $908m of quarterly service revenue, roughly 2.3 times Guatemala's $392m, and only three of the ten markets — Panama, El Salvador and Ecuador — use the dollar at all. Guatemala transacts in quetzales, Honduras in lempiras.

What the $99m bought

The charge sits in other non-operating expenses: $99m for settlement agreements with four former executives of Tigo Guatemala, of which $31m was paid in the quarter and $68m remains payable over seven years. It resolved all pending material litigation and claims involving those parties, which management called the removal of a significant source of financial and regulatory uncertainty. The concentrated country risk in this business is fiscal rather than currency: at the end of September the estimated tax risk exposure of Millicom's subsidiaries stood at $378m, against $60m of provisions recorded.

The Colombian book earned its best margin while being absorbed

Colombian service revenue grew 7.5% organically and adjusted EBITDA of $417m landed at a 44.2% margin, the highest Millicom has recorded there, while the quarter still carried $20m of integration severance. The Tigo brand consolidation in Colombia took effect on 14 October.

"Following the acquisition of Coltel, our teams moved quickly to integrate operations, simplify our business, improve efficiencies and implement our commercial strategy," chief financial officer Bart Vanhaeren told investors on 5 November, describing the result as "bringing up the acquired lower-margin business from Coltel to our highest EBITDA margin in Colombia ever despite still incurring integration costs." Net leverage fell to 2.53x including Colombia, Ecuador and Uruguay, inside the sub-2.5x year-end path, and the $3.00 annual dividend stayed in four equal installments.

Growth comes from re-pricing the prepaid base

What one Tigo customer pays rose about a fifth: group mobile monthly revenue per user reached $7.4 from $6.2. The engine is migration, not new SIM cards. Postpaid net additions of 390,000 took that base to 17.2m, and 73% of postpaid gross sales came from existing prepaid users, a conversion rate of 87% in Guatemala.

Two lines went the other way. Millicom lost 86,000 home customers sequentially, attributed about one-third to the Colombian earthquake and two-thirds to deliberate pricing, and business-to-business revenue of $404m grew only 2.1% organically as large government projects in Medellín and Panama rolled off, with further roll-off flagged for the fourth quarter.

The decline belongs to Millicom alone

The shares have fallen 14.9% over three months and 23.8% from a closing high of $104.31 on 6 August, with the downtrend dating from 5 November, the day results landed. One session, 12 November, took 9.6% on 4.8m shares, roughly six times the prior month's daily average, a week after results and two days after a UBS upgrade. Over the same three months Liberty Latin America rose 14.0%, TIM Brasil 16.5% and América Móvil 6.9%.

So the operating record accounts for almost none of the fall, and the settlement accounts for the earnings line without touching cash generation: equity free cash flow rose 27% to $316m, with roughly $870m banked against about $1.1bn guided for the year. What the price may instead be discounting is share. In the same quarter América Móvil's Claro grew Central American service revenue 11.4% and the region's EBITDA 16.1%, and Colombian postpaid service revenue 12%, ahead of Tigo's 6.5% in Guatemala and 7.5% in Colombia. Nor is Millicom the discounted asset of the group: at 6.87x trailing enterprise value to EBITDA it is dearer than América Móvil at 5.36x, and the case for the fall being an error rests on the one-off charges not recurring — against next year's consensus earnings of $6.80 a share, the stock stands near 11.7x by our arithmetic, versus 21.1x on this year's charge-depressed base.

The largest holder is not waiting for that argument to settle. Xavier Niel's Atlas Investissement owns 49.5% of the shares outstanding excluding treasury and entered a derivative on 24 November for up to 1,677,459 more — settlement conditional on regulatory clearances and on Millicom confirming that crossing half the company would not accelerate any of its material debt.

Originating hypothesis

single ticker band breakdown dollarized arpu vs peso integration mechanics · subject: TIGO

Millicom is the one Latin American carrier on this shelf still breaking down rather than repairing, and the one the Desk has never examined as a business — TIGO has stepped strongly bullish to strongly bearish across the 90-, 180- and 365-day windows and mildly bearish to strongly bearish inside 30 days, with no appearance in any 1-month, 3-month, 6-month or 12-month mover list, so this is a gradual year-long de-rating rather than a pop — and rather than another regional-discount or price-versus-business divergence (the shape already run on Liberty Latin America, América Móvil and TIM Brasil, where Millicom appeared only as a trend-band mention and none of its own numbers were load-bearing), the question is a what-does-one-Tigo-customer-actually-pay-and-in-what-currency question told end to end for one company: Millicom earns the majority of its service revenue in fully dollarized Guatemala, Honduras, Panama, El Salvador and Costa Rica, markets where it bought out its Comcel partners to reach full ownership and where mobile is overwhelmingly prepaid, so the revenue line carries no FX translation at all but does carry concentrated regulatory and tax exposure the "LatAm" label conceals, bolted onto a Colombian fixed-and-mobile book priced in pesos that it has just enlarged by absorbing Telefónica's Colombian operation, financed with dollar debt against a stated leverage target and an equity-free-cash-flow figure management uses to size buybacks and its reinstated dividend, with towers and spectrum partly sold or leased back — so the thing to settle is whether the dollarized Central American cash annuity, the Colombian integration or the balance sheet is what actually deteriorated.
