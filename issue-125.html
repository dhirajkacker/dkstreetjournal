Subsidized Mexican Buses Are Why Volaris Could Not Pass On Its Fuel Bill and Copa Could

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Latin America's five listed carriers all bought the same dollar-priced jet fuel this year, and the share of the increase each got back from passengers ranged from about 86% at Copa Holdings to 49% at Volaris. The sorting variable is the currency the fare is written in: Copa prices dollar itineraries through Panama with only 15-20% of costs in local money, while Volaris's peso domestic fares are capped by subsidized long-distance buses and Azul's reais lost 15.6% against the dollar after Brazil's October election.

November's rally treated the group as one trade as Brent fell about 22% from its late-October peak. The business evidence splits them: Copa still earned an 18.6% operating margin and cut full-year guidance to 16-17%, Volaris beat its own EBITDA margin guidance at 25.3% on a net loss, and Azul reported record unit revenue with operating income down 32.6%. Copa's shares have re-rated to 10.0x trailing earnings from 7.4x at end-2025 while consensus 2026 earnings fell.

Jet fuel across Latin America reached about $4.61 a gallon by mid-October, 108.6% above a year earlier, taking fuel from 28% of the region's airline operating cost to 38%. What sorted the five listed carriers through that shock was not the fuel bill, which everyone pays in dollars, but the currency the fare is written in. Copa Holdings, which sells connecting itineraries through a single Panama hub where tickets are priced in dollars, recovered roughly 86% of its fuel increase last quarter. Volaris, the Mexican ultra-low-cost carrier, recovered 49%.

The market has been reading the group as one position. Brent is down about 22% from a late-October peak above $117 a barrel to $90.97 on 25 November, and in the 30 days to 27 November every one of the five rose, from LATAM Airlines at 9.5% to Volaris at 36.8%. The recapture numbers describe three different businesses, and the largest gains went to the two that passed on the least.

Dollar in, dollar out

Copa's September-quarter revenue rose 31.9% to $1.204bn, with unit revenue up 13.5% to 12.6 cents and load factor at 88.7%. Its fuel cost rose 61.5%. "Strong demand and higher yields enable us to recover approximately 86% of the increase in fuel-related expense in the quarter through higher revenues," chief financial officer Peter Donkersloot Ponce told investors on 19 November. Only 15-20% of Copa's costs sit in local currencies; the main driver of its 1.1% rise in ex-fuel unit cost was Colombian peso appreciation.

Even so, the operating margin fell to 18.6% from 23.2%, gross profit grew 9.3% on that 31.9% revenue gain, and the company cut 2026 operating-margin guidance to 16-17% on a $3.90-a-gallon fuel assumption. Net debt is 0.8x EBITDA against $1.6bn of cash. The shares now trade at 10.0x trailing and 10.3x forward earnings, against 7.4x at the end of 2025 and 6.0x at the end of 2024 — the forward figure sits above trailing because consensus 2026 earnings of $14.95 a share are 9.7% below last year's $16.28.

A bus timetable sets the Mexican fare ceiling

Volaris crossed $1bn of quarterly revenue for the first time, up 32.4%, with unit revenue up 19% to 10.31 cents and an EBITDA margin of 25.3% against its own 22% guidance. It still lost $26m.

The split inside that number is the story. Transborder US-Mexico revenue grew 53% and fully covered its fuel; peso-denominated domestic fares covered only 20-25%, because subsidized long-distance diesel buses cap what Volaris can charge Mexicans. So the airline is moving revenue into the currency its leases and fuel are in: international reaches 49% of fourth-quarter capacity, and on published fourth-quarter schedules Volaris is the largest carrier in the transborder market by seats. Grounded Pratt & Whitney-powered aircraft averaged 21 in the quarter against 41 at the start of the year, with compensation covering most fixed costs of each engine off wing but not lost revenue. The fleet still shrinks, from 154 to about 137 by end-2027, cutting lease liabilities from $3.2bn to $2.8bn.

The peso gave no help: it weakened from 18.80 to a 19.70 reference rate on 26 November, 8.8% down on the year. At 3.9x trailing EV/EBITDA and 0.26x forward sales, with consensus still modeling losses until 2028, the stock remains below its $8.88 close at end-2025. Mexico's competition authority was reported in October as set to approve the Volaris-Viva Aerobus merger before year-end.

Azul recaptured half, in a currency that fell 15%

Azul produced record unit revenue of BRL0.4723 per available seat kilometer and flat revenue of BRL5.636bn, while operating income fell 32.6% to BRL560.1m. "If you just compare our 22% with our 42% fuel RASK, that's about a 50% recapture already," president Abhi Shah said on 12 November. "Our intention is to recover 100%."

Half of a doubled dollar cost, earned in a currency that lost 15.6% after Lula's 25 October re-election, lands on BRL22.6bn of gross debt whose dollar obligations convert at R$5.18 at an average 9.9% rate. The $552m of listed equity is roughly BRL3.2bn behind that stack. The shares fell 32.6% in the 26 October session on 988,600 shares against roughly 100,000 on a typical day; American Airlines' $100m warrant exercise on 19 October moved them less than 1%. What Azul has is density — 82% of its routes carry no nonstop competitor, and loyalty, cargo and maintenance supply about 20% of unit revenue.

The spectrum holds at the top

The two larger carriers sit where the mechanism predicts. LATAM offset about 80% of a $594m quarterly fuel increase with $410m of incremental pricing and cargo revenue, guides 2026 EBITDA to $3.9-4.1bn, and trades at 10.3x forward earnings — Copa's multiple on roughly half Copa's margin. Grupo Aeroméxico recaptured 90-93% with premium cabins at 42% of revenue; its 81x forward multiple is an earnings trough, 2026 consensus of $0.22 a share rising to $1.55 in 2027.

So the durable work is the recapture, and the November prices are mostly the fuel curve. Volaris went from $5.68 on 23 October to $6.10 after its print, then to $8.68 by 11 November as crude collapsed: the quarter was worth about 7% and oil the rest. Copa's advance is a 35% multiple expansion over 2026 while its trailing earnings fell from $16.28 to $15.30 a share, with consensus assuming a 29% earnings rebound in 2027 that requires the margin squeeze to reverse. Azul's de-rating needs no explaining beyond its own balance sheet.

What none of it settles is whether the fares stay once the fuel does not. "I think it is very sticky, and we will not expect pricing to change that much if fuel comes down, especially if it comes down in a significant way," Copa chief executive Pedro Heilbron said on 19 November, noting that average fares are only about 5% above 2019 before inflation. Every dollar of that stickiness is margin for the carrier billing in dollars, and the test arrives with the next set of unit-revenue numbers.

Mexico Is Letting Its Airports Charge More Per Passenger as Fewer Passengers Arrive

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Three Mexican airport concessions stepped out of a five-month share decline in November on what looks like a traffic recovery. Their October passenger counts fell at all three, and the gain is coming from the regulated price instead.

Each group trades committed capital spending for a maximum tariff per passenger-equivalent, and each is billing a rising share of that ceiling: OMA ended the year near 93% compliance and targets 95%, GAP expects a compound increase near 7% in the first week of January. It shows in the margins, not the volumes — GAP's third-quarter EBITDA rose 19.2% while its traffic fell.

The split is in cash: OMA is cheapest at 9.63x trailing EV/EBITDA on a 6.18% free-cash-flow yield; GAP's development program leaves 2.03%; ASUR's Brazilian buying cut its EBITDA margin 7.4 points.

Mexico's aviation regulator has agreed that the country's three listed airport groups may charge more for every passenger they put through a terminal, and the next step up lands in the first days of January. In October each of the three handled fewer passengers than a year earlier.

Through November the group has been treated as a traffic story turning. The monthly counts say otherwise. At ASUR, which operates Cancún and eight other Mexican airports plus San Juan and six Colombian airports, October traffic fell 0.9% with its Mexican airports down 5.4%. GAP, twelve Mexican airports plus two in Jamaica, saw its Mexican traffic fall 7.6%, Puerto Vallarta down 19.8%. OMA, thirteen airports led by Monterrey, fell 4.8%, with Monterrey itself down 7.5%. The damage is imported: the Mexican Caribbean scheduled roughly 20% fewer US flights for summer 2026 after Spirit's May shutdown, with United cutting nearly 17% of capacity and American almost 15%.

The ceiling, and how much of it gets billed

Each group operates under a five-year Master Development Program: committed investment in exchange for a maximum tariff per workload unit, the regulated ceiling on landing, parking, jetway and security charges. The groups need not bill the whole ceiling, and how much they bill is the swing factor. "We anticipate to end this year somewhere around 93% compliance with maximum tariff. For next year, we are looking to be somewhere around 95%. We are planning to reach 100% by the third year of the five-year plan," chief executive Ricardo Dueñas said on OMA's 29 October call.

That ratchet did the work in OMA's quarter: aeronautical revenue rose 6.7% year on year and 5.3% per passenger on April's adjustment alone, with a further nominal rise "slightly above 4%" due 1 January. The uncapped book grew faster — commercial revenue up 11% to MXN 66 per passenger, VIP lounges up 28%, retail up 18.5% — and cargo, the industrial-corridor line, grew 42% on new Monterrey customers. It is the cheapest of the three at 9.63x trailing EV/EBITDA on a 6.18% trailing free-cash-flow yield, and Barclays initiated coverage at Overweight on 24 November with an MXN 300 target, citing nearshoring.

GAP needs the ratchet more, because its per-workload-unit caps edge down in December-2024 peso terms across the 2025-2029 program. "We are expecting this to increase the tariff on the first week of January in the coming year... we will have a compound increase around 7% is what we are expecting," chief executive Raul Revuelta Musalem said on 19 October, with 95% fulfilment targeted. Third-quarter EBITDA rose 19.2% to MXN 6.1bn and the margin widened 350 basis points to 67.8% on three things: the tariff, internalized technical-assistance fees, and consolidation of Cross Border Xpress, the pedestrian bridge carrying travelers from San Diego into Tijuana airport, which produced MXN 775m of revenue and MXN 558m of EBITDA in the quarter on about a million crossings. Passengers still fell 1.4%. And the cash is spoken for: MXN 8.6bn of committed capital spending in nine months leaves a 2.03% trailing free-cash-flow yield, against the dearest valuation of the three at 11.48x.

ASUR bought its way out of the regime, and paid in margin

"ASUR has evolved into the leading international airport operator across the Americas... our portfolio spans 36 airports across seven countries," chief executive Adolfo Castro said on 21 October. The CPC Aeroportos purchase closed on 1 September for BRL 5.5bn, about $992m, and took net debt to 2.6x trailing EBITDA from 0.2x. Group adjusted EBITDA margin fell 7.4 points to 60.3%, because the Brazilian assets contribute roughly 46% margins and the US commercial operations about 11%, while Mexican EBITDA fell 10%. Castro was blunt on demand: "We are not so happy of what we are seeing, and we do not know when all of this conflict in the Middle East will end and will bring a better outcome for the airlines." The shares sit at 11.37x trailing EV/EBITDA on consensus 2026 EBITDA that is 6.2% below 2025's.

The New York lines rose over the 30 days to 27 November — ASUR's ADR 9.9%, OMA 8.5%, GAP 4.0% — while the Mexico country fund EWW fell 1.7% in dollars, so this was specific to the sector. The Mexico City lines gained more, ASUR's 16.2% and GAP's 9.6%, largely on translation: the dollar-peso rate implied by the ADR-to-local ratio moved from 18.63 to 19.63 over the month, and the peso closed at 19.4242 on 24 November, its weakest since May 2025, after Banco de México cut its rate to 6.25%.

So the advance is explained by price and currency, with nothing from volume. Consensus 2026 EBITDA is flat to lower at all three — ASUR -6.2%, GAP +1.7%, OMA +0.3% — with the growth deferred to 2027 at 16.8%, 16.3% and 14.9%. November pulled that year forward. The order of who got paid follows the cash: OMA's capped tariff plus uncapped hotels, cargo and parking convert to free cash today; GAP's development program absorbs the tariff gain before it reaches shareholders; ASUR's acquisitions diluted the very margin its Mexican ceiling cannot repair, and management has disclaimed synergies.

Two decisions could settle it before February's results. Mexico's antitrust commission is expected to clear the Volaris-Viva Aerobus merger before year-end, which matters most at OMA, where the two carriers are 76% of passengers and management says it cannot yet size the effect. And the FAA's review of Mexico's civil-aviation authority identified 21 gaps to be closed by December, with a Category 2 downgrade possible as early as February 2027.

The tariff increases arrive on the calendar, in the first days of January, without reference to how many people are flying. If the US carriers that pulled out of Cancún this summer stay out, the three groups will spend next year charging more to fewer travelers, which flatters an income statement for a while and is not the same thing as growth.

Lula's Fourth Term Cost Brazil's Banks a Fifth or More in One Session; Vale Fell 3%

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

One session priced a Brazilian election, and it priced it almost entirely inside the lenders. The banks and the digital-banking platform in the iShares MSCI Brazil ETF lost between a sixth and nearly a third of their value the day after Lula's runoff win; the iron-ore exporter in the same fund lost 3.2%, and the Mexico, Peru, Chile and Colombia funds barely moved.

What was marked down was earnings in reais exposed to the federal budget and to a Selic held at 13.75%, not Latin America. Petrobras, 17.3% of the fund, reported third-quarter revenue up 40.4% and net income up 28.4% on record output, and its shares still fell 38.6% from 23 October to 25 November. Vale is the one large holding whose earnings actually deteriorated, and it fell the least of any of them.

Brazil's banks did the losing. In the first session after Lula won a fourth term with 51.8% of the runoff vote on 25 October, Itau Unibanco's American shares fell 20.4% and Bradesco's 28.0%, and the Sao Paulo exchange paused trading when the index breached a 10% circuit breaker on its worst day since 1998. Vale, the Rio de Janeiro miner that sells iron ore to Chinese steelmakers in dollars, fell 3.2%.

That asymmetry is the episode. The day was read as a country being de-rated, and the country funds obliged: the iShares MSCI Brazil ETF lost 19.8% close to close and its small-cap sibling 31.6%. But the selling landed on earnings denominated in reais and exposed to the federal budget, and it stopped at the border. That same session the Mexico fund closed up 0.05%, Peru up 0.2%, Chile down 1.2% and Colombia down 0.7%.

Two different businesses inside one wrapper

About 46% of the Brazil fund sits in five names as of 30 October: Petrobras at 17.3%, Vale 8.7%, Nu Holdings 8.5%, Itau 8.0% and the electric-motor maker WEG at 3.4%. Two of those earn dollars from commodities; two lend in local currency. Crash day sorted them accordingly. Banco do Brasil's local line fell 29.3%, Nu Holdings, the Sao Paulo digital bank with 86 million monthly active users in Brazil, fell 17.0%, and Petrobras fell 14.0%. The iShares Latin America 40 fund lost 11.2%, close to its Brazil weight multiplied by the Brazil fund's move; Petrobras, Nu, Vale and Itau are its four largest holdings as well.

Petrobras's quarter accelerated into its own selloff

The state-controlled oil group reported third-quarter revenue of $32.96bn, up 40.4% year on year, and net income of $7.74bn, up 28.4%, on production averaging 2.823m barrels of oil equivalent a day. It raised full-year operating cash flow guidance to $45-50bn from $35bn. "In the last two years alone, the production went up 3x as much as it did during 10 years," chief executive Magda Chambriard told the 11 November call.

The shares fell 38.6% between 23 October and 25 November, against 29.2% for the fund that holds them. What the market was pricing was not the quarter but the policy attached to it: fears of renewed state intervention, fuel-price controls, higher taxation and dividend risk, the levers Lula used in his third term. On the same call management confirmed fuel pricing is under internal review, with a "situational" adjustment possible by year-end and no structural change announced. Petrobras now trades at 3.5x forward earnings against 4.3x trailing, on consensus 2026 earnings of $4.80 a share, up 55% year on year, with a trailing free-cash-flow yield of 16.8%.

Vale is the counter-case, and it is the one that makes the point. Its third-quarter operating income fell 40.9% and net income 53.3% as gross margin compressed to 24.4% from 36.4%, and consensus 2026 earnings have been cut to $1.47 a share, down 27.1%. It is the only large holding whose earnings genuinely turned down, it trades at 7.0x forward earnings, and it is the holding that barely moved. A weaker real cuts its Brazilian mining costs; it does not cut the value of its iron ore.

The small-cap side travelled twice as far, both ways

The Brazil small-cap fund owns the domestic book directly: Totvs at 6.0%, the water utility Copasa at 4.9%, the grocer Sendas Distribuidora at 4.6% and Lojas Renner at 3.4%, with consumer cyclicals, real estate and utilities dominating 83 holdings. Those earnings move against the policy rate, and the central bank held the Selic at 13.75% on 4 November in a unanimous vote, ending five consecutive quarter-point cuts and citing unanchored inflation expectations.

Then the rate story reversed. Lula named Bradesco board chairman Luiz Carlos Trabuco finance minister on 26 November; the Ibovespa rose about 6.1% and long swap rates fell roughly 50 basis points. Because the US market was shut for Thanksgiving, two Brazilian sessions landed in one print: the small-cap fund gained 13.8%, the large-cap fund 10.5% and the 2x leveraged Brazil fund 20.6%. Itau rose 12.3% on 27 November; Vale rose 5.1%.

Moving-average trend measures were no help through any of it. They stayed bullish on the Brazil fund through 5 November, three weeks after the 19.8% day, and turned bearish on the Latin America 40 and leveraged funds on 18 November, two sessions before the Brazil fund's lowest close of the episode at 28.54.

Why the fund never looked cheap

The Brazil fund's trailing price-to-earnings ratio was 10.70 on 27 November, essentially where it stood in May. A drawdown of nearly a third produced no cheapening because the real went from 5.33 per dollar a year ago to 5.97 on 23 November, and a 12% devaluation cuts the dollar price and the dollar-translated local earnings together. The cheapening is specific to holdings paid in dollars.

Nor is the leverage telling anyone anything about Brazil. Over the 30 days to 25 November the 2x fund returned minus 20.4% against twice the Brazil fund's minus 20.6%, a fifth of a point of slippage. Compounding costs show up only over long windows: minus 28.0% over a year against twice minus 13.5%.

So the drawdown accounts for a policy path rather than an earnings path. The lenders and domestic small caps fell because a held Selic and a looser budget hit their revenue directly, and they recovered for the symmetrical reason when a private banker took the ministry. Petrobras fell hardest of the big names with its production and cash generation rising, which leaves its discount resting on the fuel-pricing and dividend decisions of a government that has made those decisions before.

Trabuco takes office on 5 January. Petrobras's year-end pricing review comes first.

Originating hypothesis

category band break index composition mechanics · subject: EWZS, COLO, EWW, EWZ, ILF

The LatAm regional-ETF shelf has been marked down at genuinely gradual intensity — the ten-fund group averages -10.3% over 30 days against only -5.5% over twelve months, with not one member in any 1-month, 3-month, 6-month or 12-month mover list, yet almost every fund has stepped strongly bullish to strongly bearish across the 180- and 365-day windows (ILF, EWZS, FLN, EPU, ECH, BRZU) while two have gone the other way or barely moved (COLO is the only member in a bull band, mildly bullish over both 90 and 180 days, and EWW has repaired strongly bearish to mildly bearish inside 30 days) — and rather than another "LatAm is selling off" regional read, the question is a what-do-you-actually-own-inside-each-wrapper question told end to end: establish, holding by holding, what each fund is paid by, since EWZ is a cap-weighted bet on a handful of dollar-priced commodity exporters and big Brazilian banks, EWZS owns the domestically-geared Brazilian small caps whose earnings move inversely to the Selic and whose index is the one that broke hardest, BRZU is a daily-reset leveraged wrapper whose band flip from strongly bullish to strongly bearish in 30 days is partly compounding mechanics rather than a view on Brazil, ARGT's weight sits heavily in a Nasdaq-listed dollar earner rather than in peso assets, ECH and EPU are effectively copper-and-precious-metals funds with a pension-and-currency overlay, and COLO is a two-name oil-and-bank fund — so the same regional discount is landing on a dollar-revenue export book, a local-rate domestic book, a leverage-decay product and a commodity proxy at once.

HDFC Bank told investors in October that its margin had reached bottom, and the thing it expects to lift that margin is the interest-rate increase the Reserve Bank of India had just delivered. The Monetary Policy Committee voted unanimously on 7 October to raise the policy repo rate 25 basis points to 5.50%, its first increase since February 2023, moving to a stance it called calibrated tightening and saying cuts were off the table in the near term. The September quarter that both of India's largest private lenders reported ten days later closed before any of that reached a loan book.

So the margins in hand went down and the margins in prospect went up, and November's rally in Indian bank shares was read as earnings confirmation. It was not that: HDFC Bank's net interest margin of 3.32% was the lowest point management identified, its reported net income grew 3.7% year on year — the weakest of the eight quarters on file — and its American depositary receipts gained a third in the month to 27 November.

A floating loan book against deposits that reprice at maturity

HDFC Bank, which gathers savings, current and term deposits across India and lends them as home, vehicle, personal and business credit, could not move its funding cost in the quarter. Cost of funds was flat at 4.4%, because time deposits supplied ₹1.3tr of the ₹1.57tr of deposit growth. Inherited borrowing maturities from HDFC Limited of roughly ₹2.6 lakh crore keep forcing that mix, holding the low-cost current and savings share down even as retail balances in those accounts grew 11.2% year on year.

The post-merger normalization of loans to deposits also stalled: deposit growth of ₹1.57tr was matched by loan growth of ₹1.58tr.

What changes is the asset side. "The 70% of floating rate book should be factored in by December [for the 25 bps rate hike], so directionally, yes, it should start improving from Q3 itself," chief financial officer Srinivasan Vaidyanathan told analysts on 17 October. Outgoing chief executive Sashidhar Jagdishan put it flatly on the same call: "The trajectory will be upward from the next quarter onwards." Asset quality gives them room — gross non-performing loans of 1.11%, down 13 basis points, with credit costs of 44 basis points and a 1.8% return on assets.

The shares now carry 16.5x trailing earnings and 2.17x book. Consensus fiscal-2027 earnings per share of ₹160.89 sits just 3.3% above the trailing four quarters' ₹155.73, so the forward multiple derived from it is about 15.9x — barely half a turn of relief for a year of waiting. The case rests on fiscal 2028, where consensus is 16.3% higher.

ICICI keeps 86 basis points more, and says where it leaked

ICICI Bank, a universal lender with 7,656 branches running deposits, mortgages, cards, insurance and wholesale banking, earned 4.18% on a comparable quarter against HDFC Bank's 3.32%. Its own compression had two named causes: overseas branches, which earn about 1% margin versus more than 4% domestically and grew as a share of the balance sheet after a foreign-currency deposit mobilization of $18bn, and retail term deposits, which pushed cost of deposits up to 4.50% from 4.41% in June, though still below 4.64% a year earlier.

"From Q4, I would expect margins to come back in a pretty healthy way as these factors start to reverse out, even assuming that there are no further rate hikes," chief financial officer Anindya Banerjee said on 17 October. At 19.3x trailing earnings, about 18.6x consensus for this fiscal year and 2.93x book, ICICI is the dearer of the two on every anchor, bought on consensus earnings growth of 20.3% this year against HDFC Bank's 3.3%.

What November actually bought

HDFC Bank's receipts rose 33.3% in the 30 days to 27 November and ICICI's 25.6%; across the year HDFC Bank is still down 21.6% while ICICI is up 16.3%. In Mumbai, HDFC Bank gained about 25% in November, its biggest monthly rise since May 2009. Foreign institutional investors bought for 16 straight sessions, a record roughly ₹1.29 lakh crore, with financial services the largest recipient and the rupee at an eight-month high. India was the biggest emerging-market recipient of those flows while South Korea and Taiwan saw heavy outflows — the same rotation that marked down Korean lenders whose own margins barely moved.

The disclosed results account for the move at one bank and not the other. ICICI's price is paying for earnings already reported and credit already improving; HDFC Bank's is paying for a spread its chief financial officer has dated to December and a year of earnings consensus still values at 3.3% growth. The December quarter is the first to contain the hike in full. If the loan book reprices on the two-quarter arithmetic management laid out, the margin turn arrives into a price that has already allowed for it; if the term-deposit book catches up first, HDFC Bank will have to find the earnings somewhere its own guidance has not promised them.

Argentine sovereign risk broke four digits on 20 November, for the first time since October last year, and the company the selling punished hardest was the one paid in dollars. YPF, Argentina's state-controlled integrated energy company, fell 33.7% in the month to 27 November. Grupo Financiero Galicia, the Buenos Aires conglomerate that owns Banco Galicia, the Naranja X digital lender and Galicia Seguros, fell 16.1%; Banco Macro, which lends to Argentine households and mid-sized companies, fell 13.2%.

Through the autumn the working assumption was that dollar revenue was the hedge against Argentine macro: the banks had been de-rating since September as peso rates came down, while the oil producers held. November reversed the ranking for two reasons unrelated to pesos. The dollar price of a barrel collapsed, and a sovereign spread reprices equity whatever currency the invoice is written in. Over twelve months the old order still reads clearly: Galicia down 41.9%, Macro down 31.7%, YPF down 7.4%.

Eight sessions did most of it

Country risk went from 586 basis points on 7 October to 1,025 points on 20 November, a 10.1% jump in a session that took New York-law global bonds down more than 3%, and on to 1,096 by 24 November, with the peso 3.5% weaker across the month. The Buenos Aires Herald attributed the bond selling to a complicated international backdrop, growing investor doubt over the financial programme presented by Economy Minister Luis Caputo and Finance Secretary Federico Furiase, and an early election climate. Galicia's shares fell 15.0% in the eight sessions from 13 to 20 November, and the Argentina country exchange-traded fund fell 12.6% over the same stretch. Morgan Stanley assigns roughly 50% odds to a bearish outcome after the presidential vote of 24 October 2027, and the economy entered technical recession as third-quarter activity fell 1.8%.

The barrels improved while the barrel got cheaper

YPF's quarter was its second best on record: adjusted EBITDA of $2,503m, up 84% year on year on revenue of $6.6bn, with free cash flow of $901m. Shale oil averaged 224,000 barrels a day, up 32% and now 86% of its oil; lifting cost fell to $7.8 a barrel of oil equivalent and net leverage to 0.81x, the lowest in over ten years. It placed a $1.2bn nine-year bond at 300 basis points over Treasuries in the quarter, its tightest spread ever. It is the largest producer in Vaca Muerta, the shale play that passed half of Argentina's oil and gas output in 2026, and holds about 58% of the domestic fuel market.

What moved was the price. Brent fell from $113.35 at the end of October to below $87, more than 23%, as global supply rose 2.3m barrels a day in October to 105.4m on recovering Persian Gulf exports, record US output and the restart of Libya's Sharara pipeline. Goldman Sachs cut its YPF target 14% to $48.70 on 11 November, preferring Vista Energy for its lower exposure to local macroeconomic volatility. YPF closed 27 November a third below even that reduced figure.

Where the earnings really did shrink

Macro is the quarter that confirms the carry unwind. Net interest income fell 15% sequentially, net interest margin dropped 360 basis points to 20.5%, and the margin excluding currency and securities fell to 15.6% from 17.6%, so the squeeze has reached core deposit spreads rather than only the inflation-linked and Treasury-bill book. Total financing shrank 3%, commercial overdrafts by 19%, and net income of ARS 78.4bn was down 64% sequentially as reported, including ARS 39.6bn of early-retirement and severance charges. The arithmetic sits in the policy rate: the benchmark TAMAR lending rate fell from roughly 36-37% in 2025 to 24-25% by the third quarter, while real private-sector peso credit grew only about 2.6% in a year.

Galicia's financial margin fell to 14.4%, return on equity to 9.3% from 11.3%, with peso loans up 3%, government securities down 6% and earnings below consensus. Its credit book moved the other way: non-performing loans fell to 7.8% from 8.3%, cost of risk to 8% from 9.3%, provisions down 12%. "For next year, we are seeing also our cost of risk reducing by like 200 basis points from this year because we are confident that the portfolio will continue improving regardless what happened with the GDP," chief financial officer Gonzalo Fernández Covaro told investors on 25 November. Operating expenses are down 36% year on year, the efficiency ratio is 39.4% and total capital 25.7%.

What these prices now assume

None of YPF's fall is traceable to its own operations; it is an oil price and a sovereign spread, and the analyst cut that followed the first of those accounts for only 14 percentage points of it. The shares sit at 4.2x trailing enterprise value to EBITDA and 1.08x book, on a 22.1% trailing free-cash-flow yield.

The bank declines do have an earnings reason, but the two are priced strangely against one another. Macro, with the shrinking loan book and the deeper margin compression, is marked at 0.94x book; Galicia, where delinquency and provisions are falling, at 0.68x, against roughly 1.1x in early October. Consensus has followed neither: analysts still carry 2027 earnings per share growth of 62% for Galicia and 49% for Macro, while modelling YPF's down 10.5%. One of those three numbers is going to move a long way.

The pipes meant to make Vaca Muerta's barrels worth more were about 85% built when the shares gave up their year. "And on January, we will start to fill all pipes, and we are expecting that in March, everybody will start exporting," chief executive Horacio Marín told investors on 29 October. What the barrels fetch will be decided at an OPEC+ table in Vienna and in the price of Argentine sovereign risk, and YPF drills neither.

One re-rating is paid for, the other is borrowed

Earnings explain most of Grid Dynamics' advance: the beat was wide, the margin trend is three quarters old, and brokers raised numbers on the print rather than after the move. They explain much less of EPAM's, where volume slowed, output per engineer did not change, and the gain arrived alongside a guide that matched what analysts already carried.

Why the two diverge is contractual. Kotak Institutional Equities reported in November that more than half of Cognizant's fixed-price portfolio had already passed through an AI-led deflation cycle: a vendor keeps a productivity gain only until the contract is re-signed. Grid Dynamics bills engineering pods into a consolidating supplier list at rising rates, so the gain shows up in its own revenue line.
