Argentine sovereign risk broke four digits on 20 November, for the first time since October last year, and the company the selling punished hardest was the one paid in dollars. YPF, Argentina's state-controlled integrated energy company, fell 33.7% in the month to 27 November. Grupo Financiero Galicia, the Buenos Aires conglomerate that owns Banco Galicia, the Naranja X digital lender and Galicia Seguros, fell 16.1%; Banco Macro, which lends to Argentine households and mid-sized companies, fell 13.2%.

Through the autumn the working assumption was that dollar revenue was the hedge against Argentine macro: the banks had been de-rating since September as peso rates came down, while the oil producers held. November reversed the ranking for two reasons unrelated to pesos. The dollar price of a barrel collapsed, and a sovereign spread reprices equity whatever currency the invoice is written in. Over twelve months the old order still reads clearly: Galicia down 41.9%, Macro down 31.7%, YPF down 7.4%.

Eight sessions did most of it

Country risk went from 586 basis points on 7 October to 1,025 points on 20 November, a 10.1% jump in a session that took New York-law global bonds down more than 3%, and on to 1,096 by 24 November, with the peso 3.5% weaker across the month. The Buenos Aires Herald attributed the bond selling to a complicated international backdrop, growing investor doubt over the financial programme presented by Economy Minister Luis Caputo and Finance Secretary Federico Furiase, and an early election climate. Galicia's shares fell 15.0% in the eight sessions from 13 to 20 November, and the Argentina country exchange-traded fund fell 12.6% over the same stretch. Morgan Stanley assigns roughly 50% odds to a bearish outcome after the presidential vote of 24 October 2027, and the economy entered technical recession as third-quarter activity fell 1.8%.

The barrels improved while the barrel got cheaper

YPF's quarter was its second best on record: adjusted EBITDA of $2,503m, up 84% year on year on revenue of $6.6bn, with free cash flow of $901m. Shale oil averaged 224,000 barrels a day, up 32% and now 86% of its oil; lifting cost fell to $7.8 a barrel of oil equivalent and net leverage to 0.81x, the lowest in over ten years. It placed a $1.2bn nine-year bond at 300 basis points over Treasuries in the quarter, its tightest spread ever. It is the largest producer in Vaca Muerta, the shale play that passed half of Argentina's oil and gas output in 2026, and holds about 58% of the domestic fuel market.

What moved was the price. Brent fell from $113.35 at the end of October to below $87, more than 23%, as global supply rose 2.3m barrels a day in October to 105.4m on recovering Persian Gulf exports, record US output and the restart of Libya's Sharara pipeline. Goldman Sachs cut its YPF target 14% to $48.70 on 11 November, preferring Vista Energy for its lower exposure to local macroeconomic volatility. YPF closed 27 November a third below even that reduced figure.

Where the earnings really did shrink

Macro is the quarter that confirms the carry unwind. Net interest income fell 15% sequentially, net interest margin dropped 360 basis points to 20.5%, and the margin excluding currency and securities fell to 15.6% from 17.6%, so the squeeze has reached core deposit spreads rather than only the inflation-linked and Treasury-bill book. Total financing shrank 3%, commercial overdrafts by 19%, and net income of ARS 78.4bn was down 64% sequentially as reported, including ARS 39.6bn of early-retirement and severance charges. The arithmetic sits in the policy rate: the benchmark TAMAR lending rate fell from roughly 36-37% in 2025 to 24-25% by the third quarter, while real private-sector peso credit grew only about 2.6% in a year.

Galicia's financial margin fell to 14.4%, return on equity to 9.3% from 11.3%, with peso loans up 3%, government securities down 6% and earnings below consensus. Its credit book moved the other way: non-performing loans fell to 7.8% from 8.3%, cost of risk to 8% from 9.3%, provisions down 12%. "For next year, we are seeing also our cost of risk reducing by like 200 basis points from this year because we are confident that the portfolio will continue improving regardless what happened with the GDP," chief financial officer Gonzalo Fernández Covaro told investors on 25 November. Operating expenses are down 36% year on year, the efficiency ratio is 39.4% and total capital 25.7%.

What these prices now assume

None of YPF's fall is traceable to its own operations; it is an oil price and a sovereign spread, and the analyst cut that followed the first of those accounts for only 14 percentage points of it. The shares sit at 4.2x trailing enterprise value to EBITDA and 1.08x book, on a 22.1% trailing free-cash-flow yield.

The bank declines do have an earnings reason, but the two are priced strangely against one another. Macro, with the shrinking loan book and the deeper margin compression, is marked at 0.94x book; Galicia, where delinquency and provisions are falling, at 0.68x, against roughly 1.1x in early October. Consensus has followed neither: analysts still carry 2027 earnings per share growth of 62% for Galicia and 49% for Macro, while modelling YPF's down 10.5%. One of those three numbers is going to move a long way.

The pipes meant to make Vaca Muerta's barrels worth more were about 85% built when the shares gave up their year. "And on January, we will start to fill all pipes, and we are expecting that in March, everybody will start exporting," chief executive Horacio Marín told investors on 29 October. What the barrels fetch will be decided at an OPEC+ table in Vienna and in the price of Argentine sovereign risk, and YPF drills neither.

The satellite-connectivity shelf looks like a market marking down direct-to-device service. It is one company. AST SpaceMobile fell about a third in the month to 25 November; Globalstar rose, and Iridium's small slip tracked Rocket Lab's share price, since both are now priced as takeover consideration.

AST's business improved into the autumn. September-quarter revenue of $42.3m was up 187% year on year, gross margin recovered to 40.2%, and full-year guidance of $150-200m was reiterated on 9 November. What did not improve is how the constellation gets paid for: roughly $310m of capital spending in the quarter, $400-500m guided for the fourth, and 10 million shares handed to Firefly Aerospace for an engine licence with no cash coming in.

Price/sales has compressed to 74.6x from 387x in May. Dilution, not demand, is what moved.

AST SpaceMobile bought the right to build its own rocket engines this month and paid for it without spending a dollar. A prospectus filed on 18 November disclosed 10,000,000 Class A shares going to Firefly Aerospace in exchange for a perpetual licence to build the Miranda engine and its vacuum variant, the Vira, plus roughly 780,052 shares to Ligado and One Dot Six. The shares fell about 8% on the second filing in two days.

That reaction has been read across the whole satellite group as the market deciding direct-to-device connectivity has peaked. The quarter says otherwise. What repriced in November was the cost of funding a constellation, and the repricing sits almost entirely on the one member of the group that still has to fund one.

Revenue nearly tripled and the guide held

AST SpaceMobile, which builds satellites that connect directly to ordinary unmodified phones and sells the coverage to mobile operators and governments, reported September-quarter revenue of $42.3m, up 187% from $14.7m a year earlier. Gross margin recovered to 40.2% from 25.2% in the June quarter as hardware and government work shifted the mix. Full-year revenue guidance of $150-200m was reiterated on 9 November, and consensus of $166.5m sits inside it. Added to that: a roughly $100m U.S. Space Force contract for airborne moving-target indication sensing, the sixteenth government award to date, and a $1bn Japanese government subsidy for a mid-band constellation in a joint venture where AST holds 49% and exclusive revenue rights in three Asian markets.

"We are on track for nationwide intermittent service activation in United States and other key markets before year end 2026," chairman and chief executive Abel Avellan told investors on 9 November, describing "launches planned in 2027 and 2028 with SpaceX, Blue Origin and others. Supporting a plan of 100 satellites in orbit."

$310m of spending against $42m of sales

Satellites cost $21-23m each at present, the company said, and the plan calls for roughly 45 in orbit by mid-2027. Capital expenditure was about $310m in the September quarter against that $42.3m of revenue, better than seven to one, with $400-500m guided for the fourth. Cash stood at $3.2bn. The operating loss was $168.7m.

The debt is not the pressure point. Of $2.225bn of convertible principal outstanding, the $1.075bn of 2.25% notes due 2036 convert near $116.30 a share, and the 1.625% notes due 2034 carry a cap call at $149.20, both far above the $26.02 close. They behave as debt now. The equity is where the cost lands: diluted shares reached 300.1m in the quarter, against 272.8m a year earlier and 199.2m two years ago, and the Firefly shares bought an engine licence while bringing in no cash.

Globalstar trades on an Amazon contract, Iridium on Rocket Lab's price

The group average that implied an industry-wide markdown does not survive inspection. Globalstar rose 3.6% over the same 30 days and closed at $85.87, a 4.6% discount to the $90.00 cash Amazon agreed to pay in April; its outlook is suspended, its September-quarter revenue of $70.9m fell 4% with Apple at 64% of nine-month revenue, and its price/sales of 40.1x is where it was in May. Iridium slipped 3.5%. Its holders approved a Rocket Lab acquisition on 24 September, with 99.6% of votes cast in favour, for $27.00 cash plus a collared stock stub; with Rocket Lab at $47.02, below the $67.50 collar floor, the ratio is pinned at 0.4000, so the package is worth $45.81, down 5.6% over the window. Iridium's own operational EBITDA of $135.6m was flat year on year, its buyback has been paused since October 2025, and net leverage of 4.2x sits above its 3.0x target. Its price/gross-profit of 6.6x has barely budged from 6.3x in May.

Neither price is a judgment on satellite demand. One is a contract, the other an exchange ratio.

The de-rating is doing rational work

Nothing was cut into AST's 34% monthly fall, and the month was not orderly: a 12.9% drop on 5 November, a 10.2% gain on the 9th, a 9.1% drop on the 24th. Consensus does not show positive net income until 2028, and then only $9.3m, with $472m in 2029 — the longest-duration asset on the shelf in a month when the Russell 1000 Growth index fell 11.4% against Value's 6.1% as rising rates discounted distant profits. Price/sales has gone from 387x in May to roughly 206x in early October to 74.6x trailing and 64.0x forward, a five-fold compression while revenue grew.

The arithmetic that justifies it is the share count. With the price down about 80% from its high and the funding requirement unchanged, each dollar raised now costs roughly four times the shares it did in May. B. Riley's Mike Crawford upgraded the stock to Buy on 19 November with a $48 target on the view that rocket and deployment risk is already in the price; director Adriana Cisneros bought $500,000 of stock near $24. The competitive overhang is harder to price: Elon Musk said on 18 November that SpaceX's next direct-to-cell satellites, launching in 2027, will carry 40 times the bandwidth of the current generation, and SpaceX's spectrum purchase points it at the same carriers AST sells through.

Avellan's plan rests on more than 60 operator agreements covering three billion subscribers and on engines that have so far run 175 hot fires on a test stand. Every month the plan grows, so does the number of shares carrying it.

Flex gets paid to put the artificial-intelligence rack together, and in the September quarter that fee got better rather than thinner. The company's Cloud & Power Infrastructure segment — power shelves, switchgear, busways, enclosures and, since the JetCool purchase, liquid-cooled racks — booked $2.5bn of revenue, up 54% year on year, at a 10.7% operating margin against 8.3% a year earlier. The power sub-segment alone reached roughly 16%.

That matters because the premise behind this autumn's markdown of everything that supplies the data-center build was that integrators like Flex carry more customer-funded material each year and keep the same sliver of it. Group gross margin is thin, at 9.6%. But it was 9.0% a year earlier, and group gross profit grew 32.2% on revenue growth of 24.8%. The content is rising and so is the take.

The fee is not for a standard rack

Flex's cloud unit is deliberately turning away the commodity end of the work. Management said it is concentrating on custom configuration, vertical integration and aftermarket service while avoiding low-margin standard racks, with cooling products still early and launches planned over the following six months. The hardware backs the claim: Flex's liquid-cooled racks integrate JetCool direct-to-chip cold plates and cool up to 120 kW per rack and beyond, and JetCool has launched a 6U in-rack coolant distribution unit rated at 300 kW and scalable to 2.1 MW at row level. Flex builds the box, not only the frame around it.

It comes with capital attached. Fiscal 2027 capital spending is guided at $1.5-1.6bn, about two-thirds of it for the cloud and power segment, and component shortages are already inside guidance as a multi-year headwind.

"You cannot walk away from the fact that hyperscalers are spending the most amount of money," chief executive Revathi Advaithi told investors on 28 October. "If you want to be able to grow your market share, you have to grow with these hyperscalers."

Three ways to get paid for one coolant loop

The same water that leaves a cold plate passes two other listed cash registers, each on different terms. Dover sells the per-part hardware — quick disconnects through Colder Products, dozens per liquid-cooled rack, and brazed-plate heat exchangers through SWEP — and is paid on shipment: its Climate & Sustainability Technologies segment turned $481.5m in the September quarter, up 18% organically, on bookings of $594.1m, up 43.1%, a book-to-bill of 1.23. Dover trades at 19.2x forward earnings, against 20.2x in late May on the identical estimate; almost nothing moved.

SPX Technologies is paid at the far end, per megawatt, at the price struck when the job is quoted — roughly $100,000 of revenue per megawatt for evaporative cooling and about $200,000 via its dry-cooling system, according to disclosed unit economics — against a heating-and-cooling backlog that closed the quarter at $1,102.5m, up 69% organically. Its forward earnings multiple is 17.1x, down from 26.1x in May on an unchanged estimate.

On price to forward gross profit, the only comparison that survives a 9.6%-margin integrator sitting beside 39-40%-margin equipment makers, Flex is at 7.3x, Dover 7.9x and SPX 6.5x. Parker Hannifin, which competes with Dover for the coupling socket but told investors on 5 November that data centers are about 1.5% of sales, is at 12.1x.

What the discount is actually attached to

Flex has fallen 53% from its 26 May close, including a 13.6% session on 5 November when the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.4% on hyperscaler funding and post-midterm permitting fears. Robert W. Baird cut its target to $84 from $135 on 23 November. Earnings estimates did not follow: consensus of $5.01 for the March 2027 year sits inside the raised $4.84-$5.16 guide, and that guide was lifted by roughly $950m of revenue in October. Hold the estimate still and the multiple went from 28.6x in late May to 13.4x.

So the markdown is price, not profit — but one piece of it has a name. On 2 October Flex, Axiom and General Catalyst signed a $2.0bn Series A preferred agreement, 200,000 shares at $10,000 each, struck at an initial enterprise value for Axiom of $37.5bn and paying a 10% annual cash dividend. Flex guarantees redemption at 115% of purchase price in cash, or 125% in Flex shares, if the separation is not completed by 31 December 2027; the shares fell 6.9% in an afternoon as investors worked through those terms. At its 10 November Innovation Day in Austin, Flex put Axiom's calendar-2026 revenue near $10bn and the separation on track for the first quarter of calendar 2027.

That is the honest split in the de-rating. The operating business is converting the build at widening margins, and Dover's shipment book and SPX's quoted backlog say the loop they all feed is still filling. What repriced in November was the financing and permitting of the halls themselves, and in Flex's case a dated obligation of its own making.

Until the spin clears, the preferred keeps paying 10% in cash, and the calendar keeps running toward a deadline Flex wrote down in a contract.

Grid Dynamics turned $114.3m of September-quarter revenue out of 4,559 people, down from 4,971 a year earlier, and reported gross margin widened from 34.8% to 40.3% across the year. Grid Dynamics finished September with fewer engineers than it had a year earlier and more revenue to show for them. Management told investors on 29 October that revenue per employee had cleared $100,000 for the first time in the company's history.

That single measure is the test the whole digital-engineering shelf has been marked down against. Since mid-2026 these firms have been priced as though generative software tools would quietly eat the billable hours inside a fixed-fee statement of work; Morningstar cut EPAM's economic-moat rating to none and Accenture's to narrow on exactly that reasoning. Both of the names examined here have been re-rated upward since the October and November prints as if the thesis had been settled. One of them has the per-head numbers to settle it.

Fewer engineers, more revenue at San Ramon

Grid Dynamics, which builds search, data-analytics and platform-engineering software for Fortune 1000 retailers, telecom carriers and consumer-goods makers, booked $114.3m in the September quarter, up 9.7% year on year. Growth accelerated through 2026, from 3.7% to 7.0% to 9.7%, and reported gross margin widened from 34.8% to 40.3% over those same three quarters, with operating margin moving from minus 3.5% to 8.0%. Headcount fell to 4,559 from 4,971. Annualise the quarter over those staff counts and revenue per head works out near $100,300 against roughly $83,800, a gain of about a fifth.

"In a sector where headcount and revenue have historically moved in lockstep, Grid Dynamics is demonstrating that the two can and should be decoupled," chief executive Leonard Livschitz said on the 29 October call. Revenue from AI work grew 45% year on year for a third consecutive quarter and now approaches 32% of sales. Adjusted earnings of $0.18 a share beat the $0.13 consensus, and EBITDA of $22.9m cleared a $20m-topped guide of $16.5m-$17.5m. TD Cowen lifted its target from $9 to $13 and Needham from $10 to $15 the same day.

The efficiency came with a shorter client list. The top five customers supplied 45.4% of revenue against 40.1% a year earlier, and the top ten 63.3% against 58.3%, which management attributes to clients cutting their roster of suppliers in its favor. At $14.12 the shares trade on 26.1x forward earnings, the dearest of the four large names on this shelf, where Accenture sits on 16.4x, and against roughly 16.7x at the 2025 year-end.

Cash of $304.3m against a $1.13bn market value softens that: on enterprise value the shares are near 9.8x 2027 consensus EBITDA.

Where EPAM's extra margin actually came from

EPAM, the Newtown, Pennsylvania platform-engineering firm serving banks, life-sciences, media and software clients, reported $1.426bn, up 2.3%, with more than 57,350 delivery professionals, up 2.2%, and utilisation of 76.5% — unchanged from a year earlier and down from 78.3% in June. Per delivery head that is roughly $99,500 annualised against roughly $99,400: flat. The 1.8 points of non-GAAP gross-margin expansion, to 32.8%, came from pricing, offshore mix and a Poland research tax benefit that halves from about $40m this year to about $20m in 2027, and not from busier engineers.

Reported revenue growth decelerated across the year, 7.6% to 4.5% to 2.3%, and the December quarter is guided to organic constant-currency growth of 0.5% to 1.6%. The raised full-year non-GAAP earnings guide of $13.23-$13.31 brackets the $13.28 consensus; one outlet headlined the 22.2% session of 5 November as a jump on in-line results. A $500m accelerated repurchase and a fresh $1.8bn authorisation landed with the print. Asked whether double-digit growth could return, chief executive Balazs Fejes said: "Jamie, at a very high level, definitely not in 2027. I do foresee that we can actually get to double-digit growth. But over time, right?"

The shares at $164.63 are on 12.4x forward earnings and 11.4x the 2027 consensus of $14.43, below their own 15.4x at the 2025 year-end, and still 11.4% under where they traded twelve months ago. AI-native revenue reached $185m in the quarter, about 13% of sales, with the 2026 target raised to at least $650m from $600m.

One re-rating is paid for, the other is borrowed

Earnings explain most of Grid Dynamics' advance: the beat was wide, the margin trend is three quarters old, and brokers raised numbers on the print rather than after the move. They explain much less of EPAM's, where volume slowed, output per engineer did not change, and the gain arrived alongside a guide that matched what analysts already carried.

Why the two diverge is contractual. Kotak Institutional Equities reported in November that more than half of Cognizant's fixed-price portfolio had already passed through an AI-led deflation cycle: a vendor keeps a productivity gain only until the contract is re-signed. Grid Dynamics bills engineering pods into a consolidating supplier list at rising rates, so the gain shows up in its own revenue line. EPAM's larger book is repriced at renewal, which is why its improvement surfaced in gross margin and in the tax line instead.

The open question is which one survives contact with 2027. Grid Dynamics' finance chief Anil Doradla told the October call the mandate now is to scale, with "the appetite right now for larger acquisitions, which we hope to do in 2027." A per-head record built on 45% of revenue from five customers is a figure that can move in either direction with one of them.
