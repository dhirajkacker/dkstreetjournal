IonQ, D-Wave and Rigetti Hold $4.7bn With No Proof Quantum Beats Classical Computing

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The quantum shelf was marked down this month on what looked like a financing problem. The filings say the opposite: the three largest listed pure-plays hold about $4.7bn between them, enough for roughly four to seven years at current burn rates, and Rigetti's own earnings-call cash figure understates its liquidity about tenfold.

What the quarter actually split was results. IonQ grew revenue 336% to $173.8m and raised full-year guidance to $505–515m, while D-Wave's third-quarter bookings fell 19% and Rigetti's revenue fell 27.5%. All three fell together anyway, and so did Quantinuum, which was funded by a $1.68bn institutional listing in June and holds $1.9bn.

The likelier reading is a flood of new quantum paper meeting an admission from the companies themselves that none can yet show a return against a classical computer.

Rigetti Computing told investors it ended September with roughly $49.7m of cash. Its quarterly filing shows $451.3m more in short- and long-term US Treasury securities and no debt, a total of $497.1m; the company, which builds superconducting quantum processors in Berkeley and rents access to them, burned $49.1m in operations over nine months plus $42.6m of capital spending on dilution refrigerators and fab equipment. That is about $30.6m a quarter, or four years of money, and it is the smallest treasury of the three largest listed quantum names.

That matters because the month's markdown has been read as a financing event: companies that pay for their research by selling stock, cut off when the stock stops cooperating. On the evidence the financing is already done. IonQ, D-Wave and Rigetti hold about $4.7bn between them, summing the three balance sheets, and none of it is borrowed.

The treasuries, and how long they last

IonQ priced a $2.0bn equity offering on 14 October — shares and pre-funded warrants at $40.00, a 15% premium to the prior close, bought by an entity managed by Heights Capital Management, with 100m seven-year warrants struck at $69.46. By the 16 October closing, the shares themselves were at $31.28. The deal leaves $3.7bn of pro forma cash against a third-quarter adjusted EBITDA loss of $133.6m, which includes about $40m of one-off integration cost: on the order of seven years.

D-Wave, which sells quantum annealing systems and the Leap cloud service, holds $503.4m plus a definitive agreement with the Commerce Department for up to $100m of funding under the CHIPS and Science Act, none of it drawn at 30 September. Its adjusted EBITDA loss widened to $37.7m from $20.7m a year earlier. Thirteen quarters, roughly.

The quarter split; the shares did not

IonQ's revenue reached $173.8m, up 336%, and management raised full-year guidance to $505–515m from $450–460m, with remaining performance obligations of $1.2bn. The quality is thinner than the growth: gross margin fell to 21.9% from 48.0% a year earlier, because SkyWater Technology, the foundry it bought on 31 July for about $1.94bn, supplied $83.5m of the total.

The other two went backwards. D-Wave booked $1.9m in the quarter, down 19% year on year, with nine-month bookings of $37.4m flattered by a single $20m Florida Atlantic University system due in the fourth quarter. Rigetti's revenue fell 27.5% to $1.4m on acceptance timing, though nine-month revenue of $10.9m against $5.2m keeps the year pointing up toward roughly $20m of guidance.

Dilution tracks the reverse of the usual complaint. IonQ added 106m diluted shares over the year and bought $134m of incremental quarterly revenue with them. D-Wave added 32m shares for $0.8m. Rigetti added 14m, including 7.7m issued to the Commerce Department, while revenue fell.

The one nobody financed with retail stock

Quantinuum, the Honeywell-founded trapped-ion company, listed on Nasdaq in June at $60.00, raising $1.68bn from an upsized, 20-times-oversubscribed book. It holds $1.9bn, booked $98m in the third quarter and grew revenue 233% to $9.05m at a 74.6% non-GAAP gross margin. It closed at $30.33 on 24 November, down 49% from the offer price and 19% over the trailing 30 days — the same direction as the companies whose cash came from at-the-market sales.

The decline came in dated legs, and the openers were about supply and usefulness. On 3 November Xanadu disclosed plans for a US listing and the group fell within hours, D-Wave by 15.2%. On 10 November D-Wave's chief executive Alan Baratz told investors: "As of today, we are not aware of any quantum computing company that has produced even 1 logical qubit that meets that fault tolerance standard." Rigetti's Subodh Kulkarni said the same day that "None of us are at a point where we can demonstrate ROI with quantum computing over classical computing". Then on 20 November the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.28% and Nvidia 14% as the artificial-intelligence trade unwound.

What is left to explain

Runway explains none of it. Results explain part: D-Wave's falling bookings and Rigetti's shrinking quarter are real deterioration, and D-Wave at 69.8x forward sales and Rigetti at 129x were priced for neither. IonQ's 18.9x forward sales looks like the cheap one until the margin mix is applied, at which point it is 66.6x forward gross profit, and its headline 31.5x trailing earnings is an artifact of warrant fair-value gains rather than operations. Quantinuum, at 250x forward sales, is dearest of the four and has the best bookings.

What the four have in common is that a great deal of new quantum equity reached the market in 2026 — the June listing, IQM's July Nasdaq debut, Infleqtion's, Xanadu's plan, IonQ's $2bn — while the chief executives conceded there is no demonstrated advantage yet. Without flow data the likelier reading is that more paper met a shrinking appetite for unproven duration, inside a broad de-risking of anything sold on 2030 cash flows.

IonQ's Niccolo de Masi told investors on 5 November: "I do not see headwinds, and I have not seen headwinds the last 2 years." Kulkarni, five days later, put his own company about three years from quantum advantage. Both can be right, and the companies have bought themselves enough money to find out which.
