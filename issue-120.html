Synopsys Books Less of Its Revenue on Contract Than Cadence Does Since Buying Ansys

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The two companies that split the market for chip-design software were marked down together this month on one question — whether the artificial-intelligence buildout keeps funding design budgets. Their own filings say the contracted dollar sits at the smaller of the two.

Recurring revenue was 79% of Cadence's September quarter, its backlog is a record $8.2bn, and 58% of that converts to revenue within twelve months. At Synopsys, time-based products fell to 40% of July-quarter revenue from 53% a year earlier, because the Ansys simulation book it bought recognizes multi-year lease licenses at lease commencement; 49% of its backlog converts inside a year, and it carries $10.0bn of senior notes.

Business momentum and the cheaper earnings multiple sit at opposite names: Cadence raised guidance and accelerated, while Synopsys's organic tool growth slowed to 8.5%.

The software duopoly that designs the world's semiconductors was marked down this month on a single question: whether hyperscale computing budgets keep paying for chip design. What the two companies' filings show is that the more contracted of the pair is the smaller one. Cadence Design Systems, which sells simulation, synthesis and sign-off tools plus Palladium emulation hardware and design IP, booked 79% of its September-quarter revenue as recurring and 21% upfront, against 82% and 18% a year earlier.

That matters because the ranking is usually read the other way. Synopsys, the larger business, has long been described as the ratable one: multi-year time-based licenses for its electronic-design-automation tools, with hardware and silicon IP as the lumpy edge. Its third-quarter filing says time-based products were 40% of the July quarter's $2.477bn, down from 53% of revenue a year earlier, with upfront products at 27%. The de-rating is landing on already-signed backlog at one name and on delivery-timed revenue at the other.

Ansys moved the Synopsys dollar off the ratable line

The mechanism is in the acquired company's own accounting. Ansys recognizes its multi-year subscription lease licenses up front at lease commencement, a practice Ansys said makes revenue "more volatile" and the reason it reported annual contract value separately. Synopsys closed the deal in 2025; Ansys contributed $622.2m of the third quarter's revenue increase, and roughly $2.98bn of guided fiscal-2026 revenue.

The rest of the dollar moved with it. Of Synopsys's $10.9bn backlog, excluding $1.9bn of non-cancellable commitments, about 49% is expected in revenue over the next twelve months — nine percentage points less forward conversion than Cadence. Gross margin was 72.6% in the quarter against 78.1% a year earlier. Organic growth in the design-software business slowed to 8.5%, a deceleration Morgan Stanley had flagged in a downgrade, and the $10.0bn of senior notes that financed the purchase cost $429.3m of interest over nine months. Design IP did return to growth, up about 11%.

None of this is order weakness. The sequential dip in backlog was attributed to the sale of the processor IP business, which GlobalFoundries completed on 2 June 2026.

Cadence's upfront line is building supply into orders

Cadence's lumpy segment is doing the opposite of clearing. Its chief financial officer commentary says inventories were raised specifically to work through a significant emulation order backlog, with no current capacity or supply constraints. "Q3 revenue grew 22% year-over-year, with strong double-digit growth across all product categories, driven by growing momentum from AI-driven designs," John Wall, chief financial officer, said on 26 October. Recurring revenue grew 18%, double the prior year's 11%, and full-year guidance went to 21% revenue growth with the earnings target up $0.30 to $8.40.

China, the exposure usually invoked to explain either discount, is the larger line at Cadence — 16% of the quarter, down from 18% — against 10% at Synopsys.

Both are also digesting a simulation deal. Cadence completed its purchase of Hexagon's Design & Engineering business on 23 February 2026 for about €2.7bn, adding roughly $160m of 2026 revenue at minimal operating profit and about 28 cents of dilution before turning accretive in 2027.

The cheaper earnings sit with the lumpier revenue

Cadence trades at 31.2x consensus fiscal-2027 earnings of $9.85, against roughly 50x forward in late July. Synopsys is at 22.7x the 2027 consensus of $18.84.

Shares in both are well below their highs of the past year — Synopsys by 20%, Cadence by 26% — so the markdown predates November. The month's move is concentrated in two sessions: both fell about 8% on 20 November, when the Nasdaq Composite wiped out its 2026 gain and Nvidia dropped 14% despite record revenue, then rebounded 8% to 9% on 23 November. No disclosure specific to either company sits on those dates.

The quantified bear case is a customer-budget case rather than a backlog case: Bank of America estimates that holding cloud spending near current levels implies roughly a 20% cut to projected capital expenditure and 35% to 40% downside to artificial-intelligence semiconductor earnings. That would reach tool renewals eventually. It has not reached them yet: Cadence's $4.2bn of revenue expected from contracted obligations over the next twelve months is already signed, core tools grew 20%, IP 24% and system design 30%, and the board authorized a further $2.5bn of repurchases after buying $400m of stock in the quarter.

So the results account for Synopsys's discount and not for Cadence's. Synopsys pays for its lower multiple with mix, margin and leverage; its headline 42% revenue growth is acquisition arithmetic sitting on 8.5% organic expansion. Cadence pays a premium for a dollar that arrives on contract, and that dollar grew faster this quarter than last. The demand both managements point to is physical. "TSMC showed their process road map and said in the next 5 years, the chip size will go up by 48x. 48x," chief executive Anirudh Devgan told investors on 26 October, explaining why verification hardware keeps selling.

Synopsys has not spoken to the market since 26 August, when chief executive Sassine Ghazi said "AI is driving unprecedented complexity and increasing demand for the silicon IP and engineering solutions necessary to deliver next-generation AI compute." Its fourth-quarter and fiscal-year results land after the close on 8 December — the first test of whether the organic tool growth management promised would reach double digits in the quarter actually did.

Gentex Keeps Selling More Electronics Per Car While the Market Prices It With Robotaxis

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A mirror maker shipped far fewer mirrors last quarter and barely lost any car revenue, because the content it fits on each vehicle it still supplies went up. That is the whole defense, and it is now colliding with a component bill repricing faster than Gentex can pass it through.

Gentex shipped 9.61m auto-dimming mirrors in the September quarter, down 14.5% from a year earlier, while automotive revenue fell only 3.7% to $552.5m. Gross margin still slipped 100 basis points to 33.4%, and management flagged $10-15m of additional fourth-quarter costs it does not expect to recover until the first half of 2027.

The stock is priced on the same shelf as Mobileye and Hesai, where the autonomy story has been marked down hard. Gentex is not in that trade: its revenue follows build rates and content wins, and it trades at 9.8x forward earnings against roughly 16.6x two years ago while consensus earnings rose.

Gentex, which builds the electrochromic auto-dimming rearview mirrors, driver-monitoring cameras and full display mirrors that carmakers fit from Zeeland, Michigan, shipped 9.612m mirror units in the September quarter. That is 14.5% fewer than a year earlier, and its automotive revenue fell 3.7%, to $552.5m. Dividing one into the other gives roughly $57.5 of automotive revenue per mirror shipped, against about $51.0 a year ago — a derived figure, and the entire explanation for why a seventh of the unit base went missing without the revenue following.

That gap is the business. Gentex is classified among autonomous-driving and vehicle-electronics suppliers, and over the twelve months to 23 November its shares fell 12.8% while mainstream parts makers in the same table rose — Magna 27.3%, BorgWarner 16.3%. The markdown has been read as autonomy being de-rated. Gentex's revenue is not set by autonomy at all: it is set by how many cars get built and how much equipment the company fits inside each one.

The content wins are real; the component bill is repricing faster

North American automotive revenue rose about 3% against a 5% decline in regional light-vehicle production, and European revenue rose 13% excluding a lost Volkswagen program. Driver and in-cabin monitoring and full display mirror take rates did that work. "This acceleration in growth is being driven by new technology, higher content per vehicle, and a more diversified customer base," chief executive Steven Downing told investors on 23 October, explaining a reaffirmed 2027 revenue range of $2.8-2.9bn — 6-9% growth against primary markets the company expects to shrink about 1%.

The cost side moved the other way. Gross margin fell 100 basis points to 33.4% on precious-metal inflation and weaker operating leverage, and 2026 revenue guidance came down to about $2.65bn after a roughly $35m hit from a Hyundai/Kia union strike that landed mid-launch. Then there is memory, already the story of this quarter across electronics hardware.

"Anywhere from 8x to 10x price increases on a per unit basis. So I mean, literally, a component that cost $4 or $5 this earlier this year is now $40," Downing said on the same call, describing DDR memory and raw printed-circuit-board supply. Guidance carries $10-15m of incremental fourth-quarter cost with recovery not expected until the first half of 2027.

The two names beside it are not having the same quarter

Mobileye, which sells EyeQ vision chips and higher-content assisted-driving stacks, shipped 9.5m EyeQs, up 3.3%, at a $49.10 average system price against $51.70 a year earlier; gross profit still rose 7.4% on revenue down 2.4%, and full-year guidance went up. The shares fell 57% over twelve months, to 0.48x book. Hesai, the Shanghai lidar maker, shipped 729,673 units, up 65.3%, for 34.3% more revenue — about 19% less revenue per unit — with gross margin at 41.0% against 42.1% and fourth-quarter guidance implying growth of roughly 10-15%.

So one shelf holds three different problems. Mobileye's economics improved while its price halved. Hesai is buying platform share with price and watching operating income fall as units compound. Gentex is the only one whose units and margin both genuinely deteriorated, and the only one whose fix is already contracted: content per car, won years ago, landing now.

Its de-rating is a multiple. Forward earnings are capitalized at 9.8x against roughly 16.6x two years ago, while consensus earnings over that span rose; the stock carries a 7.0x enterprise value to EBITDA and an 11.2% trailing free-cash-flow yield. Gentex repurchased 5.7m shares in the quarter at an average $23.44, about a fifth above the 23 November price of $19.54.

The mirror count will likely keep falling as build rates drift lower, which means content per car has to keep outrunning it. The awkward part is that the cheapest items on the bill of materials are the ones repricing — and Downing said the fourth-generation full display mirror is being designed to need no DDR memory at all.

Siliconware Precision Industries, the packaging subsidiary of ASE Technology Holding, signed a multibillion-dollar, multi-year agreement with Nvidia on 17-18 November covering turnkey advanced packaging lines in Taiwan — bumping, interposer wafer fabrication, assembly and test — intended to triple Nvidia-dedicated capacity by mid-2029. Amkor Technology, the Tempe assembler that counted Apple as 29.8% of 2025 revenue and Qualcomm as 11.1%, has been brought in as a third supplier of 2.5-dimensional packaging and remains in early-stage collaboration, with engineering runs and pilot production but no meaningful volume expected in 2027, Digitimes reported on 23 November.

That reorders a hierarchy the market had taken for granted. Earlier this year the assumption was that TSMC's chip-on-wafer-on-substrate capacity was the binding constraint on accelerator output and that the independent assemblers were its overflow valve, with Amkor first in line as the non-Taiwanese second source. Both names have since been sold as one trade. The disclosures separate them on the only measure that matters: what a finished package pays.

One leading-edge package pays ASE three times what the electronics business does

ASE's assembly, test and materials segment turned over TWD 151.5bn in the September quarter, up 51% year over year, on a 30.6% gross margin and a 20% operating margin. That segment is 62% of consolidated revenue and 91% of group operating profit. The electronics manufacturing arm, whose volumes still grew 35%, ran an 8.3% gross margin after component pass-through costs, down from 8.9% in the June quarter. Consolidated revenue overstates the franchise; the segment table corrects it completely.

"The so-called structural margin ceiling has now become the base for us," chief financial officer Joseph Tung told investors on 29 October. Leading-edge advanced packaging revenue was guided above $4bn for 2026, up from $3.7bn mid-call, and Tung said ASE expects "to double our LEAP revenue" in 2027. Full-process CoWoS goes from $300m this year to above $1bn next; co-packaged optics is guided above $0.5bn in 2027. Utilization is at or near 100%, with physical space rather than demand named as the constraint, and capital spending was raised to roughly $11.5bn against net debt-to-equity of 66%.

Amkor's mix turned over while its revenue stood still

Amkor's September quarter was a profit record on a flat top line: revenue up 0.18% year over year after 25.6% growth in the June quarter, with gross profit up 27.8% and operating income up 41.4% as gross margin reached 18.26% from 14.32%. Communications fell to 37% of revenue from 51%, Computing rose 25% sequentially to a record on a high-density fan-out processor program, and automotive and industrial grew 37%. Advanced products were $1.632bn of $1.991bn in net sales, about 82%. The handset weakness is an artifact of memory: DRAM wafers diverted to high-bandwidth stacks starved the system-in-package line of components.

Amkor is spending into the gap. It committed $1.3bn to Korea on 21 October, taking multi-year commitments with Nvidia to roughly $3bn, and has taken about $3bn of advance payments from a dozen customers against future capacity, enough to fund capex without new debt through 2027. Arizona Phase 1 completes late 2027. "Customers are not only planning further ahead, but are also making long-term commitments," chief executive Kevin Engel said on 26 October.

What reaches either company is the cheaper end. TrendForce reported in August that CoWoS-R and CoWoS-S are the processes being released first, and HBM stacking stays inside the memory makers, from through-silicon-via fill to final test.

Two sessions, both market-wide, did the damage

Over the month to 23 November Amkor fell 11.4% and ASE 17.6%. Compound 5 November and 20 November alone and the declines are 24.3% and 31.1% — more than the full-month moves. Those were the two worst sessions in the market this month, the first on the post-midterm turn against artificial-intelligence capital spending plus Qualcomm's 26% drop in handset chip revenue, the second on a reported SEC probe of Nvidia. Neither day carried a disclosure from either assembler. On 23 November, with Jensen Huang reinstated, Amkor rose 15.1% and ASE 21.4% against a market mean of 3.3%.

The rule governing Pentagon money until 11 December pays for last year's programs and forbids starting new ones, and that clause of appropriations law separates three listed drone makers more cleanly than their business descriptions do. AeroVironment, which builds Switchblade loitering munitions and, since its BlueHalo purchase, space and directed-energy systems, is paid largely on reorders of things the Army already buys. So is Kratos, the target-drone, hypersonics and satellite-software contractor. Red Cat, which supplies reconnaissance and first-person-view drones to government buyers, needs orders that have not been funded yet.

All three were marked down as one trade. Over the thirty days to 23 November AeroVironment fell 26.4%, Kratos 26.3% and Red Cat 57.3%, while the primes sat still: General Dynamics was off 0.4%, L3Harris up 0.3%, Lockheed Martin down 5.4%. The reading embedded in that gap is that unmanned systems are a budget bet and the budget is stuck. Two of the three order books say the opposite.

The stopgap bars starts, not deliveries

The continuing resolution holds defense at the extended fiscal 2026 baseline of $838.5bn against a $1.5 trillion request, and bars any program not already funded and active in the prior year. Breaking Defense counted more than 150 fiscal 2027 initiatives blocked from starting, including $17.5bn of Golden Dome missile-defense work. The Senate Appropriations Committee finished releasing draft text for all twelve fiscal 2027 bills on 16 November, but floor action on the defense authorization is unlikely before December and the odds of a year-long Pentagon stopgap have risen. A rule like that is close to neutral for a contractor shipping against funded orders and punitive for one waiting on an award.

AeroVironment's consumables keep being reordered

AeroVironment's first fiscal 2027 quarter brought record funded backlog of $1.5bn, up 37% year on year, with $1.4bn unfunded and a 1.4 book-to-bill; Precision Strike revenue, the loitering-munition and counter-drone line, was $197m, up 8%. The Army placed a $51m delivery order for additional Switchblade 600 Block 2 systems on 26 August, and in November the company took a $405m international order for LOCUST X3 high-energy laser counter-drone systems, a BlueHalo line whose economics rest on power and thermal limits rather than magazine depth. Guidance was reaffirmed at $2.125–2.225bn of revenue and $305–325m of adjusted EBITDA on 9 September. "AV's fiscal year 2027 is off to a strong start, with record first-quarter revenue and funded backlog and landmark strategic wins," chairman and chief executive Wahid Nawabi said that day.

Fair Isaac, which licenses the FICO credit score into lenders' transaction flows and sells decision-management software to banks, grew its Scores business by more than two-fifths in the fiscal year ended 30 September while being paid for fewer mortgage score pulls than a year earlier. Scores revenue reached $1.652bn, up 41%, and in the September quarter alone mortgage-origination score revenue rose 72% even as unit volumes fell high single digits. The growth was price.

That is a defensible way to run a business when the buyer has no alternative, and until this autumn the buyer did not. FICO's wholesale royalty on a mortgage-origination score went from about 60 cents to $10 over roughly five years, including a doubling for 2026 from $4.95, while Experian and TransUnion offer a VantageScore 4.0 mortgage score at $0.99. The gap did not matter while government-sponsored enterprise loan pricing treated the two scores unequally.

A ten-to-one price gap stopped being academic

On 9 September the Federal Housing Finance Agency directed Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac to let all approved lenders use VantageScore 4.0, removing the prior-written-approval requirement after a four-month pilot with 50 lenders. VantageScore 4.0 was already used to securitise about 9% of Fannie and Freddie mortgages between 1 May and 31 August. Then, on 28 September, FHFA director Bill Pulte said the enterprises would merge their two loan-level pricing grids into one, with VantageScore 4.0 joining the Classic FICO grid from 1 October. The prior arrangement effectively required a VantageScore roughly 20 points higher to earn the same loan price; the single grid ends that.

FICO's shares fell 29.2% close-to-close the next session, from $846.22 to $599.51, its worst day since 1989. In October the company launched the FICO Preferred Program, bundling Classic FICO with FICO Score 10T and holding the $10 wholesale price flat for 2027 for lenders agreeing to pull FICO on all applications. It then declined to issue fiscal-2027 guidance, citing unresolved mortgage pricing negotiations and weak loan volumes.

Chief executive William Lansing was blunt about what the company has learned. "I think that transparency and detail on our programs and pricing have not been particularly helpful for FICO," he told investors on 4 November. "On a go-forward basis, our intention is to not share every detail. We're not going to share every pricing program. We're not going to share what the prices are."

The trailing numbers argue the other way

Nothing in the reported results looks like a company in trouble. Revenue grew 13.5%, then 15.9%, then 27.3% across the last three fiscal years to $2.535bn; gross margin widened from 79.7% to 86.1%; free cash flow rose 40% to $1.034bn. The software side is compounding independently of mortgages, with Platform annual recurring revenue of $453m, up 72%, and net revenue retention on that platform of 156%. FICO repurchased $3.028bn of stock, about a tenth of the shares, which left shareholders' equity negative and leverage at 3.29x before buybacks paused in the fourth quarter to repay term debt.

Against that record the shares are 58.6% lower than a year ago and 60.8% below their December peak. Twelve months ago the stock carried roughly 66x trailing earnings; it now carries 19.8x trailing and 16.3x forward.

The forward estimates are where the two pictures reconcile. Consensus models fiscal 2027 revenue of $2.592bn, growth of 2.5% after 27.3%, with net income down 4.3% and earnings per share up 3.8% to $44.27 across a range of $40.41 to $49.04. On those numbers the step from trailing to forward multiple is share count, not growth. A 16x multiple on a business compounding at low single digits with negative book equity is not obviously cheap; it is cheap only against FICO's own history.

Volumes make the point harder. The Mortgage Bankers Association forecast total single-family originations of $2.2tn in 2026, up about 8% from roughly $2.0tn, so a high-single-digit decline in score pulls is worse than the market behind it. Management attributed the decline to elevated rates and said there had been no meaningful loss to competition; the mix also shifted from first-lien mortgages toward lower-priced home equity lines.

What was priced, and what the shelf did not price

The 20.7% recovery from the September low is the market handicapping the defence rather than dismissing it. FICO has signed direct licences with resellers representing roughly 73% of mortgage reseller volume, and its 10T Adopter Program covers 84 lenders with $684bn of eligible annual originations, with the Federal Housing Administration making 10T available from 1 January 2027. Conforming-market use still awaits enterprise sign-off. "10T is the best score available," Lansing said on 4 November. "Everyone is waiting for GSEs to sign off finally so that the conforming market can use it."

This is not a sector being marked down for software disruption. Over the same thirty days to 23 November, Vertex, which sells corporate indirect-tax software and raised full-year guidance on e-invoicing growth, rose 16.4%; Tyler Technologies, the public-sector software vendor, rose 4.2%; Q2 Holdings, which sells digital banking to community institutions and lifted guidance on 12% revenue growth, fell 4.1%. Over twelve months those three are down between 6% and 10.2%. BlackLine, which automates the accounting close, is the other broken name at -54%, and its cause is its own: a 25.1% one-session fall on 6 November after guiding fourth-quarter revenue to $195m to $197m against consensus near $207.8m, with dollar-based net revenue retention of 103.5%.

FICO's de-rating, then, is not a multiple catching down to a weak business. It is the market repricing one line of a strong one: a royalty that behaved like a regulated toll and now faces a competing bid at a tenth of the price on the same pull. How much of Scores' 41% growth repeats depends on how many lenders enrol in a program whose terms the company has just said it will stop disclosing.

The next twelve months of that negotiation will happen in private, by design. Investors will learn the outcome the way they learned this one: from a revenue line, after the fact.

Innodata's volume held and its per-task price did not

Innodata's growth barely decelerated — 54.4%, 57.8% and 52.9% across the first three quarters of 2026 — while gross profit growth fell from 82.7% in Q2 to 37.3% in Q3. Operating income declined 19.6% year on year as revenue grew.

What each decline is actually pricing

Gartner's shares fetch 2.48x gross profit on both trailing and forward numbers, against roughly 4.0x a year ago and about 10x at the 2025 high; the de-rating exceeds the 24.9% fall in the price because $1.7bn of buybacks cut the diluted share count 14.7%. Innodata's problem is narrower and sharper — the volume of AI data work is still compounding, but the price per task fell nine points of margin in a single quarter and two customers decide the revenue line.

Hall's clients are now paying Gartner to tell them why their AI spending has not paid off. Innodata is paid to build the thing they bought. One invoice renews every twelve months at 3% more; the other ends when a program does, by email, mid-shift.

That print has been read as the first crack in the thermal-demand story that carried the shares through 2025. The disclosure does not support it. The hiring bill, priced at five cents

Chief financial officer Mark Carano put about $15m of the quarter's shortfall on growth-related labor and outsourcing inefficiency and about $10m on input-cost inflation, and Truist cut its price target to $233 from $266 on 2 November on that margin pressure while keeping a Buy rating; JPMorgan went to $190 from $225 on 11 November. The spending was deliberate. "We made a decision, it was an intentional decision to kind of accelerate our hiring and secure additional suppliers to ensure that we can meet the demand that's out there really for the balance of this year and really set us up for next year," Carano said on the 29 October call. SPX also has price increases embedded in the backlog and frame agreements with hyperscalers that adjust for material input-cost moves, which is why management does not frame this as a pricing failure.

The arithmetic of the blemish: the top of the full-year adjusted earnings range came down to $8.50, taking the midpoint to $8.35, a five-cent reduction. Full-year revenue guidance of $2.745–2.805bn still implies about 23% growth at the midpoint.

The selling came a week later, and not from SPX

The shares closed at $163.45 on 3 November and $142.71 on 6 November, a 12.7% fall across three sessions with no company disclosure in between. On 5 November the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.4% as investors dumped chip and data-center names, on fears of construction restrictions after the midterms, hyperscaler capex of $720–740bn against negative free cash flow, and CoreWeave credit-default spreads at record wides. SPX closed at a 52-week low of $136.29 on 20 November and $145.37 on 23 November, 41% below its 52-week high close and down 28.9% over twelve months. The likelier reading is that the market is discounting the conversion of a construction backlog whose customers' financing just repriced, rather than the backlog itself.

The move was not SPX's alone: Vertiv fell 26% and Modine 27% over three months against SPX's 27.9%. Within heat rejection for data centers, SPX's rivals are private: it, Baltimore Aircoil and EVAPCO together hold about half the 2025 data-center cooling-tower market by value.

The component suppliers kept their ratings

Dover, the diversified industrial whose Climate & Sustainability Technologies segment sells SWEP brazed-plate heat exchangers and quick-disconnect couplings, reported segment revenue of $481.5m, up 18% organically, with bookings up 43.1% to $594.1m — a book-to-bill of 1.23 — and segment margin down to 16.7% from 18.6% on footprint consolidation. Dover put data-center growth at 30% for 2026 and SWEP lead times at about 25 weeks, with capacity in the main data-center line doubling by mid-2027. Its shares are unchanged over three months, at 19.0 times forward earnings against 23.5 times trailing.

Parker-Hannifin, the motion-and-control group whose Quick Coupling Division competes with Dover's couplings, reported record quarterly sales of $5.992bn and raised full-year guidance. Management put data centers at about 1.5% of sales and "not yet large enough to be its own vertical" — roughly $380m on the coming year's consensus base. Parker is the dearest of the three at 24.8 times forward earnings, and 13.7 times trailing gross profit against 15.6 times in August.

What five cents explains

The guidance cut accounts for a fraction of a percent of SPX's earnings power and none of a 28% twelve-month decline. On the business, the direction is the other way: orders at a record, organic cooling growth guided near 30% for the second half, and $1.2bn spent on four HVAC acquisitions plus FIS Water this year for roughly $310m of combined revenue run-rate at a blended 11.7 times, leaving leverage near 1.8x. The shares now carry 17.3 times forward earnings against 24.5 times in August.

Two things keep the doubt open. Consensus revenue of $2.779bn for 2026 sits near the top of management's own range, so the forward figure rests on execution of the ramp that just cost $15m. And a backlog of cooling towers is a claim on halls that are not yet energized — the one thing the 5 November repricing questioned.

SPX's non-data-center markets are mixed: power, industrial, semiconductor and pharma strong, education, government and new commercial soft. The company is being valued as though the 90% of its data-center book that belongs to hyperscalers is the fragile part.

In October SPX fell to a 52-week low and the explanation offered was Nvidia's claim that its next rack generation needs no chillers. The low is 13% lower now, and the disclosure since then has been a record order book and a hiring bill. Whatever is being priced, it is not the quarter SPX reported.

Hewlett Packard Enterprise told investors on 23 November that it expects to keep handing component price increases to its customers, and the quarter it reported the same day shows it already has. The company that sells ProLiant and Cray servers, storage and Juniper networking gear through resellers posted fourth-quarter revenue of $14.5bn, up 50% year on year, non-GAAP earnings of $1.43 a share against a $1.27 consensus, and free cash flow of $1.9bn. Gross margin of 38.0% was 450 basis points better than a year earlier, and HPE attributed the improvement to favorable pricing in cloud and AI.

That matters because the autumn markdown in server stocks rested on a specific belief: that an AI rack is mostly bought-in accelerators and memory, resold at thin prices agreed long before the parts are purchased, so a memory shock lands on the builder. Morgan Stanley's November 2025 downgrade of Dell and HPE put memory at about 40% of the materials cost of a high-end server and cut its Dell fiscal 2027 gross-margin forecast to 18.2%. Dell's July quarter printed 20.93%.

The box out-earned the business HPE bought

HPE's Cloud & AI segment, which ships the servers, turned $11.257bn of quarterly revenue, up 68.6%, into an 18.4% operating margin against 9.3% a year earlier. Applied to that revenue, the margin is roughly $2.1bn of segment operating profit. Networking, the franchise acquired with Juniper, produced $675m at a 22.1% margin, up 6% year on year — about a third as much. Management told investors on the call that going forward "more than 40% of profit is going to come from networking," which is a forecast about a pool servers presently dominate.

HPE also said it expects memory, NAND and other critical components to stay constrained to at least the second half of calendar 2028, and that it will continue passing price increases through. It guided gross margin rate to moderate later in fiscal 2027 as AI volumes build, with gross profit dollars still growing. The order book supports the volume: an AI backlog of $10.5bn, up more than 80%, on full-year orders of $57.2bn. Fiscal 2027 non-GAAP operating profit is guided up 23% to 30%. The shares sit at 10.2x forward earnings, the bottom of the 10-13x range they held in May and down from 16.2x in September.

Dell's own figure for what a rack earns

Dell, the largest AI server original equipment manufacturer by volume, reported record Infrastructure Solutions Group revenue of $31.8bn in its July quarter, with AI-optimized servers at $16.4bn. Segment operating income of $4.781bn is a 15.0% margin, against 8.8% a year earlier, on a record $95bn AI backlog.

The dilution is real but smaller than assumed. Chief financial officer David Kennedy has said the AI server business runs in line with its target of a mid-single-digit operating margin, below the group rate, so each incremental AI dollar lowers the blend while raising the absolute profit. Dell reports its October quarter after the close today, guided to about $49bn of revenue and roughly $19bn of AI servers, with segment margin expected up slightly more than a point year on year and memory a headwind all year. At 12.9x forward earnings, the stock is well under the roughly 18x it carried in May.

Super Micro's strain is in the working capital

Super Micro, the San Jose builder of liquid-cooled rack-scale systems, tripled September-quarter revenue to $19.9bn and lifted gross margin to 12.74% from 9.31%, though sequentially it fell from 17.5%. The pressure point is elsewhere. One data-center customer was 44% of revenue, and operations consumed $434m of cash.

"The largest single driver was customer prepayment," chief financial officer Mark Long told investors on 3 November. "Deferred revenue increased approximately $3.1 billion in the quarter. Accounts receivable grew $3.6 billion, and inventory grew $2.5 billion to support the revenue ahead of us, and prepayments funded most of that build." A trailing free-cash-flow yield of minus 40% and a 3.8x forward multiple are describing the same disclosed facts.

Who actually pays for the memory

On 23 November the Financial Times reported that Dell, HPE, Lenovo and Supermicro are demanding price increases of 25% to 45% on AI equipment months after contracts were agreed, and none would confirm whether their contracts contained clauses against price volatility. Buyers are paying: memory-heavy processor nodes have doubled or tripled while TrendForce has server DRAM staying undersupplied with contract prices up 10-15% in the fourth quarter. HPE last year amended its quoting terms so a booked order can be repriced for component cost between quote and shipment.

The shares did not wait for the disclosures. Dell is down 23.0% over three months and HPE 10.1%, with Super Micro off 33.4% and down 22.9% over twelve months, having never participated in the gain its two peers made. The worst of it was one market-wide session on 20 November, when US equities lost roughly $3.2tn and Nvidia stood at about half its 6 October value; three sessions later Nvidia rose about 31% and carried Dell up 22.5%, HPE 17.5% and Super Micro 14.6% in a day.

So the derating and the disclosures point opposite ways for two of the three, and the reason is who holds the scarce good. Dell and HPE allocate constrained accelerators and memory into order books they can reprice, and both expanded segment margin while AI revenue grew fastest. Super Micro sells speed of deployment rather than contract leverage, and its 44% customer funds the inventory through prepayments, which is why the same selling reads as a priced risk there instead of a mistake.

HPE says the parts stay scarce into the second half of 2028. That is a long run of quarters in which these companies quote a price before they know what the memory will cost, and each one re-asks the question this week's reporting answered for only one of them.
