SPX Technologies Filled a Record Cooling Backlog With Overtime, and Cut Guidance by Five Cents

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A cooling-tower maker whose hyperscaler order book has never been bigger is trading where it was two years ago. SPX Technologies told investors on 29 October that its heating, ventilation and air-conditioning backlog hit a record $1.1bn, up 69% organically, and that hiring and outsourcing to ship it cost roughly $15m in the quarter.

The damage to earnings was five cents off the full-year midpoint, now $8.35. The shares have since fallen to a 52-week low, 41% below their high, most of it in the three sessions to 6 November when nothing about SPX was disclosed and the Nasdaq dropped 5.4% on data-center financing fears.

Dover, flat over three months, and Parker-Hannifin, which puts data centers at about 1.5% of sales, were not marked down the same way. The deterioration at SPX is real and sized; it is far smaller than the repricing.

SPX Technologies, which builds Marley and Recold evaporative cooling towers and fluid coolers for industrial and data-center heat rejection, told investors on 29 October that the order book in its cooling business had reached a record and that filling it cost more than planned. Both halves were true at once. The heating, ventilation and air-conditioning backlog stood at $1,102.5m at the end of the September quarter, up 69% year on year organically and 15% sequentially, with more than 90% of the data-center work tied to hyperscale customers. Segment margin fell about 140 basis points.

That print has been read as the first crack in the thermal-demand story that carried the shares through 2025. The disclosure does not support it. Group revenue rose 20.5% to $714.5m, HVAC revenue rose 35.5%, and management reaffirmed that capacity projects remain on track to support roughly $1.1bn of annual data-center revenue at full production. What deteriorated was the cost of shipping, not the demand behind it.

The hiring bill, priced at five cents

Chief financial officer Mark Carano put about $15m of the quarter's shortfall on growth-related labor and outsourcing inefficiency and about $10m on input-cost inflation, and Truist cut its price target to $233 from $266 on 2 November on that margin pressure while keeping a Buy rating; JPMorgan went to $190 from $225 on 11 November. The spending was deliberate. "We made a decision, it was an intentional decision to kind of accelerate our hiring and secure additional suppliers to ensure that we can meet the demand that's out there really for the balance of this year and really set us up for next year," Carano said on the 29 October call. SPX also has price increases embedded in the backlog and frame agreements with hyperscalers that adjust for material input-cost moves, which is why management does not frame this as a pricing failure.

The arithmetic of the blemish: the top of the full-year adjusted earnings range came down to $8.50, taking the midpoint to $8.35, a five-cent reduction. Full-year revenue guidance of $2.745–2.805bn still implies about 23% growth at the midpoint.

The selling came a week later, and not from SPX

The shares closed at $163.45 on 3 November and $142.71 on 6 November, a 12.7% fall across three sessions with no company disclosure in between. On 5 November the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.4% as investors dumped chip and data-center names, on fears of construction restrictions after the midterms, hyperscaler capex of $720–740bn against negative free cash flow, and CoreWeave credit-default spreads at record wides. SPX closed at a 52-week low of $136.29 on 20 November and $145.37 on 23 November, 41% below its 52-week high close and down 28.9% over twelve months. The likelier reading is that the market is discounting the conversion of a construction backlog whose customers' financing just repriced, rather than the backlog itself.

The move was not SPX's alone: Vertiv fell 26% and Modine 27% over three months against SPX's 27.9%. Within heat rejection for data centers, SPX's rivals are private: it, Baltimore Aircoil and EVAPCO together hold about half the 2025 data-center cooling-tower market by value.

The component suppliers kept their ratings

Dover, the diversified industrial whose Climate & Sustainability Technologies segment sells SWEP brazed-plate heat exchangers and quick-disconnect couplings, reported segment revenue of $481.5m, up 18% organically, with bookings up 43.1% to $594.1m — a book-to-bill of 1.23 — and segment margin down to 16.7% from 18.6% on footprint consolidation. Dover put data-center growth at 30% for 2026 and SWEP lead times at about 25 weeks, with capacity in the main data-center line doubling by mid-2027. Its shares are unchanged over three months, at 19.0 times forward earnings against 23.5 times trailing.

Parker-Hannifin, the motion-and-control group whose Quick Coupling Division competes with Dover's couplings, reported record quarterly sales of $5.992bn and raised full-year guidance. Management put data centers at about 1.5% of sales and "not yet large enough to be its own vertical" — roughly $380m on the coming year's consensus base. Parker is the dearest of the three at 24.8 times forward earnings, and 13.7 times trailing gross profit against 15.6 times in August.

What five cents explains

The guidance cut accounts for a fraction of a percent of SPX's earnings power and none of a 28% twelve-month decline. On the business, the direction is the other way: orders at a record, organic cooling growth guided near 30% for the second half, and $1.2bn spent on four HVAC acquisitions plus FIS Water this year for roughly $310m of combined revenue run-rate at a blended 11.7 times, leaving leverage near 1.8x. The shares now carry 17.3 times forward earnings against 24.5 times in August.

Two things keep the doubt open. Consensus revenue of $2.779bn for 2026 sits near the top of management's own range, so the forward figure rests on execution of the ramp that just cost $15m. And a backlog of cooling towers is a claim on halls that are not yet energized — the one thing the 5 November repricing questioned.

SPX's non-data-center markets are mixed: power, industrial, semiconductor and pharma strong, education, government and new commercial soft. The company is being valued as though the 90% of its data-center book that belongs to hyperscalers is the fragile part.

In October SPX fell to a 52-week low and the explanation offered was Nvidia's claim that its next rack generation needs no chillers. The low is 13% lower now, and the disclosure since then has been a record order book and a hiring bill. Whatever is being priced, it is not the quarter SPX reported.

HPE Doubled Its Server Margin by Charging AI Buyers for the Memory Shortage

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The November selling in enterprise servers priced these builders as assemblers of other people's chips, with no way to defend a margin when memory costs double. HPE's fourth quarter, reported on 23 November, says otherwise: the segment that ships its servers lifted operating margin to 18.4% from 9.3% a year earlier, and the company credits pricing in cloud and AI for the gain.

On the same day, the Financial Times reported that Dell, HPE, Lenovo and Supermicro are all demanding increases of 25% to 45% on AI-server orders agreed months earlier. Dell's infrastructure segment ran a 15.0% operating margin in its July quarter and reports again today.

Super Micro is the exception in cash rather than margin: one customer was 44% of September-quarter revenue, and the shares trade at 3.8x forward earnings, the cheapest and most concentrated of the three.

Hewlett Packard Enterprise told investors on 23 November that it expects to keep handing component price increases to its customers, and the quarter it reported the same day shows it already has. The company that sells ProLiant and Cray servers, storage and Juniper networking gear through resellers posted fourth-quarter revenue of $14.5bn, up 50% year on year, non-GAAP earnings of $1.43 a share against a $1.27 consensus, and free cash flow of $1.9bn. Gross margin of 38.0% was 450 basis points better than a year earlier, and HPE attributed the improvement to favorable pricing in cloud and AI.

That matters because the autumn markdown in server stocks rested on a specific belief: that an AI rack is mostly bought-in accelerators and memory, resold at thin prices agreed long before the parts are purchased, so a memory shock lands on the builder. Morgan Stanley's November 2025 downgrade of Dell and HPE put memory at about 40% of the materials cost of a high-end server and cut its Dell fiscal 2027 gross-margin forecast to 18.2%. Dell's July quarter printed 20.93%.

The box out-earned the business HPE bought

HPE's Cloud & AI segment, which ships the servers, turned $11.257bn of quarterly revenue, up 68.6%, into an 18.4% operating margin against 9.3% a year earlier. Applied to that revenue, the margin is roughly $2.1bn of segment operating profit. Networking, the franchise acquired with Juniper, produced $675m at a 22.1% margin, up 6% year on year — about a third as much. Management told investors on the call that going forward "more than 40% of profit is going to come from networking," which is a forecast about a pool servers presently dominate.

HPE also said it expects memory, NAND and other critical components to stay constrained to at least the second half of calendar 2028, and that it will continue passing price increases through. It guided gross margin rate to moderate later in fiscal 2027 as AI volumes build, with gross profit dollars still growing. The order book supports the volume: an AI backlog of $10.5bn, up more than 80%, on full-year orders of $57.2bn. Fiscal 2027 non-GAAP operating profit is guided up 23% to 30%. The shares sit at 10.2x forward earnings, the bottom of the 10-13x range they held in May and down from 16.2x in September.

Dell's own figure for what a rack earns

Dell, the largest AI server original equipment manufacturer by volume, reported record Infrastructure Solutions Group revenue of $31.8bn in its July quarter, with AI-optimized servers at $16.4bn. Segment operating income of $4.781bn is a 15.0% margin, against 8.8% a year earlier, on a record $95bn AI backlog.

The dilution is real but smaller than assumed. Chief financial officer David Kennedy has said the AI server business runs in line with its target of a mid-single-digit operating margin, below the group rate, so each incremental AI dollar lowers the blend while raising the absolute profit. Dell reports its October quarter after the close today, guided to about $49bn of revenue and roughly $19bn of AI servers, with segment margin expected up slightly more than a point year on year and memory a headwind all year. At 12.9x forward earnings, the stock is well under the roughly 18x it carried in May.

Super Micro's strain is in the working capital

Super Micro, the San Jose builder of liquid-cooled rack-scale systems, tripled September-quarter revenue to $19.9bn and lifted gross margin to 12.74% from 9.31%, though sequentially it fell from 17.5%. The pressure point is elsewhere. One data-center customer was 44% of revenue, and operations consumed $434m of cash.

"The largest single driver was customer prepayment," chief financial officer Mark Long told investors on 3 November. "Deferred revenue increased approximately $3.1 billion in the quarter. Accounts receivable grew $3.6 billion, and inventory grew $2.5 billion to support the revenue ahead of us, and prepayments funded most of that build." A trailing free-cash-flow yield of minus 40% and a 3.8x forward multiple are describing the same disclosed facts.

Who actually pays for the memory

On 23 November the Financial Times reported that Dell, HPE, Lenovo and Supermicro are demanding price increases of 25% to 45% on AI equipment months after contracts were agreed, and none would confirm whether their contracts contained clauses against price volatility. Buyers are paying: memory-heavy processor nodes have doubled or tripled while TrendForce has server DRAM staying undersupplied with contract prices up 10-15% in the fourth quarter. HPE last year amended its quoting terms so a booked order can be repriced for component cost between quote and shipment.

The shares did not wait for the disclosures. Dell is down 23.0% over three months and HPE 10.1%, with Super Micro off 33.4% and down 22.9% over twelve months, having never participated in the gain its two peers made. The worst of it was one market-wide session on 20 November, when US equities lost roughly $3.2tn and Nvidia stood at about half its 6 October value; three sessions later Nvidia rose about 31% and carried Dell up 22.5%, HPE 17.5% and Super Micro 14.6% in a day.

So the derating and the disclosures point opposite ways for two of the three, and the reason is who holds the scarce good. Dell and HPE allocate constrained accelerators and memory into order books they can reprice, and both expanded segment margin while AI revenue grew fastest. Super Micro sells speed of deployment rather than contract leverage, and its 44% customer funds the inventory through prepayments, which is why the same selling reads as a priced risk there instead of a mistake.

HPE says the parts stay scarce into the second half of 2028. That is a long run of quarters in which these companies quote a price before they know what the memory will cost, and each one re-asks the question this week's reporting answered for only one of them.
