TSMC Told Chinese Designers to Stop Ordering Below 16 Nanometers Under a New US Rule

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A Commerce Department rule published on 19 November took effect the day it appeared, stripping out the self-check that had let foundries clear Chinese designers' chips without an individual licence. TSMC then told those customers to stop placing orders at its more advanced nodes. China was 5% of its September-quarter revenue.

Nothing in the three foundries' own disclosures turned down. TSMC grew revenue 50.9% year on year with gross margin of 66.4%, UMC lifted capacity utilization to 92% from 85%, and GlobalFoundries posted its first year-on-year blended price increase since 2023. All three guided the December quarter up and none cut capital spending.

UMC fell hardest of the three over the past month, even though the rule's mechanical effect is to push blocked Chinese demand down into the 28- and 40-nanometer band where UMC sells.

The Bureau of Industry and Security's interim final rule on remote access to advanced semiconductors was published in the Federal Register on 19 November and took effect on publication, and its foundry provision removed the transistor-count self-determination route that had let contract manufacturers treat Chinese designers' chips as uncontrolled. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, which prints other companies' logic designs on advanced processes and supplies Nvidia, Apple and Broadcom, told Chinese chip designers to stop placing orders on 16-nanometer and more advanced processes unless they hold a US export licence.

That is the first thing inside this de-rating that is a foundry event rather than a market event. The earlier leg, in the first week of November, was a sector-wide unwind on data-center regulation fears. The new rule is dated, specific and narrow, and it arrives against three sets of September-quarter disclosures that all moved the other way: revenue accelerating, margins expanding, December guided up, capital budgets raised. The belief being tested is that the AI wafer order book has cracked. What the filings show is a licensing problem bounded by how much Chinese business these fabs actually had.

How little China was in the numbers

China was 5% of TSMC's net revenue in the September quarter, against 79% from North America. The clients reported affected are smartphone brands including Xiaomi, Oppo and Vivo, not the accelerator customers that drive the advanced-node mix. Nodes at 7nm and below were 79% of wafer revenue last quarter, with 3nm at 33% and the newly ramping 2nm at 8%.

The rest of TSMC's quarter is a straight extension of the AI build. Revenue grew 50.9% year on year, gross margin reached 66.4% against 59.5%, and management guided December revenue to $52.3-54.3bn with gross margin of 65-67%. Capital expenditure stayed at $60-64bn for 2026, with 2026-28 spending described as significantly higher than the past three years. "The gap between demand and supply are still quite big, and that is why we put all our strengths to increase the capacity," chairman and chief executive C.C. Wei said on the 15 October call. October revenue of NT$586.89bn was up 59.7% year on year, consistent with the 14% sequential rise the December guide assumes.

At $318.71 the shares carry 20.4x trailing earnings on a 51% net margin, against 33.7x in May.

The company the rule should help fell the hardest

United Microelectronics, a pure-play foundry selling mature-process wafers to fabless designers, is the awkward case. The rule's consequence is that blocked Chinese designers are generally limited to 28nm and 40nm processes provided they use no high-bandwidth memory or advanced packaging, and Taiwanese mature-node specialists are described as expecting relatively limited direct exposure. UMC fell 39.2% in the thirty days to 20 November, worse than TSMC's 25.3% or GlobalFoundries' 25.2%.

Its quarter was the strongest of the three in direction. Utilization rose to 92% from 85% on record shipments of 1.2m twelve-inch equivalents, gross margin reached 34.0%, and 22nm hit 18.3% of wafer sales, its highest ever. The board approved an extra $3.2bn of capital expenditure. "We expect 2027 to mark a turning point in our blended ASP trajectory, moving from an environment of ASP erosion toward a meaningful positive uplift beginning in the first quarter," chief executive Jason Wang said on 28 October, flagging a 10% blended price rise year on year.

Reported gross margin is the wrong place to judge the company, and its own finance chief says so. "For gross margins for next two years at least, depreciation, along with the rising raw material cost, is going to put pressures on our overall financial gross margin," chief financial officer Chitung Liu said the same day. On enterprise value to trailing EBITDA the stock is at 5.8x, down from 7.4x on 11 November, with a 6.9% trailing free-cash-flow yield.

The one with a US government shareholder

GlobalFoundries makes radio-frequency, power-management and embedded-memory parts from Malta, New York, and has no Chinese-designer order book at the restricted nodes. Revenue grew 12.5% to $1.899bn, non-IFRS gross margin set a record at 31.4%, and blended selling prices rose 4.8% year on year, which chief financial officer Sam Franklin on 4 November called "the first quarter of year-over-year blended pricing improvement since 2023." More than two-thirds of revenue came from outside smart mobile for the first time; communications infrastructure and data center reached 17% of the quarter, up 81%.

Chief executive Tim Breen told investors the same day: "We are absolutely booked out through 2027, increasingly booked out through 2028, and the question for us is how fast we can add more capacity to meet their requirements in 2028." Net capital expenditure of about $4bn is planned for 2027, roughly a third funded by customer prepayments and about $760m by government grants, after the company issued a further 4% of equity to the US government. Reported earnings are unusable as an anchor: IFRS diluted EPS was -$0.49 against $0.53 non-IFRS. Price to trailing gross profit is 9.5x against 20.4x in May, while that gross profit grew 36%.

What the rule can and cannot account for

None of the move is explained by the businesses. Wafer pricing across the shelf is rising, with 2027 foundry increases of 10% to more than 20% being notified to designers, and the buyer of the leading-edge wafers did not weaken either: Nvidia reported revenue up 103% year on year on 17 November with a bullish outlook, five days after Jensen Huang's resignation.

The rule is real and its licensing friction is real, but the split in where it lands has a cause: TSMC's exposure is a small, named slice of consumer customers at nodes it is capacity-constrained on anyway; GlobalFoundries is part-owned by the government writing the rule; UMC sits in the node band blocked designers get pushed toward. The uniformity of the price response does not follow from any of that. What does cover all three at once is location. Taiwan's Taiex lost 17.5% in the week to 20 November, its worst five sessions since 1990, with MSCI's emerging-Asia index down nearly 9% — the likelier reading is a regional unwind in which a licensing rule supplied the trigger and the node-by-node economics supplied nothing.

The separate half of the rule, covering AI chips and servers sold to data centers anywhere that may serve restricted-country compute, does not bite for 90 days. Until then, the first disclosure that can show whether orders actually stopped is a monthly revenue line, and November's is not published until December.

Originating hypothesis

category emerging bear wafer economics by node · subject: TSM, UMC, GFS

The foundry shelf — the companies that own the fabs everyone else's AI silicon is printed in — has flipped to an emerging bear at gradual intensity (segment average -29.6% over 30 days against +24.3% over twelve months) with TSMC, UMC and GlobalFoundries sliding together, and the question is mechanical rather than directional: trace what a single wafer actually earns at each tier of this business — TSMC's N2/A16 ramp, where wafer price, customer prepayments and multi-year capex are contracted years ahead, versus UMC's and GlobalFoundries' mature 28nm-and-above capacity, where the P&L is a pure function of loading against fixed depreciation and Chinese mature-node price competition — and establish whether the past 30-60 days contain any real change in monthly revenue, node pricing, utilization or capex guidance, or whether one de-rating has been applied indiscriminately to two businesses that move on different clocks.
