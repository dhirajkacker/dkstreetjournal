In fiscal 2026 the company retired Digital Media segment reporting for two broad customer groups, publishing total ending annual recurring revenue of $27.50bn, up 11.2%, and monthly active users past 1 billion, but no paid subscriber count.

Figma, which does, reported 136% net dollar retention.

Adobe's August quarter was the largest in its history, and it grew faster than the two quarters before it. The disclosure an investor would need to learn whether paying creative seats are still being added is no longer published.

That matters because the de-rating of design software was built on seats, not revenue. Per-seat pricing fell from 21% to 15% of software companies in twelve months while hybrid pricing rose from 27% to 41%, and roughly $2 trillion of business software market value was erased in 2026 on the thesis that AI agents cut the number of human logins a customer needs. Settling that argument at Adobe, which sells Creative Cloud, Acrobat and a marketing suite by subscription to professional creators and enterprises, requires a subscriber count. In fiscal 2026 Adobe stopped reporting Digital Media and Digital Experience revenue and moved to two customer groups, reporting total company annual recurring revenue in place of Digital Media ARR, with no paid subscriber figure and no segment net new ARR.

Volume up, monetization postponed

What remains points away from a shrinking base. Ending ARR was $27.50bn, up 11.2%, with monthly active users past 1 billion and creative free-tier users above 100 million, up more than 70%. Revenue rose 12.9% to $6.76bn, after 12.7% and 12.0% in the two prior quarters.

The figure the market seized on was a roughly 36-37% year-over-year fall in net new ARR, arithmetic an analyst performed on the September 10 call that management did not validate or break into seats, price and free-tier effects. Management's explanation was a choice: it deprioritized Creative Cloud pricing optimization and redirected free-tier traffic to maximize user acquisition, calibrating conversion timing later. The lever exists and was recently pulled, with Creative Cloud Pro raised from $59.99 to $69.99 a month, 16.7%, effective June 17, 2025 in the US, alongside an increase in included Firefly generative credits from 3,000 to 4,000. It is being held in reserve.

"I see immense opportunity for Adobe to be the leader in Agentic software for creativity, productivity, and customer experience," said Anil Chakravarthy on the September 10 call. He becomes chief executive on December 1, 2026, succeeding Shantanu Narayen after 29 years.

The meters that did weaken

Remaining performance obligations, the contracted backlog, grew 8% — single-digit for the first time since early fiscal 2023 — with current obligations up 9%; management tied both to conversion timing and a focus on active users over near-term billings. Gross margin slipped to 88.1% from 89.3%, and operating income grew 8.3% against revenue's 12.9%. The AI products themselves are small and fast: total AI-first ending ARR above $650m, more than 150% higher, with Firefly ARR up 40% in a quarter.

The shares closed at $242.27 on October 9, down 30% over twelve months and 5% over thirty days, through a month in which the Federal Reserve raised its target range to 3.75-4.00%. At 13.5 times trailing earnings and 9.9 times forward, on consensus earnings of $24.48 a share for fiscal 2026 (up 17.6%), Adobe carries roughly half the forward multiple of Autodesk and an 11.4% trailing free-cash-flow yield.

Neither peer closes the gap

Autodesk, which sells AutoCAD, Revit and a construction platform to architects, engineers and contractors, discloses no seat count either, and its forward meters disagree with each other: billings up 10% and current obligations up 12% against total obligations up only 2%, all below revenue growth of 16%, with net revenue retention at the top of its 100-110% range and operating income up 33%. Its 11.0% gain over five sessions was a read-through from Schneider Electric's $23.7bn cash bid for PTC at about 9.4 times recurring revenue, not Autodesk news; it trades at 18.7 times forward earnings against 30.3 trailing.

Figma, the browser-based design platform that listed in 2025, is the one that counts: 136% net dollar retention and 15,964 customers above $10,000 of ARR, up 34%, with AI credits embedded inside the seat rather than sold beside it, as revenue accelerated to 48.2% growth. Its shares are down 66.9% over twelve months, part of that explained by an extended lock-up covering about 54.1% of Class A shares releasing in tranches. At 8.6 times trailing sales against consensus growth halving to 24% next year, it is normalized rather than cheap.

What the numbers can and cannot settle

Nothing in these three disclosures shows a creative seat base in retreat. Adobe is adding users at a rate that would be impossible if demand for its tools were collapsing, and Figma's expansion rate says customers buying AI features are buying more seats alongside them. But Adobe's revenue line would look the same whether its paid base were growing slowly at a higher price or shrinking behind the one-time 16.7% increase and credit consumption, and the company has removed the series that distinguished those cases. The weakening meters that remain — backlog growth at 8%, margin down a point, operating income growing slower than sales — are consistent with the deliberate trade-off described on the call, which is also what the bear case predicts. The likelier reading is deferred monetization rather than erosion, held as a judgment rather than a measurement.

Adobe is now asking to be valued on a free user base of 100 million people, which is a bet that conversion is a timing question. Its own reporting no longer contains the line that would show the conversion happening.

GE Vernova is building gas turbines faster than it said it would a year ago, and that is the most consequential thing it has told investors since July. Chief executive Scott Strazik disclosed at the Morgan Stanley Laguna conference on 16 September that the company is accelerating its gas capacity additions by 50% this year, with factory output reaching a 5 gigawatt quarterly run-rate in the second half of 2026, 6 in the second half of 2027 and 7 in 2028. New orders are already pricing 10 to 20 points higher per kilowatt than at the end of 2025. Scarcity is what that price is, and the three companies that own roughly 90% of the heavy-duty market are each dismantling it on schedule.

The belief being tested is that a sold-out slot book through 2032 is the same thing as a decade of contracted revenue. It is not what the disclosure says.

What a reserved slot is worth before anyone orders anything

A gas-turbine dollar arrives in three separable forms. First comes the deposit: GE Vernova will not label a customer commitment a slot reservation agreement until roughly 20% of contract price is posted in cash, money that lands in working capital and a contract liability. In the June quarter working capital was a $6.4bn cash benefit "driven primarily by higher down payments on increased orders and slot reservations at Power." Then the firm equipment order. Then the annuity: long-term service agreements that run 5 to 25 years, with more than 70% of the existing book carrying over a decade of remaining life and about 70% renewal rates. Service revenues were approximately 70% of Power segment revenues in 2022, and aftermarket margins substantially exceed new-unit margins.

"Every gigawatt of HAs will generate $0.5 billion of high-margin services revenue over the first 20 years of the cycle," Strazik said on 16 September. The profit is real and it is distant.

Which is why 116 gigawatts is not 116 gigawatts of revenue. The June-quarter split was 53 gigawatts of firm backlog against 63 of reservations; the company converted 10 gigawatts of reservations into orders and shipped 3. Management said it expects to reach the point where firm backlog exceeds reserved gigawatts in the second half of 2026. Gordon Johnson of GLJ Research used that same 54% reservation share to initiate a Sell with a $470 target, and the stock fell 8.6% in September.

The results and the price moved opposite ways

The operating record is accelerating. Revenue rose 21.9% to $11.1bn in the June quarter, operating income 73%, and Power segment orders reached $16.7bn, up 134% organically, with segment EBITDA margin of 18.8%, up 320 basis points organically on volume and favorable price at Gas Power.

The shares nonetheless entered a sustained downtrend on 23 September and have stayed there, as have Caterpillar, Cummins and Mitsubishi Heavy. GE Vernova closed at $1,002.08 on 9 October, still up 58% over twelve months. At 33.0x forward earnings the stock looks cheaper than it is: consensus FY2026 earnings of $30.49 a share contain the $4.5bn pre-tax Prolec GE gain, and FY2027 consensus falls 19.6% to $24.51, which puts the real figure near 41x against roughly 37x forward in May.

Mitsubishi Heavy is the supply-side gauge, and it is loosening. Its large-frame backlog stands at about 35 gigawatts, it plans 30% more turbine capacity by FY2028 and has since discussed doubling against 2024 levels; Caterpillar is spending $725m to roughly 2.5x Solar Turbines output by 2030. Mitsubishi's own June-quarter revenue slipped 0.7% while operating income rose 40%, and the shares are down 22.5% over six months at 30.9x trailing earnings with a 7.8% free-cash-flow yield.

The half of Cummins being marked down is trucks

Cummins is the one name where the decline tracks the business. Power Systems, the standby and data-centre genset arm, posted sales of $2.3bn, up 19%, at a 24.5% EBITDA margin against 22.8%. Chief executive Jennifer Rumsey credited "disciplined execution and robust demand for data center backup power." The truck-levered majority went the other way: Engine EBITDA margin fell to 12.5% from 13.8% and Components to 13.2% from 14.7%, so revenue up 9.4% produced operating income up only 4.3%. ACT Research put September Class 8 orders at 18,700 units, down 9.5%, with the EPA 2027 pre-buy effectively over. At 17.7x forward earnings against roughly 24x in May, the de-rating has already removed most of the premium.

So the shelf splits on cause. Cummins' markdown is a truck cycle correctly priced; GE Vernova's and Mitsubishi's is not visible in any results line, and with no turbine order cancellation disclosed in the window, the likelier reading is multiple compression against broad doubt about artificial-intelligence capital budgets and a 10-year Treasury yield of 5.24%, which discounts 2032 deliveries harder than 2027 ones. The company-specific crack is narrower and real: GE Vernova's gas customers are roughly 80% utilities and only 20% data centres, so the demand it is being valued on reaches it second-hand, through someone else's procurement committee.

GE Vernova's third-quarter report lands on 28 October. The number that matters is not how many gigawatts the company has lined up, but how many of them a customer has actually ordered.

Enphase Energy, which makes the microinverters that turn a rooftop array's direct current into household alternating current, has spent 2026 shipping a large volume of equipment that nobody will install for years. On 7 October Deutsche Bank downgraded the shares and put arithmetic on what that leaves behind: set aside roughly $255m of safe-harbour sales booked this year, and consensus estimates for 2027 require core organic revenue to grow about 33%. The bank's own 2027 adjusted earnings number, $1.73 a share, sits 21% below the Street.

That matters because the stock's falling price-to-earnings ratio has been read as a business getting cheaper. It is better read as a denominator getting smaller. Enphase trades at 32.2x trailing earnings and 16.5x forward, but its forward price-to-sales multiple of 3.7x sits above the 3.3x trailing, and forward price-to-gross-profit of 7.8x above 7.0x trailing: consensus expects next year's sales and gross profit to be lower than this year's. Full-year 2025 revenue was $1.47bn; the 2026 estimate is $1.18bn, with 2027 growth of under 2%.

The deadline that pulled the sales forward

The Section 25D credit that paid homeowners a share of a cash or loan purchase ended for any system installed after 31 December 2025, with no phase-out. What survives in 2026 is Section 48E, claimable only by the company that owns a leased or power-purchase system, and 48E ends for solar placed in service after 31 December 2027 unless construction began by 4 July 2026. Buying hardware early is how a leasing company starts construction on paper, which is why the purchases clustered into mid-2026.

Enphase is the supplier of that hardware. Safe-harbour revenue was $84.3m in the June quarter against $34.5m in March, inside total revenue of $291.9m that fell 19.6% year on year. Third-quarter guidance of $290-320m includes about $75m of safe-harbour shipments, roughly a quarter of the midpoint.

The underlying channel is weaker than the revenue line. US revenue fell about 3% in the June quarter while US sell-through, what distributors actually sold on to installers, fell 34%; European revenue rose about 35%. Enphase exited the quarter with channel inventory normal for batteries and slightly elevated for microinverters, and guided to under-shipping in the September quarter to work it down. The reported 60% gross margin, meanwhile, was lifted 15.6 points by $45.4m of tariff refunds credited against cost of revenues.

Chief executive Badri Kothandaraman has not hidden where this goes. The industry "must evolve rapidly" toward leases and power purchase agreements, he said in remarks reported on 24 July 2025, adding that his "personal view is that we will see a 20% drop" in the residential market from 2025 to 2026.

A rival grew into the same de-rating

SolarEdge Technologies, which sells string inverters and power optimizers from its base in Herzliya, Israel, ran the other way. June-quarter revenue rose 19.6% to $346.2m and GAAP gross margin reached 27.5% from 11.1% a year earlier, delivering its first non-GAAP operating profit since 2023, $10.2m. "Strong demand in Europe combined with strength in U.S. C&I, more than offset industry-wide softness in U.S. residential," chief executive Shuki Nir said of the quarter. Then the company guided September revenue to $310-340m, below what it had just delivered, citing US residential uncertainty, and the shares fell about 24%.

On price per dollar of gross profit the two are now within a whisker: 6.6x for the Israeli company against 7.0x for Enphase, whose multiple is down from 14.55x in May. The market is pricing the shared policy dollar rather than either company's reported trend.

The demand did not move house

The tidy version of this story has volume migrating from homeowners to leasing companies. Sunrun, the largest US residential solar and storage financier, says otherwise: June-quarter storage installed fell 15% year on year to 332 MWh and aggregate subscriber value fell 24% to $1.2bn, with full-year guidance cut to $4.6-4.9bn from $4.8-5.2bn. Value per customer rose 10%, to $55,033, on bigger systems and a 74% battery attachment rate. Fewer, larger, better-monetized systems. The market itself installed 995 MWdc in the quarter, the segment's weakest in five years, and Wood Mackenzie and the Solar Energy Industries Association now model a 23% contraction for 2026.

So the de-rating that took both inverter names out of uptrends on 7 July has a cause the earnings do not contradict. Enphase's profits are holding up on falling sales because tariff refunds and credit monetization are doing work a price increase would normally do, while the sales themselves are partly a deadline being met. The Israeli company's recovery is real and geographic, and its own guidance says US housing is not the source. A 10-year Treasury yield at 5.24%, its highest since 2002, sets the loan rate a homeowner now pays without a credit, and it is the context both stocks have been trading on.

The September quarter is the first in which the pre-buy shrinks rather than grows. Whatever core demand is left will be visible in it, unobscured by a deadline that has already passed.

Prudential Financial announced on 5 October that Prismic Life Reinsurance, the Bermuda company it launched with Warburg Pincus in 2023 and took a 20% interest in, will reinsure about $5bn of reserves backing dollar-denominated Japanese whole-life policies written by its Japanese affiliates. The June quarter at these companies was read as the higher-yield story finally arriving: assets bought near 5% standing behind promises priced years ago. The disclosures describe something else doing much of the work. Liabilities are leaving the ceding company's books, the capital behind them is released and spent on shares, and adjusted earnings are growing several times faster than the equity they are earned on.

Twenty percent on earnings, three percent on the book

MetLife, which sells group life, dental and disability cover to employers alongside annuities, funding agreements and pension-risk-transfer buyouts, reported adjusted earnings up 15% to $1.6bn and adjusted earnings per share up 20% to $2.43, with adjusted return on equity of 17% for a second consecutive quarter, the top of its target range. Adjusted book value per share, the measure that strips out the mark on its bond portfolio, rose 3% to $57.71. Part of that gap is retired shares; part is the hedging and derivative marks that adjusted earnings exclude and book value absorbs.

Behind the buybacks sits Chariot Re, the Bermuda vehicle MetLife started with General Atlantic, whose inaugural $10bn transaction took on structured settlement annuities and group annuity contracts from pension risk transfers MetLife had originated, and which MetLife expected to raise available capital by roughly $1bn. At 30 June the arrangement carried an $11.1bn funds-withheld liability and a $10.9bn receivable. On 5 August the board authorized $3bn of fresh repurchases.

The spread dollar itself wobbled

The owned-asset half of the model had a poor quarter. Variable investment income, the private-equity and real-estate fund line reported on a one-quarter lag, came in at $231m against the roughly $400m a quarter implied by full-year guidance of about $1.6bn. "Total investment spread was 97 basis points in the second quarter, below our guidance range of 100 to 120 basis points, driven by weaker private equity returns within VII," chief financial officer John McCallion said on the second-quarter call.

That did not persist. Around 24 September MetLife pre-announced preliminary third-quarter variable investment income of at least $600m ahead of its 4 November release. The origination end was the softer one: US pension-risk-transfer buyout and buy-in sales totalled about $3bn in the second quarter, down 26% from a year earlier, on 176 contracts with no single deal above $250m.

Prudential's fee half has not cured its discount

Prudential's asset manager PGIM earned $294m of adjusted operating income against $229m a year earlier on $1.491 trillion of assets, with $4.6bn of third-party net inflows offset by $3.0bn of affiliated outflows. Group after-tax adjusted operating income reached $4.08 a share, up 14%, while adjusted book value per share grew 4.7% to $100.91.

Prudential is the cheapest of the three at 7.8x forward earnings against 10.1x trailing, roughly 1.1x that adjusted book. MetLife, on the same disclosed figures, trades near 1.7x. The likelier cause is the forward path rather than the printed quarter: consensus has Prudential earning $14.45 a share this year, a shade below last year's $14.50, and $14.91 next, while MetLife's is set to rise about 17% to $10.15.

Manulife's cession is the defensive version of the same trade. Core earnings rose 12% to C$1.923bn with Asia annualized premium equivalent sales up 21%, and the Munich Re deal was largely capital-neutral, worth about C$30m a year, while cutting cumulative long-term-care morbidity sensitivity by 24%. Its last annual actuarial assumption review was favourable, reducing pre-tax fulfilment cash flows by $605m; the next one is implemented in third-quarter results. The shares trade at 9.5x forward against 16.3x trailing.

What the twelve months do and do not explain

Over twelve months to 9 October, Manulife gained 33%, MetLife 20% and Prudential 10%, with Prudential down 4% over the trailing thirty days and its trend softening a notch on 17 September, Manulife's on 2 October.

The operating record covers the gains. It does not cover Prudential's discount, which tracks a flat earnings path and an unsettled question about what stands behind ceded reserves. US life insurers ceded $2.4 trillion of reserves in 2024, more than $1.1 trillion of it offshore, and state regulators are exploring capital charges for recapture risk and for weaker reinsurers' credit; Cayman-based reinsurers were contesting the proposals as of 7 October. The fee income meant to replace spread is still tiny: MetLife's newly separated investment-management segment earned $57m in the quarter, against $1.6bn for the group.

The capital freed offshore has already been converted into retired shares. The reserves it was holding sit with reinsurers whose required capital is still being argued over in front of the people who write the rules, and each of these three now reports its earnings on a balance sheet a little smaller than the business it sold.

Fabrinet, the Thailand-based contract manufacturer that assembles optical transceivers and modules under other companies' brands, closed its fiscal year in June with quarterly revenue up 45% and data-center work past half of sales for the first time. Its gross margin was 12.27%, against 12.23% a year earlier.

The year has been read as Fabrinet claiming its share of the 800-gigabit and 1.6-terabit upgrade. The accounts say it claimed the volume and none of the mix. Gross margin has been pinned between 11.6% and 12.7% in each of the last eight quarters and each of the last five fiscal years, a band that does not move with revenue, because Fabrinet is paid a fee for building to customer specification with customer-specified content passing through its revenue. Growth buys operating leverage below the gross line and nothing above it.

The fee holds at twelve cents whatever the volume

Full-year revenue rose 35.7% to $4.641bn and operating income rose 42.7%, lifting GAAP operating margin to 10.48% in the June quarter from 9.63% in September 2025. On the company's own non-GAAP basis, gross margin was 12.2%, ten basis points better sequentially and thirty worse year on year, while non-GAAP operating margin of 10.9% was the highest in three years. Both readings agree: a 45% revenue gain did not widen the fee.

The demand is not in question. Data-center revenue reached $669m, up 68% year on year and 51% of the total, covering transceivers, data-center interconnect, silicon photonics and co-packaged optics. "Our fourth quarter was exceptional, capping off a remarkable year of accelerating growth and strong momentum," chief executive Seamus Grady said in the results release of 17 August 2026. Reported growth accelerated through all four quarters of the year, from 21.6% to 44.6%, and consensus has fiscal 2027 revenue at $6.09bn, a mild deceleration rather than a break.

Four customers, 57% of the year

What Fabrinet manages is not demand but who the demand belongs to. Cisco at 19.9%, NVIDIA at 16.3%, Nokia at 10.7% and Amazon at 10.5% each cleared the 10% disclosure line in fiscal 2026, 57.4% of revenue between them, so one program transition decides a quarter. At Rosenblatt's technology summit after the results, management said the data-center interconnect line had reached a $1bn run rate, up 140%, and that it would build interconnect products for NVIDIA's competitors — concentration, named as the risk being worked on.

The capacity plan is the other side of the fee. Fabrinet exited the year at a $5.3bn annualized run rate with a stated path toward $12.5bn-$14bn: office conversions at Pinehurst to $5.5bn-$5.8bn, Building 10 at Chonburi adding $3bn-$3.5bn by early 2027, two further Chonburi plants of roughly 1.2m square feet each adding $1.8bn-$2.1bn apiece. Scale is the product.

Where the link's margin actually sits

Rank the most recent reported quarters along the bill of materials of an optical link and the gross margin falls as you move from light to labor. Lumentum, which makes the lasers, modulators and transceiver engines, earned 47.4% on revenue up 109%, fourteen percentage points better than a year earlier. Amphenol, the connector and interconnect maker whose IT datacom market was 43% of second-quarter sales and grew 89% on record orders and a 1.23:1 book-to-bill, earned 40.5%, up four points. Corning's consolidated glass, fiber and connectivity margin was 36.1%, flat. Fabrinet's was 12.3%.

Price runs the other way. Fabrinet's shares carry 31.3x trailing and 23.9x forward gross profit; Amphenol's carry 19.2x trailing. The chain's thinnest layer holds its dearest gross-profit dollar.

What fell, and what did it

Fabrinet is down 26.2% over six months and up 15.4% over thirty days, and the gross-profit multiple has compressed by roughly a third from the 47.7x of 11 May 2026. Nothing in the operating record explains that: revenue growth, data-center share and operating margin all moved the other way. The identifiable shock was financing, not demand. When Corning disclosed a $2bn at-the-market equity program in mid-September its shares fell as much as 13.6%, and Coherent dropped 11%, Lumentum 9% and Fabrinet 6% in the same session. The 9 October carrier rout on SpaceX's agreement to buy 800-megahertz spectrum licences never reached the suppliers: Corning closed 0.4% lower, Fabrinet 0.3%.

Corning itself has kept de-rating at the margin, to 21.9x trailing gross profit against 26.3x in May, with quarterly revenue growth easing from 20.4% to 16.6% and consolidated gross margin flat. It reports third-quarter results on 27 October against management guidance of about 16% core sales growth to $4.9bn-$5.0bn and core earnings of $0.85-$0.89 a share.

So the markdown across the optical complex is not one judgment about photons. It is three differently paid businesses: an engine maker whose margin widens with speed, a connector maker whose margin widens with volume, and an assembler whose margin is a contract term. Only the first two capture the upgrade in the price of what they ship. Fabrinet captures it in square feet.

Two new Chonburi factories are meant to add about $2bn of revenue each. On the fee Fabrinet has earned for five years, each of them brings home roughly a quarter of a billion dollars of gross profit, and the content inside still belongs to somebody else.

Lincoln Electric sold more welding wire, electrode and robotic cells in its June quarter than in any quarter of its history, and only about two of the ten points of organic growth came from shipping more of it. The rest was price. The Cleveland company, founded in 1895 and selling arc welding equipment and consumables through industrial distributors, reported net sales up 12.0% to a record $1,220m, with organic sales up 10.1% split roughly 8 points of price, 2 points of volume, 1.5 points from the Alloy Steel acquisition and 40 basis points of currency.

That quarter was taken as evidence that the American industrial cycle had turned under the price increases. It is better evidence of something narrower: Lincoln is paid in metal it passes through, and the pass-through is what laps.

The dollar is burned, not bought

Most of what Lincoln ships is consumed. On 2025 revenue the split was roughly $2.28bn of consumables against $1.08bn of equipment and $870m of automation, and Lincoln's North American brand share across the complete equipment-plus-consumables package is estimated near 40%. A stick electrode is reordered because a factory ran a shift; a robotic cell is a quoted project that can be deferred. Both sit in one revenue line, and they can move in opposite directions inside it.

In the June quarter they moved the same way. Americas Welding sales rose 11.2% to $774.4m with volume contributing 7.1 of those points, equipment organic sales accelerated to high-teens growth, and consumable volumes grew at a low-double-digit rate that management put in line with general industrial production. Automation sales were $229m on a record backlog. "Second quarter marked a solid inflection to volume growth in the business after nine quarters of compression," chief executive Steven Hedlund said on the July 30 call. One quarter earlier, pricing had contributed 10.4 points while volumes fell 2.6%.

The cost side explains where the price came from. US hot-rolled coil averaged about $1,213 a ton in September and the weekly mill benchmark rose for three consecutive weeks with 50% steel tariffs capping imports. Nucor has raised prices eight weeks running. Lincoln's own list prices follow, late.

Where the brazing adders go next

The clearest price-masked volume is the Harris Products Group, Lincoln's gas-equipment and brazing-alloy business, which is about a sixth of group sales. Harris revenue rose 26.9% to $201.9m with pricing contributing 34.2 percentage points, mechanical adders that pass silver and copper through, while volume fell 8.2% on weak HVAC and retail channels. Silver set a record of $121.62 an ounce on January 29 and traded at $61.56 on October 6. When the comparison laps, that contribution runs in reverse against volume already shrinking.

International Welding is the other soft leg: adjusted operating profit down 13% to $27m on volumes off about 5% in Europe, with the Middle East conflict costing the segment $6m-$7m of sales a quarter.

Then the capex leg arrived separately from the quarter. Shares fell 8.4% intraday on September 10 after chief financial officer Gabe Bruno told the Jefferies industrials conference that customers were delaying final investment decisions on major capital projects, that automotive production was down mid-single digits, and that Americas pricing actions only became effective in September.

What the results account for, and what they do not

Operating income grew 23.9% on revenue up 12.1% and margin reached 18.38% while gross margin slipped 40 basis points: the earnings came from leverage on volume, which is the part of the mix that is one quarter old. The group's reported growth stands on price that the metal market is now unwinding at Harris and only began flowing in the Americas last month. Lincoln is 9.5% below its August 27 record close and still up 11.4% over twelve months, so the market has marked down the price contribution without disputing the volume turn. At 23.4x forward earnings against 26.0x trailing, it is discounting barely a year of the 14.9% earnings growth consensus carries for 2026. September's ISM manufacturing reading of 54.5 with prices paid at 77.9 says factory hours are expanding and input costs faster.

ESAB, which sells the same consumables-and-equipment package through independent distributors with roughly two-thirds of revenue in consumables, shows the geography running the other way: second-quarter revenue up 12.9% to $807.6m but only 2.5% organic, with double-digit Middle East declines, and full-year adjusted profit guidance raised to $615m-$625m. "We expect to mitigate the transitory cost inflation related to logistics and commodity price increases over the next few quarters," chief executive Shyam Kambeyanda said on the August 6 call. The shares are down 43.5% over twelve months at 12.3x forward earnings, carrying total debt that went from $1.24bn to $2.42bn for the $1.45bn Eddyfi inspection deal.

Lincoln's third-quarter report comes before the open on October 27. It is the first quarter in which the price contribution starts to shrink on its own, and the first in which the wire has to move by itself.

Mid-America Apartment Communities has spent three weeks losing a full turn of its valuation without changing a word of its guidance. The Sunbelt apartment owner, which held interests in roughly 102,772 units across 16 states and the District of Columbia, closed at $124.82 on 16 September and $116.31 on 9 October. Against full-year Core funds from operations guidance of $8.35 to $8.71 a share, unchanged through both prints, the midpoint of $8.53 puts the shares at 13.6 times that cash-flow measure, down from 14.6 times — arithmetic from the guidance and the closes, not a disclosed figure.

September's improving lease data was read as the start of an apartment recovery. What moved was the denominator. The Federal Reserve raised its target range a quarter point to 3.75%-4.00% on 16 September, its first increase in more than three years, and the 10-year Treasury touched 5.349% on 5 October, its highest since April 2002, easing to 5.23% by 9 October. MAA's quarterly dividend of $1.53 a share, $6.12 annualized, is a 5.26% yield on that close. The risk-free note pays essentially the same for none of the leverage, and September's 54-point rise in the 10-year was 49 points of real yields with almost no inflation component. The FTSE Nareit All Equity REITs index fell 5.7% that month.

The spread that turned three quarters running

The rent roll is genuinely better. MAA's blended lease-over-lease spreads ran -1.7% in the fourth quarter of 2025, -0.3% in the first quarter of 2026 and +0.7% in the second, composed of new leases still 5.3% below the expiring rent against renewals up 5.2%, on 95.3% occupancy. "We expect third quarter blended pricing to be better than the second quarter, a trend not seen in the last four years," chief operating officer Tim Argo told analysts on 29 July 2026. At Bank of America's global real estate conference on 16 September the company said third-quarter blended rates were running three to five percentage points better year on year, renewals near 4.8%, with new-lease pricing still negative and deliveries still pressing Austin, Phoenix and Nashville. The cost side helps too: insurance premiums renewed more than 12% lower, a third straight annual decline, and same-store expense growth guidance came down to about 1.75%.

None of it has reached the income statement.

Revenue grew 0.95% year on year in the second quarter while operating income fell 7.0%, and the annual operating margin has compressed from 32.1% in 2023 to 28.0% in 2025. Consensus has earnings per share falling to $3.66 this year and $3.14 next.

Independence Realty is withdrawing the free rent

The smaller case is cleaner because concessions are visible. Independence Realty Trust, a $3.46bn owner of apartment communities in non-gateway markets including Atlanta, Louisville, Memphis and Raleigh, improved blended rent growth from +0.7% in the first quarter to +1.3% in the second and +2.5% in July, and cut concession use from 54% of new leases in April to roughly 28% in July. Weeks of free rent never show up in asking rent; they show up in effective rent, which is what the owner banks. Its renovation program turned 600 units in the quarter at a 16.4% weighted average return for $279 of extra monthly rent against unrenovated comparables.

IRT trades at 1.03 times book against MAA's 2.48 times, yet its 15.4 times trailing enterprise value to EBITDA is indistinguishable from MAA's 15.2 times: no discount for class-B assets, no premium for the concession reversal. Its own shares are also partly an arbitrage now, with Centerspace holders voting on 10 November on a merger paying 3.800 IRT shares for each of theirs.

What the recovery can and cannot repair

The operating inflection explains none of the three-week de-rating and the discount rate explains most of it: guidance did not move, the 10-year did. What the lease data does establish is that the Sunbelt supply wave is passing through. Multifamily starts in buildings of five units or more fell to 344,000 annualized in August, down 22.5% in a month, and completions were 25.6% lower year on year in July at 329,000. The cure for a transformer shortage is more transformer factories, and the industry has committed to a wave of them. Eaton, the power-management group that builds switchgear and utility distribution gear, is putting up a $340m transformer plant in South Carolina targeting 2027 production; Siemens Energy has a $150m plant in North Carolina. A 128-week quote is still a 128-week quote

ONE Gas sold less gas in the June quarter than it had a year earlier and made more money doing it. Revenue fell 2.9% to $411.6m as the commodity it buys for customers got cheaper, while operating income rose 15.1% to $82.7m. On 5 August the company raised its 2026 adjusted earnings outlook to the upper half of its range, $4.89 to $4.95 a share.

The shares went the other way. ONE Gas is 19% below its 52-week high at $73.02, and on 6 October seven of the eight listed natural gas distributors stood in established downtrends, having flipped within days of one another at the start of the month. The calendar tightness is the clue: the ten-year Treasury was 5.27% on 6 October after the Federal Reserve raised its target range on 16 September, and the utilities sector exchange-traded fund fell about 6% over the month while the S&P 500 barely moved. The group is being repriced as a bond substitute, which assumes the businesses inside it are interchangeable regulated pipes. One of the three examined here fits that description.

Why a 42% warm quarter barely registered

ONE Gas delivers to about 2.2 million customers in Oklahoma, Kansas and Texas over roughly 41,600 miles of distribution mains. It earns a regulated return on that steel; the gas itself passes through to customers at cost, which is why the commodity swings reported revenue while the rate order sets margin. Operating margin widened to 20.1% from 17.0%, on $16.4m of new rate revenue plus customer growth. The quarter ran 42% warmer than normal, an effect the company says was "mitigated by weather normalization mechanisms."

That makes capital spending the growth line and regulatory lag the brake.

ONE Gas expects average rate base of about $6.3bn in 2026 against capital investment of about $800m, with allowed returns on equity of 9.4% in Oklahoma and 9.5% in Kansas and a 9.8% partial settlement in Texas awaiting a Railroad Commission decision. The limit is the interval between spending and recovery. The company's June-quarter filing states that its rates "do not generally provide for a return on investment for amounts the company has deferred as regulatory assets": lag, described as a balance that earns nothing.

New Jersey Natural Gas put a number on that interval. Its 1 June filing with New Jersey's Board of Public Utilities seeks a $157.6m delivery increase on a $4,046.1m rate base at a requested 10.10% return on equity, covering roughly $950m spent since its last case concluded in 2024. It was lodged alongside cuts to the pass-through gas supply and conservation charges, so the net residential bill falls 8.9% from 1 October, even as residential gas bills nationally are forecast 8% to 14% higher this winter. Cheaper supply bought room for a bigger delivery charge.

The one whose quarter actually got worse

NiSource is classified with the gas distributors but runs an electric utility serving about half a million customers in northern Indiana alongside its Columbia gas operations, and it is the only one of the three where earnings deteriorated. Adjusted earnings of $0.16 a share missed the $0.23 consensus on storm damage and workforce-continuity costs, operating income fell 13.2%, and the shares dropped about 5% on the 5 August print. Its $28.6bn 2026-30 capital plan carries $7.6bn of data-center infrastructure behind 9% to 11% rate-base growth, funded with $400m to $600m of equity a year. At 2.04x book and 21.4x trailing earnings against 24.9x in May, it is the dearest of the three and the only one whose quarter argues for the markdown.

New Jersey Resources has the opposite shape. Energy Services, its unregulated wholesale gas marketing book, earned $84.5m of net financial earnings in nine months against $39.4m a year earlier on gas price volatility, and is guided at 21% to 23% of fiscal 2026 earnings. Consensus has earnings per share easing to $3.44 in fiscal 2027 from $3.58, and at 1.98x book its forward price-to-earnings ratio of 15.1x sits above the trailing 14.2x. A declining base is being priced.

Two of the three earned their markdown

At NiSource the results account for the de-rating; at New Jersey Resources the forward estimates do. At ONE Gas they account for none of it. A fully regulated distributor that raised guidance, grew adjusted earnings per share 52% in the quarter and landed three separate recovery outcomes now trades at 1.30x book and 15.7x trailing earnings, against the 20.2x it carried in May, with market value down to $4.58bn from $5.53bn. "Solid execution across the business and the continued strength of our growth strategy, supported by constructive jurisdictions," chief executive Sid McAnnally said of the quarter on 5 August. What did change is the cost of funding the plan: the diluted share count rose 4.5% year on year and trailing free cash flow is negative, so each dollar of new rate base is bought with equity priced by the same yields that sold the sector.

Which leaves the customer, the one constraint no mechanism smooths. American households already owe roughly $23bn in unpaid electric and gas balances, and a commission weighing that against a filing can simply take another year over it. Nobody on an earnings call has to call that a miss.
