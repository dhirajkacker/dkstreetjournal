Lincoln Electric's Record Welding Quarter Was Eight Points Price and Two Points Volume

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A welding consumable is bought by the hour, not by the project, which is why Lincoln Electric's June quarter was read as proof that American factory activity has turned. The decomposition is less comforting: of 10.1% organic growth, roughly 8 points were price and 2 were volume, and the price is metal passed through with a lag.

Volumes did inflect across all three product areas after nine quarters of compression, and Americas consumable volumes grew at a low-double-digit rate. But the Harris Products brazing slab put up 26.9% sales growth on volume down 8.2%, carried by silver and copper adders, and silver has halved since January.

Lincoln trades at 23.4x forward earnings against 26.0x trailing. ESAB, running the same consumables-plus-equipment model abroad, raised full-year profit guidance while its shares fell more than 40% in twelve months.

Lincoln Electric sold more welding wire, electrode and robotic cells in its June quarter than in any quarter of its history, and only about two of the ten points of organic growth came from shipping more of it. The rest was price. The Cleveland company, founded in 1895 and selling arc welding equipment and consumables through industrial distributors, reported net sales up 12.0% to a record $1,220m, with organic sales up 10.1% split roughly 8 points of price, 2 points of volume, 1.5 points from the Alloy Steel acquisition and 40 basis points of currency.

That quarter was taken as evidence that the American industrial cycle had turned under the price increases. It is better evidence of something narrower: Lincoln is paid in metal it passes through, and the pass-through is what laps.

The dollar is burned, not bought

Most of what Lincoln ships is consumed. On 2025 revenue the split was roughly $2.28bn of consumables against $1.08bn of equipment and $870m of automation, and Lincoln's North American brand share across the complete equipment-plus-consumables package is estimated near 40%. A stick electrode is reordered because a factory ran a shift; a robotic cell is a quoted project that can be deferred. Both sit in one revenue line, and they can move in opposite directions inside it.

In the June quarter they moved the same way. Americas Welding sales rose 11.2% to $774.4m with volume contributing 7.1 of those points, equipment organic sales accelerated to high-teens growth, and consumable volumes grew at a low-double-digit rate that management put in line with general industrial production. Automation sales were $229m on a record backlog. "Second quarter marked a solid inflection to volume growth in the business after nine quarters of compression," chief executive Steven Hedlund said on the July 30 call. One quarter earlier, pricing had contributed 10.4 points while volumes fell 2.6%.

The cost side explains where the price came from. US hot-rolled coil averaged about $1,213 a ton in September and the weekly mill benchmark rose for three consecutive weeks with 50% steel tariffs capping imports. Nucor has raised prices eight weeks running. Lincoln's own list prices follow, late.

Where the brazing adders go next

The clearest price-masked volume is the Harris Products Group, Lincoln's gas-equipment and brazing-alloy business, which is about a sixth of group sales. Harris revenue rose 26.9% to $201.9m with pricing contributing 34.2 percentage points, mechanical adders that pass silver and copper through, while volume fell 8.2% on weak HVAC and retail channels. Silver set a record of $121.62 an ounce on January 29 and traded at $61.56 on October 6. When the comparison laps, that contribution runs in reverse against volume already shrinking.

International Welding is the other soft leg: adjusted operating profit down 13% to $27m on volumes off about 5% in Europe, with the Middle East conflict costing the segment $6m-$7m of sales a quarter.

Then the capex leg arrived separately from the quarter. Shares fell 8.4% intraday on September 10 after chief financial officer Gabe Bruno told the Jefferies industrials conference that customers were delaying final investment decisions on major capital projects, that automotive production was down mid-single digits, and that Americas pricing actions only became effective in September.

What the results account for, and what they do not

Operating income grew 23.9% on revenue up 12.1% and margin reached 18.38% while gross margin slipped 40 basis points: the earnings came from leverage on volume, which is the part of the mix that is one quarter old. The group's reported growth stands on price that the metal market is now unwinding at Harris and only began flowing in the Americas last month. Lincoln is 9.5% below its August 27 record close and still up 11.4% over twelve months, so the market has marked down the price contribution without disputing the volume turn. At 23.4x forward earnings against 26.0x trailing, it is discounting barely a year of the 14.9% earnings growth consensus carries for 2026. September's ISM manufacturing reading of 54.5 with prices paid at 77.9 says factory hours are expanding and input costs faster.

ESAB, which sells the same consumables-and-equipment package through independent distributors with roughly two-thirds of revenue in consumables, shows the geography running the other way: second-quarter revenue up 12.9% to $807.6m but only 2.5% organic, with double-digit Middle East declines, and full-year adjusted profit guidance raised to $615m-$625m. "We expect to mitigate the transitory cost inflation related to logistics and commodity price increases over the next few quarters," chief executive Shyam Kambeyanda said on the August 6 call. The shares are down 43.5% over twelve months at 12.3x forward earnings, carrying total debt that went from $1.24bn to $2.42bn for the $1.45bn Eddyfi inspection deal.

Lincoln's third-quarter report comes before the open on October 27. It is the first quarter in which the price contribution starts to shrink on its own, and the first in which the wire has to move by itself.

Mid-America's Apartment Rent Recovery Is Real but Too Small to Outrun a 5.2% Treasury

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

An apartment landlord whose lease pricing has improved for three straight quarters has just lost a full turn of its cash-flow valuation in three weeks, and not one word of its guidance changed in between. That gap is the finding.

Mid-America Apartment Communities' blended lease-over-lease spreads went from -1.7% in the fourth quarter of 2025 to +0.7% in the second quarter of 2026, insurance renewed more than 12% cheaper, and expense guidance came down. Meanwhile the 10-year Treasury touched its highest level since April 2002, leaving MAA's $6.12 annualized dividend at a 5.26% yield against 5.23% risk-free.

The operating recovery is genuine at MAA and at Independence Realty, which cut concessions from 54% of new leases in April to about 28% in July. It is also far slower than the discount rate that re-priced both.

Mid-America Apartment Communities has spent three weeks losing a full turn of its valuation without changing a word of its guidance. The Sunbelt apartment owner, which held interests in roughly 102,772 units across 16 states and the District of Columbia, closed at $124.82 on 16 September and $116.31 on 9 October. Against full-year Core funds from operations guidance of $8.35 to $8.71 a share, unchanged through both prints, the midpoint of $8.53 puts the shares at 13.6 times that cash-flow measure, down from 14.6 times — arithmetic from the guidance and the closes, not a disclosed figure.

September's improving lease data was read as the start of an apartment recovery. What moved was the denominator. The Federal Reserve raised its target range a quarter point to 3.75%-4.00% on 16 September, its first increase in more than three years, and the 10-year Treasury touched 5.349% on 5 October, its highest since April 2002, easing to 5.23% by 9 October. MAA's quarterly dividend of $1.53 a share, $6.12 annualized, is a 5.26% yield on that close. The risk-free note pays essentially the same for none of the leverage, and September's 54-point rise in the 10-year was 49 points of real yields with almost no inflation component. The FTSE Nareit All Equity REITs index fell 5.7% that month.

The spread that turned three quarters running

The rent roll is genuinely better. MAA's blended lease-over-lease spreads ran -1.7% in the fourth quarter of 2025, -0.3% in the first quarter of 2026 and +0.7% in the second, composed of new leases still 5.3% below the expiring rent against renewals up 5.2%, on 95.3% occupancy. "We expect third quarter blended pricing to be better than the second quarter, a trend not seen in the last four years," chief operating officer Tim Argo told analysts on 29 July 2026. At Bank of America's global real estate conference on 16 September the company said third-quarter blended rates were running three to five percentage points better year on year, renewals near 4.8%, with new-lease pricing still negative and deliveries still pressing Austin, Phoenix and Nashville. The cost side helps too: insurance premiums renewed more than 12% lower, a third straight annual decline, and same-store expense growth guidance came down to about 1.75%.

None of it has reached the income statement.

Revenue grew 0.95% year on year in the second quarter while operating income fell 7.0%, and the annual operating margin has compressed from 32.1% in 2023 to 28.0% in 2025. Consensus has earnings per share falling to $3.66 this year and $3.14 next.

Independence Realty is withdrawing the free rent

The smaller case is cleaner because concessions are visible. Independence Realty Trust, a $3.46bn owner of apartment communities in non-gateway markets including Atlanta, Louisville, Memphis and Raleigh, improved blended rent growth from +0.7% in the first quarter to +1.3% in the second and +2.5% in July, and cut concession use from 54% of new leases in April to roughly 28% in July. Weeks of free rent never show up in asking rent; they show up in effective rent, which is what the owner banks. Its renovation program turned 600 units in the quarter at a 16.4% weighted average return for $279 of extra monthly rent against unrenovated comparables.

IRT trades at 1.03 times book against MAA's 2.48 times, yet its 15.4 times trailing enterprise value to EBITDA is indistinguishable from MAA's 15.2 times: no discount for class-B assets, no premium for the concession reversal. Its own shares are also partly an arbitrage now, with Centerspace holders voting on 10 November on a merger paying 3.800 IRT shares for each of theirs.

What the recovery can and cannot repair

The operating inflection explains none of the three-week de-rating and the discount rate explains most of it: guidance did not move, the 10-year did. What the lease data does establish is that the Sunbelt supply wave is passing through. Multifamily starts in buildings of five units or more fell to 344,000 annualized in August, down 22.5% in a month, and completions were 25.6% lower year on year in July at 329,000. That pipeline is contracted and cannot be undone, but it lands in 2027 and 2028, while the rate move landed in September.

The overhang in between is local and live. Austin ended the third quarter with 13.5% vacancy and half of conventional properties discounting to new residents; Phoenix carried 12% vacancy with 61% of listings offering concessions. That is why the split runs by geography rather than by asset quality: MAA fell 14.4% over the twelve months to 8 October and Camden Property Trust 7.0%, while Essex Property Trust, whose portfolio sits on the West Coast where little was permitted, rose 2.0% and is the only one of these apartment owners still in an uptrend.

Mid-America controls one of the two clocks running against it. Third-quarter results are due on 28 October, and they can only answer whether blended pricing kept climbing; the note that now pays what the dividend pays answers to nobody in Memphis.

Announced Transformer Plants Add a Third More Output by 2028, Short of Demand Up by Half

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A shortage is supposed to be killed by new factories, and the factories have been announced. The arithmetic says they do not finish the job: announced plants lift global large-power-transformer nameplate output by roughly 35% by 2028 against 2023, while demand scenarios run to 50% or more, and the gap closes only after 2028.

The two Korean makers building into that gap were marked down hard anyway. Hyosung Heavy and HD Hyundai Electric each broke about 11% in the two sessions to 8 October, while Hyosung's backlog sat at KRW17.5tn, up 63% year over year, and HD Hyundai raised its 2026 order target by 22.8%. Quoted lead times on large power transformers still run near 128 weeks.

Nothing in the reported numbers or the order books explains the fall, and consensus earnings for both are still rising. The likelier reading is a reset in what a 2028 delivery slot is worth after a bond selloff rather than supply catching up.

The cure for a transformer shortage is more transformer factories, and the industry has committed to a wave of them. Eaton, the power-management group that builds switchgear and utility distribution gear, is putting up a $340m transformer plant in South Carolina targeting 2027 production; Siemens Energy has a $150m plant in North Carolina. Add the announced nameplate together and global large-power-transformer output rises by roughly 35% by 2028 against 2023, against demand that several scenarios put at 50% or more, with the supply gap closing only after 2028 in the base case.

That matters because the shares of the companies building the capacity have lately been priced as though the cure had already landed. The de-rating across power equipment since the summer reads naturally as the shortage premium deflating as supply arrives. The order books and the quoted lead times tell a different story, and the two makers that fell hardest in October are the ones whose backlogs lengthened most.

Hyosung Heavy Industries and HD Hyundai Electric each broke about 11% in the two sessions to 8 October. Neither disclosed anything.

A 128-week quote is still a 128-week quote

Large power transformers that took two years or less before 2020 now average around 128 weeks, with power and substation units quoted at 160-plus weeks as of August 2026 and high-capacity hyperscaler units at four to five years. Medium-voltage switchgear runs roughly 52 to 80 weeks at the 15kV class and 78 to 104 weeks at 38kV on June 2026 procurement data, sold out through 2028 in many channels. The constraint still lands on the buyer: Bloomberg, citing Sightline Climate, estimates 30% to 50% of planned 2026 US data-center capacity could slip or be cancelled because the gear cannot be delivered on time.

Five years of work on the book, and a plant still to come

Hyosung Heavy, which builds power and cast-resin transformers, high-voltage switchgear and high-voltage direct-current transmission systems alongside a separate construction division, ended June with a KRW17.5tn backlog, up 63% year over year, which analysts put at roughly five years of work. It has signed KRW386.5bn, about $277m to $287m, of ultra-high-voltage transformer contracts with two unnamed US technology companies for AI data centers. The June quarter carried a 14.96% operating margin against 10.77% a year earlier, and group operating margin has gone from 4.08% in 2022 to 11.16% in fiscal 2025: the thinnest margin among these makers, and so the most geared to the premium.

HD Hyundai Electric, which makes power and distribution transformers, gas-insulated switchgear, motors and generators, held a backlog of $8.5bn at end-June, up 23% in six months, and raised its 2026 order target by 22.8% to $5.185bn. A second plant aimed particularly at North America is due for completion in April 2027, with main Korean facilities already running near 95% utilization. June-quarter revenue rose 26% to KRW1,141.8bn at a 25.14% operating margin.

The US-listed read agrees. Eaton's Electrical Americas backlog reached $15.2bn, up 33% year over year, on a rolling-twelve-month book-to-bill of 1.3x, and "accelerating orders and growing backlogs are clear proof points that our customer-focused end-to-end solutions are winning in the market," chief executive Paulo Ruiz said in the 31 July second-quarter release.

What fell, and what did not change

Hyosung Heavy dropped 11.2% and HD Hyundai Electric 11.3% in those two sessions, extending ninety-day falls of 14.7% and 28.8%; across twelve months Hyosung is still up 75.9% and HD Hyundai up 2.6%. No company announcement is discoverable behind the break, and earlier falls in the Korean power complex were attributed to profit-taking, weakening AI sentiment and rotation into large-cap semiconductors rather than impairment. Meanwhile the 30-year Treasury yield reached 5.69% on 8 October, its highest since 2002.

So the results account for none of the decline.

Consensus still has Hyosung revenue up 22.2% and earnings up 62.4% this year, and HD Hyundai earnings up 36.7%. At the 8 October closes that leaves Hyosung near 29.6x forward earnings, derived from a stale 33.0x snapshot, and HD Hyundai near 22.8x. Estimates rose while the shares fell, which is the signature of a reset in what a delivery slot years out is worth once the discount rate moves, not of a bookings turn in the shortage.

The plants being built now are the test of the whole premium, and they report late: a Korean second line in April 2027, Eaton's South Carolina output in 2027, Siemens Energy's North Carolina capacity behind that. Until one of them shortens a quote, the shortage belongs to the seller.

RELX has put the generative-artificial-intelligence layer of its legal business inside the renewal price. The London group, which sells research and decision tools to law firms, insurers, scientists and governments, disclosed in July that roughly 90% of the value of new LexisNexis sales now runs through Lexis+ with Protégé, its package for legal research, drafting and document analysis, and that about three-quarters of renewal value comes from the same product.

For a year the market treated LexisNexis and its rival Westlaw as the obvious casualties of machines that answer legal questions for nothing: RELX's American shares are down 24.4% over twelve months and Thomson Reuters' down 35.7%. The renewal line says the AI is the thing customers are paying more for. Legal was RELX's fastest-growing division in the first half of 2026, underlying revenue up 10% to £959m, a record for the division, with adjusted operating profit up 13%.

The uplift is a line on the invoice

The per-seat arithmetic shows where that comes from. A published cost comparison puts Westlaw Classic at $132.80 a month for a single state and $266.40 for all states plus federal, Lexis+ core research plans at $80 to $135 per user per month, and estimates all-in AI-layer seats on either platform at roughly $250 to $500 a seat a month.

The AI tier is not a feature folded into the old price; it is a multiple of it.

Chief executive Erik Engstrom told investors on the 23 July half-year call that the migration to Lexis+ with Protégé is "a necessary starting point for the future growth opportunity for the next decade or so," likening it to the industry's earlier move from print to electronic products.

Margins are not paying for the migration. RELX's group adjusted operating margin widened to 35.5% from 34.8% on underlying revenue growth of 7%, with management describing the cost of tokens as under 1% of the overall cost base and its ability to manage that cost for customers as a competitive advantage. Sterling reported growth was only 3%, because a strong pound took two points off the line: currency, with nothing visible in demand. Risk grew 8% underlying, scientific and medical publishing 6% and Exhibitions 6%, by the company's count.

Thomson Reuters charges for it too

The mirror image is in Toronto. Thomson Reuters, which sells workflow software and content to legal, tax and corporate-compliance professionals, grew second-quarter organic revenue 8%, with its three core professional segments up 10% organically and 83% of revenue, and raised its full-year outlook in August to about 8.0% total and organic growth, 9.5% to 10.0% for those three. Legal Professionals grew 10% organically with recurring revenue at 97% of the segment, led by Westlaw and CoCounsel; tax and accounting grew 8% organically on filing work that statute requires.

Its agentic CoCounsel Legal bundle became generally available in August, priced publicly only for firms up to ten attorneys, with 12% off for two-year and 18% off for three-year terms. The shares trade at 22.3x forward earnings against 26.1x trailing, the same forward multiple as Verisk, a peer in the same information group that grew revenue 4.4% last quarter.

In the last thirty days RELX gave back 3.2% and Thomson Reuters 2.0%, while FactSet fell 9.4%, S&P Global 11.7% and Verisk 10.2% — a drawdown that followed FactSet's 30 September guidance for fiscal 2027 adjusted earnings of $19.25 to $19.65, below consensus, on organic subscription growth cut to 5% to 6.5%. FactSet sells workstations to the investment industry; a slower budget there says little about a law firm's research seat.

Retention is the number nobody prints

The fundamentals account for the summer recovery and then some. Two businesses accelerated and widened margins through a de-rating of roughly a quarter to a third, and the one leg of the bear case that was checkable, that AI would be surrendered free to hold seats, reads the other way at both. Seat counts are not obviously shrinking either: legal staffing data has Am Law 100 headcount up 3% since January 2023 and the second hundred up nearly 8%.

And consensus has Thomson Reuters' 2026 revenue at $7.97bn, about 6.6% growth, still short of the roughly 8% management guided in August.

The pitch both companies are making is that a corpus of case law and citations becomes more valuable when a machine reads it, and that the customer will pay the difference. Two renewal seasons' worth of invoices now say they will. The third is being signed.

The group is being repriced as a bond substitute, which assumes the businesses inside it are interchangeable regulated pipes. One of the three examined here fits that description.

Why a 42% warm quarter barely registered

ONE Gas delivers to about 2.2 million customers in Oklahoma, Kansas and Texas over roughly 41,600 miles of distribution mains. It earns a regulated return on that steel; the gas itself passes through to customers at cost, which is why the commodity swings reported revenue while the rate order sets margin. Operating margin widened to 20.1% from 17.0%, on $16.4m of new rate revenue plus customer growth. The quarter ran 42% warmer than normal, an effect the company says was "mitigated by weather normalization mechanisms."

That makes capital spending the growth line and regulatory lag the brake.

ONE Gas expects average rate base of about $6.3bn in 2026 against capital investment of about $800m, with allowed returns on equity of 9.4% in Oklahoma and 9.5% in Kansas and a 9.8% partial settlement in Texas awaiting a Railroad Commission decision. The limit is the interval between spending and recovery. The company's June-quarter filing states that its rates "do not generally provide for a return on investment for amounts the company has deferred as regulatory assets": lag, described as a balance that earns nothing.

New Jersey Natural Gas put a number on that interval. Its 1 June filing with New Jersey's Board of Public Utilities seeks a $157.6m delivery increase on a $4,046.1m rate base at a requested 10.10% return on equity, covering roughly $950m spent since its last case concluded in 2024. It was lodged alongside cuts to the pass-through gas supply and conservation charges, so the net residential bill falls 8.9% from 1 October, even as residential gas bills nationally are forecast 8% to 14% higher this winter. Cheaper supply bought room for a bigger delivery charge.

The one whose quarter actually got worse

NiSource is classified with the gas distributors but runs an electric utility serving about half a million customers in northern Indiana alongside its Columbia gas operations, and it is the only one of the three where earnings deteriorated. Adjusted earnings of $0.16 a share missed the $0.23 consensus on storm damage and workforce-continuity costs, operating income fell 13.2%, and the shares dropped about 5% on the 5 August print. Its $28.6bn 2026-30 capital plan carries $7.6bn of data-center infrastructure behind 9% to 11% rate-base growth, funded with $400m to $600m of equity a year. At 2.04x book and 21.4x trailing earnings against 24.9x in May, it is the dearest of the three and the only one whose quarter argues for the markdown.

New Jersey Resources has the opposite shape. Energy Services, its unregulated wholesale gas marketing book, earned $84.5m of net financial earnings in nine months against $39.4m a year earlier on gas price volatility, and is guided at 21% to 23% of fiscal 2026 earnings. Consensus has earnings per share easing to $3.44 in fiscal 2027 from $3.58, and at 1.98x book its forward price-to-earnings ratio of 15.1x sits above the trailing 14.2x. A declining base is being priced.

Two of the three earned their markdown

At NiSource the results account for the de-rating; at New Jersey Resources the forward estimates do. At ONE Gas they account for none of it. A fully regulated distributor that raised guidance, grew adjusted earnings per share 52% in the quarter and landed three separate recovery outcomes now trades at 1.30x book and 15.7x trailing earnings, against the 20.2x it carried in May, with market value down to $4.58bn from $5.53bn. "Solid execution across the business and the continued strength of our growth strategy, supported by constructive jurisdictions," chief executive Sid McAnnally said of the quarter on 5 August. What did change is the cost of funding the plan: the diluted share count rose 4.5% year on year and trailing free cash flow is negative, so each dollar of new rate base is bought with equity priced by the same yields that sold the sector.

Which leaves the customer, the one constraint no mechanism smooths. American households already owe roughly $23bn in unpaid electric and gas balances, and a commission weighing that against a filing can simply take another year over it. Nobody on an earnings call has to call that a miss.
