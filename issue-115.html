Chip Foundries Are Raising Wafer Prices Again After Three Years of Erosion

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The price of a finished wafer has turned up at three contract chipmakers at once, and the market marked all three down inside a single week in November. GlobalFoundries posted its first year-on-year blended price increase since 2023, up 4.8%, alongside a record 31.4% non-IFRS gross margin; UMC's loading went from 85% to 92% on record shipments and its chief executive guided a roughly 10% blended price rise for 2027.

TSMC's October revenue of NT$586.89bn was up 59.7% from a year earlier, and advanced nodes are 79% of its wafer revenue. None of that describes the markdown: the sell-off was an index-level repricing of artificial-intelligence exposure that hit UMC four times as hard as TSMC, and it was mostly retraced within a week.

Three contract chipmakers spent the better part of three years watching what they charge for a finished wafer fall. In their September-quarter disclosures, all three said that direction has changed, and one of them has already told customers roughly what next year will cost.

The group was marked down hard in early November along with everything else attached to artificial-intelligence capital spending. A gauge of US semiconductor stocks plunged 12% on 5 November, its worst day since the onset of the pandemic in March 2020, on speculation that a Democratic sweep of Congress would bring tighter regulation of US data centers. That repricing treated the foundries as a proxy for AI demand. The filings record something else: loading, wafer prices and prepaid capacity reservations all moved up in the quarter, at the leading edge and at mature nodes alike.

The same wafer, three price lists

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, which manufactures, packages and tests chips to other companies' designs, is paid an annually negotiated price per leading-edge wafer plus separately constrained advanced-packaging capacity. On its 15 October call it put 7nm-and-below at 79% of wafer revenue, with 3nm alone at 33%. Quarterly revenue growth accelerated through the year, from 35.1% to 36.0% to 50.9%, and third-quarter gross margin reached 66.4% against 59.5% a year earlier. "The gap between demand and supply are still quite big, and that is why we put all our strengths to increase the capacity," chairman and chief executive C.C. Wei told investors that day. October revenue, filed on Form 6-K on 10 November, was NT$586.89bn, up 59.7% year on year. TrendForce reported in late October that the company is eyeing a roughly 20% price increase on its CoWoS packaging.

GlobalFoundries, the Malta, New York specialty foundry selling radio-frequency, power-management and embedded-memory processes, is paid partly for reserved capacity under long-term agreements with customer prepayments. Its blended selling prices rose 4.8% year on year, "the first quarter of year-over-year blended pricing improvement since 2023," chief financial officer Sam Franklin said on 4 November. Non-IFRS gross margin hit a record 31.4%, up 540 basis points from a year earlier, and gross profit grew 36% on 12.5% revenue growth. The reserved-capacity economics are expanding: about one third of roughly $4bn of 2027 net capital spending is to be funded by customer prepayments, and third-quarter free cash flow of $136m included $150m of prepayments and government grants. "We are absolutely booked out through 2027, increasingly booked out through 2028," chief executive Tim Breen said.

UMC, the Hsinchu mature-node merchant whose margin is set quarter by quarter by how full the fabs run, lifted utilization to 92% from 85% on record shipments of 1.2m 12-inch equivalent wafers, with gross margin at 34%. "We expect 2027 to mark a turning point in our blended ASP trajectory, moving from an environment of ASP erosion toward a meaningful positive uplift beginning in the first quarter," chief executive Jason Wang said on 28 October, guiding to a 10% increase. October sales were up 18.3% year on year.

What the six sessions actually priced

The decline was concentrated and uneven. Over the 30 days to 11 November, UMC fell 28.7% and GlobalFoundries 11.4%, against 7.0% for TSMC — dispersion that tracks how much AI narrative each name had accumulated rather than anything in its wafer economics. Two of the moves were company-specific and real: GlobalFoundries fell 8.6% on 4 November on the capital-spending step-up and a further equity issuance to the US government taking its stake to roughly 7%, and UMC's quarterly earnings per American depositary share of $0.206 came in under the $0.214 consensus. By 11 November all three had recovered 12% to 18% from their 5 November lows.

So the results account for almost none of the markdown and the valuation anchors are where the episode left a mark. GlobalFoundries trades at 11.4x trailing gross profit, against 20.4x in early May, while that gross profit grew 36%. TSMC's trailing earnings multiple is 23.8x, and consensus estimates on a currency-consistent basis put it near 21.5x this year's earnings, against the 33.7x recorded in May. UMC is the cheapest of the three on a capital-structure-neutral basis at 7.4x trailing enterprise value to earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, which is the right measure for a company whose own finance chief, Chitung Liu, named depreciation and raw-material cost as what will pressure reported gross margin for at least two years.

The pricing turn is not yet in anyone's reported numbers for a full year, and that is the open question. UMC said in late October it was at a "very late stage" of 2027 price negotiations with customers; GlobalFoundries said its prepayment discussions should largely close in the fourth quarter and into early 2027. The next wafer price list is being written now, and it will settle whether three years of erosion ended in the September quarter or merely paused.

CrowdStrike sells most of its new business through a pooled commitment called Falcon Flex: an enterprise signs up for a multi-year dollar amount, then draws modules against it later as it needs them, from endpoint and identity protection to cloud workload defense and log management. Earlier in this cycle the company told investors how large that committed pool had become. It no longer does.

The intuitive reading of a de-rating at a company that signs commitments before it recognizes revenue is that the commitments have stopped converting. The filings argue the reverse. Remaining performance obligations, the contracted revenue CrowdStrike has booked but not yet earned, reached $10.7bn at 31 July 2026 against $7.2bn a year earlier. Revenue over the same span grew 26%. About 46% of that balance, roughly $4.9bn, is due to be recognized within twelve months.

Four quarters of going faster, not slower

Quarterly revenue growth has accelerated in each of the last four periods, from 22.2% to 23.3%, then 25.6% and 25.8%, on revenue of $1.471bn in the July quarter. Annual recurring revenue reached $5.84bn, up 25%, with a record $332.8m of net new ARR added in the quarter, itself up 51%. The full-year outlook for net new ARR was raised by 630 basis points, to a $1.350bn-$1.359bn range implying about 34% growth. Gross margin widened to 74.6% from 73.5%.

"Q2 was the best quarter in CrowdStrike's history," founder and chief executive George Kurtz said in the 26 August 2026 release. "Delivering record Falcon Flex results, record net new ARR, and accelerating growth—the Falcon is soaring."

What a Flex customer's bill does after signing

The conversion ladder is disclosed as uplift rather than dollars. Moving a standard subscription onto Flex lifts that account's ending ARR by more than 40% on average; a first re-commitment adds a further 25%; accounts that have re-committed at least twice carry ARR 53% above where their first Flex began. By the company's September investor briefing about 630 accounts had re-committed, six times the prior year, with the first re-commitment typically arriving around eight months into a multi-year contract. More than 935 Flex accounts were added in the July quarter alone, more than the prior three quarters combined.

That consolidation pitch rests on a real position: third-party estimates put CrowdStrike at roughly 22.7% of endpoint protection, against Microsoft Defender's 15.6% and SentinelOne's 11.5%, with its strength concentrated in large cloud-first estates.

The numbers that stopped being published

Flex total deal value, the committed-dollars headline, was last quantified at more than $3.2bn in the April-2025 quarter, up more than sixfold. Its replacement is ending ARR from accounts that have adopted Flex, which passed $2.29bn at 31 July and grew 101%. That is a recognized measure, so the gap between dollars committed and dollars converted has left the public record. The five-or-more-module adoption rate, 67% as of 31 January 2025, is gone too; the disclosures now run 51% of subscription customers on six or more modules, 35% on seven and 26% on eight. A quarterly net retention percentage is no longer given, only the statement that gross and net retention both increased.

Cash is the one check that does not depend on management's framing, and it holds: free cash flow of $377m, 26% of revenue, with the full year guided to at least 30%. Stock compensation ran at about $377m in the same quarter, which is why GAAP net income was $5.3m and why sales and gross profit are the anchors that mean anything here.

A month of selling that came from outside the company

The worst session was 5 November, when the close fell 9.9% and Palantir, CrowdStrike and Okta all dropped about 10% as investors cut exposure to the data-center build-out after the Democratic congressional sweep. Three days earlier Deutsche Bank had upgraded the stock to Buy and raised its target to $300 from $240. On 29 October, Fortinet and ServiceNow both beat revenue estimates and were sold hard anyway. Over 30 days CrowdStrike's 12.9% decline was the third-smallest among the seven software and security names on this shelf, and the stock is still up 4.7% over three months.

So almost none of the fall traces to the business, and the compression has not undone much. At 57.8x trailing gross profit, CrowdStrike is dearer than the 48.4x it fetched in late July, and 51.9x forward. Palo Alto, which sells firewalls plus subscription services to large enterprises and governments, trades at 25.8x forward gross profit; Fortinet, whose FortiGate hardware business earned a 30.6% operating margin in the September quarter, at 14.4x; Zscaler, selling cloud-delivered secure access per user on 24.9% growth and a 76.7% gross margin, at 10.5x. CrowdStrike costs twice the nearest of them. The markdown repriced the growth; it did not find a flaw in the conversion.

What an owner is paying for, then, is a conversion engine whose best evidence is now a thickening order book and management's description of it. The next test of both arrives on 1 December, after the US close.

IDT Corporation closed its fiscal year on 31 July with the shrinking half of its business finally mattering less than the growing half. Group gross profit reached $496.8m, an increase of $50.6m, while the Traditional Communications segment, prepaid international calling and wholesale voice and still two-thirds of revenue, gave up about $6.8m of gross profit, a 4% decline per the annual report. The Newark, New Jersey company added roughly eight dollars of gross profit in its growth units for every dollar the legacy book lost.

That arithmetic is what the market bought on 29 September, the session after the print, when the stock gapped 12.7% to $76.20 on volume six times the prior day's. The quarter was read as proof that a melting telecom shell had become a payments and software group. It is proof of that. What it does not establish is the price: the shares have risen 59% in six months, and with trailing earnings per share up about 6% over that span, a derived split puts roughly 50 of those 59 points in multiple expansion, from near 16x trailing earnings in May to 24.0x now.

The terminal in the corner store is the whole engine

NRS sells point-of-sale terminals, card processing and in-store advertising to independent convenience, liquor, tobacco and gas-station retailers. It ended the year with 35,400 retailer locations, 2,700 more than a year earlier, and about 95% of its revenue recurring. Revenue rose 24% to $159.4m and segment gross profit was $146.6m on 12.3% of group revenue: the margin structure of a software business attached to a hardware socket.

Both axes of unit economics moved at once. Average monthly gross profit per retailer location rose 22% to $383 from $315 a year earlier, driven primarily by adoption of NRS Pay, the payment-processing product, with revenue per terminal up about 10%. Fourth-quarter merchant services rose 31% to $28.5m and segment adjusted EBITDA rose 47% to $14.0m at a 31% margin.

The advertising leg is the soft spot. It grew 49% to $10m in the fourth quarter but declined for the full year, because industry ad rates fell as streaming services launched ad-supported tiers and flooded digital inventory. Accenture closed its fiscal year on 1 October with the biggest order book in its history, and the term that matters inside it is not a growth rate but a pricing clause. The half of the book where price is agreed first

CoreWeave, which rents graphics-processor capacity to large enterprises and artificial-intelligence labs, told investors on 10 November that the price of its debt had come down. "At the end of Q3, our weighted average cost of debt across our entire business was 7.9%. Down 250 basis points year over year," chief financial officer Nitin Agrawal said. "Looking ahead, we will not use the unsecured debt market to fund our business at current yields. Nor do we need to." In the same week, banks including JPMorgan and MUFG were still working, after more than six months, to distribute $38bn of construction debt tied to Texas and Wisconsin data centers leased to Oracle, whose cost of default insurance hit a record.

That inversion matters because the whole case for owning the buyers of compute rather than the sellers rested on funding source: hyperscalers were said to pay for capacity out of operating cash flow, neoclouds to borrow against backlog owed by a handful of counterparties. Amazon's free cash flow turned negative for the first time since 2023 — a $42.4bn outflow for the twelve months to 30 September against a $14.8bn inflow a year earlier, with long-term debt doubling to $128.9bn — against roughly $220bn of 2026 cash capex and "meaningfully more" planned for 2027. Oracle's trailing free cash flow yield is -7.4%, more negative than Amazon's -1.6%. Everyone in this business borrows. What differs is the rate they are offered.

What a signed GPU-hour owes before it earns

CoreWeave's September quarter shows the full chain. Revenue was $3.661bn, up 168%, an acceleration from 112% in the June quarter, and operating income turned positive at $207m. The net loss still widened to $709m, because interest expense reached $846m — 23% of revenue and 4.1 times the quarter's operating income — on $46.9bn of total debt, with fourth-quarter interest guided to $1.05–1.15bn. Backlog reached about $133bn, up 138%, more than half of it on contracts where customer delivery is expected to begin by the end of 2026.

The charge against this model is that six-year depreciation on accelerators flatters the margin by assuming residual value that evaporates. CoreWeave's rebuttal is specific: 130,000 GPUs come off contract in 2027 and 170,000 in 2028, "all fully amortized," and Ampere and Hopper capacity has repriced 30–50% higher quarter on quarter at premiums to original contracts. At current pricing, management put 2027 renewals alone at roughly $4bn of incremental annualized revenue with no incremental capital. Jim Chanos, who is short the stock, counters that after stock compensation and on a ten-year asset life the business earns a 2–3% pre-tax return on invested capital. Concentration is the unsettled part: Microsoft was 45% of first-quarter revenue, and Meta committed in March to pay up to approximately $21bn through 2032.

Nebius borrows against prepayments, and costs four times as much

Nebius Group, the Amsterdam-based operator of an AI cloud platform plus data, education and autonomous-driving units, funds the same mechanism differently: it expects more than $9bn of customer prepayments in 2026, with 70% of June-quarter deals carrying prepayments covering 50–60% of the associated capital cost. Its GPU-backed debt is dated and collateralized: roughly $775m maturing 31 October 2030 at the secured overnight financing rate plus 2.5 percentage points, beside $5.0bn of convertibles. Its last reported quarter, to June, grew revenue 454% to $582m at a 77.1% gross margin and a -30.2% operating margin. Yet it is the dearest name here, at 14.9 times forward gross profit against CoreWeave's 3.9 — nearly four times the price on a smaller book, with third-quarter results due 12 November against reaffirmed full-year guidance of $3.0–3.4bn.

Cipher's tenants buy the chips

"We are not a neocloud," Cipher Mining chief executive Tyler Page said on 2 November. "Lenders and investors are placing a premium on predictable, contracted cash flows, and that is exactly what our model is designed to produce." The bitcoin miner turned data-center landlord owns no accelerators; its three contracted campuses carry 700 gross megawatts, $16.6bn of contracted revenue and about $864m of projected average annualized net operating income on a 17.3-year weighted-average lease term, against roughly $6.3bn of enterprise value. Its counterparties are investment-grade or credit-enhanced: Amazon Web Services leased about 300 gross megawatts at Black Pearl for 15 years, and the 168-megawatt Fluidstack agreement at Barber Lake is backstopped by Google. Project bonds priced 7.125%, then 6.125%, then 6.0% across three successive financings. The business itself is barely born: revenue fell 62.8% to $26.7m as mining is decommissioned, gross margin was -172%, and the quarter's $31.6m of GAAP income came from a $127m non-cash warrant revaluation. Rent starts hall by hall from the fourth quarter, and the 20-year Barber Lake tenant is disclosed only as "a leading AI lab".

The equity and the debt moved in opposite directions

All three were marked down hard before any of this reported: Cipher is off 45% over 30 days, Nebius 26% and CoreWeave 24%, with the trigger a delivery-schedule repricing rather than a demand cut — Microsoft's capex came in about $5bn under plan on 28 October because third-party capacity was late. CoreWeave then rose 24.7% on 11 November, its best session since April 2025.

The de-rating is multiple compression, not deteriorating disclosure. CoreWeave has fallen from 16.4 times trailing gross profit in early May to 9.4 in August to 5.1 now, while quarterly gross profit grew from $1.36bn to $2.48bn; at a $33.5bn market capitalization it is priced at about a quarter of its contracted book. The credit market, which has the first claim on those cash flows, repriced CoreWeave the other way. Spreads on ten-year hyperscaler debt have widened about half a percentage point this year against roughly a fifth of that for industrials with no AI exposure, and the widening was worst among the weakest borrowers — which this quarter did not mean the smallest ones.

Amazon told investors its owned and long-leased model carries roughly 35 percentage points of margin advantage over colocation and "neo" competitors, the clearest statement of why renting costs more than building. It signed a 15-year lease at Black Pearl anyway.

FactSet closed its fiscal year having sold more new subscription value than in any year of its history, and earned less money than the year before. That is the sharpest thing to come out of the September-quarter disclosures at the three listed investment-data companies, because FactSet is the only one of them whose revenue still turns on how many people log in.

The market treats FactSet, MSCI and Morningstar as one position: research franchises discounted on the prospect that a chatbot replaces the analyst's subscription. The quarters show three incompatible revenue meters underneath that single story. MSCI is paid a basis-point fee on other people's fund assets. Morningstar's fastest-growing business rates debt, and gets paid by issuance volume. The capital-spending boom that is supposed to kill the research seat is currently feeding both.

The line that records someone else's assets

MSCI's asset-based fee revenue grew 25% year on year to $246.6m in the September quarter, on a record $2.9tn of ETF assets linked to its equity indexes, up 31%, with $76bn of net inflows in three months. The unit price is falling as the base grows: the average fee on those ETFs was 2.41 basis points at quarter end, and the filing attributes the increase to higher average assets partly offset by lower average fees. The subscription side, the part an AI assistant might actually erode, grew its run rate 9%, with retention up 30 basis points to 95%.

Chairman and chief executive Henry Fernandez told investors on 20 October that agentic delivery widens the customer base: "Instead of having 100, we now have maybe tens of thousands of users in our client organization. We can have hundreds of thousands and eventually millions of users of our content." Against fiscal 2027 consensus earnings of $22.52 a share, the stock stands at 29.1x, from 25.8x in mid-May — the smallest re-rating of the three, because MSCI never fell as far, up 12.8% over 180 days.

Morningstar's accelerant is the debt behind the buildout

Morningstar Credit contributed $107.8m of September-quarter revenue, up 19.1% organically on robust issuance, now the group's third-largest business. PitchBook, the private-markets seat business, managed $175.6m and 4.0% organic growth with softness among corporate and venture-capital clients, and total license-based revenue grew 3.7% organically. Group revenue growth has decelerated through the year, 10.8% to 9.6% to 8.9%, while operating income rose 31% to $167.4m and the GAAP operating margin went from 20.7% to 24.9%.

The issuance behind the ratings line is market-wide: KBRA projects $220bn of US collateralized loan obligation issuance in 2026, a new high-water mark, with hyperscaler bond borrowing at record volumes. Chief executive Kunal Kapoor's shareholder letter counted AI-channel usage rather than seat losses: "cumulative tool calls through the PitchBook Data Premium Connector surpassing 13 million in October, up from 1 million in July". On fiscal 2027 consensus of $14.96, the shares are at 16.4x against 11.8x in mid-May, the widest recovery — on a consensus only three analysts contribute to.

The seat meter re-accelerated and the P&L did not

FactSet's fiscal 2026 organic annual subscription value grew 7% to $2.56bn, adding a record $168m. "We generated $86 million of organic ASV during the quarter, our largest quarter ever," chief financial officer Joshua Warren said on 30 September. More than a tenth of that came from new artificial-intelligence products, exceeding the whole of fiscal 2025's AI contribution.

Against that, full-year operating income fell 6.5% to $700.0m, GAAP operating margin dropped to 28.3% from 32.2%, and fiscal 2027 organic subscription growth is guided to 5–6.5%, below the 7% delivered. Management blames one-time infrastructure and cybersecurity spending plus the timing of fast growth, and reaffirmed adjusted earnings of $19.25 to $19.65 a share at its 10 November investor day, with adjusted operating margin of at least 36% by fiscal 2029. The competitive backdrop is a share-gain argument from a small base: one 2026 comparison puts Bloomberg Terminal at 33% of the market against FactSet's 4.5%, and FactSet was absent from the data providers named when OpenAI launched ChatGPT for Financial Services on 10 September, a day its shares fell 5.3%. On fiscal 2027 consensus of $19.67, the stock is at 16.8x, from roughly 12.1x on then-forward estimates in May.

Six sessions did most of it

Over the 30 sessions to 11 November, Morningstar rose 19.7%, MSCI 15.4% and FactSet 14.7% — each name's entire twelve-month gain, earned in a month. The trend readings on all three turned up between 22 and 28 October, which brackets two earnings beats and a violent rotation: over the 20 sessions through 2 November the equal-weight S&P 500 gained more than 4% while the Nasdaq 100 fell nearly 10%. FactSet reported nothing inside the window.

How much of this the quarters carry

For MSCI the results do the work, and the rotation compounds it: money moving into value and dividend funds lands in vehicles that pay its basis-point fee, so flows show up twice. Morningstar earns its move on cost discipline and an issuance cycle, with the honest caveat that its subscription meters grow in the low single digits and the margin, not the volume, produced the earnings. FactSet is the disagreement. Its seat meter accelerated while its profits shrank, and the multiple expanded faster than either, which leaves the thesis resting on a margin recovery that has not yet appeared in a reported quarter.

None of the three has recovered the valuations these franchises carried before the AI scare. The one that sells seats has the furthest to prove: that a record year of new subscriptions and a better year of earnings can be the same year.
