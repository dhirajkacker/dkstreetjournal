CoreWeave's Borrowing Got Cheaper While Banks Struggled to Sell Oracle Data-Center Debt

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

The premise under this autumn's markdown of rented-compute companies was that hyperscalers pay for data centers out of operating cash flow while the renters borrow against contracted backlog. Neither half survives the September quarter. Amazon's free cash flow was an outflow of $42.4bn over the twelve months to 30 September, with long-term debt doubled to $128.9bn, and Oracle's trailing free cash flow yield of -7.4% is the most negative of the five companies examined here; banks are still trying to place $38bn of construction debt on data centers leased to Oracle.

Meanwhile CoreWeave's weighted-average cost of debt fell to 7.9% and Cipher Mining's project bonds priced tighter across three successive deals. Lenders are sorting these borrowers by credit standing and delivery record, and the sorting does not follow size: Oracle is down 46.7% over twelve months, worse than CoreWeave.

CoreWeave, which rents graphics-processor capacity to large enterprises and artificial-intelligence labs, told investors on 10 November that the price of its debt had come down. "At the end of Q3, our weighted average cost of debt across our entire business was 7.9%. Down 250 basis points year over year," chief financial officer Nitin Agrawal said. "Looking ahead, we will not use the unsecured debt market to fund our business at current yields. Nor do we need to." In the same week, banks including JPMorgan and MUFG were still working, after more than six months, to distribute $38bn of construction debt tied to Texas and Wisconsin data centers leased to Oracle, whose cost of default insurance hit a record.

That inversion matters because the whole case for owning the buyers of compute rather than the sellers rested on funding source: hyperscalers were said to pay for capacity out of operating cash flow, neoclouds to borrow against backlog owed by a handful of counterparties. Amazon's free cash flow turned negative for the first time since 2023 — a $42.4bn outflow for the twelve months to 30 September against a $14.8bn inflow a year earlier, with long-term debt doubling to $128.9bn — against roughly $220bn of 2026 cash capex and "meaningfully more" planned for 2027. Oracle's trailing free cash flow yield is -7.4%, more negative than Amazon's -1.6%. Everyone in this business borrows. What differs is the rate they are offered.

What a signed GPU-hour owes before it earns

CoreWeave's September quarter shows the full chain. Revenue was $3.661bn, up 168%, an acceleration from 112% in the June quarter, and operating income turned positive at $207m. The net loss still widened to $709m, because interest expense reached $846m — 23% of revenue and 4.1 times the quarter's operating income — on $46.9bn of total debt, with fourth-quarter interest guided to $1.05–1.15bn. Backlog reached about $133bn, up 138%, more than half of it on contracts where customer delivery is expected to begin by the end of 2026.

The charge against this model is that six-year depreciation on accelerators flatters the margin by assuming residual value that evaporates. CoreWeave's rebuttal is specific: 130,000 GPUs come off contract in 2027 and 170,000 in 2028, "all fully amortized," and Ampere and Hopper capacity has repriced 30–50% higher quarter on quarter at premiums to original contracts. At current pricing, management put 2027 renewals alone at roughly $4bn of incremental annualized revenue with no incremental capital. Jim Chanos, who is short the stock, counters that after stock compensation and on a ten-year asset life the business earns a 2–3% pre-tax return on invested capital. Concentration is the unsettled part: Microsoft was 45% of first-quarter revenue, and Meta committed in March to pay up to approximately $21bn through 2032.

Nebius borrows against prepayments, and costs four times as much

Nebius Group, the Amsterdam-based operator of an AI cloud platform plus data, education and autonomous-driving units, funds the same mechanism differently: it expects more than $9bn of customer prepayments in 2026, with 70% of June-quarter deals carrying prepayments covering 50–60% of the associated capital cost. Its GPU-backed debt is dated and collateralized: roughly $775m maturing 31 October 2030 at the secured overnight financing rate plus 2.5 percentage points, beside $5.0bn of convertibles. Its last reported quarter, to June, grew revenue 454% to $582m at a 77.1% gross margin and a -30.2% operating margin. Yet it is the dearest name here, at 14.9 times forward gross profit against CoreWeave's 3.9 — nearly four times the price on a smaller book, with third-quarter results due 12 November against reaffirmed full-year guidance of $3.0–3.4bn.

Cipher's tenants buy the chips

"We are not a neocloud," Cipher Mining chief executive Tyler Page said on 2 November. "Lenders and investors are placing a premium on predictable, contracted cash flows, and that is exactly what our model is designed to produce." The bitcoin miner turned data-center landlord owns no accelerators; its three contracted campuses carry 700 gross megawatts, $16.6bn of contracted revenue and about $864m of projected average annualized net operating income on a 17.3-year weighted-average lease term, against roughly $6.3bn of enterprise value. Its counterparties are investment-grade or credit-enhanced: Amazon Web Services leased about 300 gross megawatts at Black Pearl for 15 years, and the 168-megawatt Fluidstack agreement at Barber Lake is backstopped by Google. Project bonds priced 7.125%, then 6.125%, then 6.0% across three successive financings. The business itself is barely born: revenue fell 62.8% to $26.7m as mining is decommissioned, gross margin was -172%, and the quarter's $31.6m of GAAP income came from a $127m non-cash warrant revaluation. Rent starts hall by hall from the fourth quarter, and the 20-year Barber Lake tenant is disclosed only as "a leading AI lab".

The equity and the debt moved in opposite directions

All three were marked down hard before any of this reported: Cipher is off 45% over 30 days, Nebius 26% and CoreWeave 24%, with the trigger a delivery-schedule repricing rather than a demand cut — Microsoft's capex came in about $5bn under plan on 28 October because third-party capacity was late. CoreWeave then rose 24.7% on 11 November, its best session since April 2025.

The de-rating is multiple compression, not deteriorating disclosure. CoreWeave has fallen from 16.4 times trailing gross profit in early May to 9.4 in August to 5.1 now, while quarterly gross profit grew from $1.36bn to $2.48bn; at a $33.5bn market capitalization it is priced at about a quarter of its contracted book. The credit market, which has the first claim on those cash flows, repriced CoreWeave the other way. Spreads on ten-year hyperscaler debt have widened about half a percentage point this year against roughly a fifth of that for industrials with no AI exposure, and the widening was worst among the weakest borrowers — which this quarter did not mean the smallest ones.

Amazon told investors its owned and long-leased model carries roughly 35 percentage points of margin advantage over colocation and "neo" competitors, the clearest statement of why renting costs more than building. It signed a 15-year lease at Black Pearl anyway.

ETF Flows Pay MSCI, Bond Issuance Pays Morningstar, and Seats Pay Only FactSet

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Three investment-data companies have been marked down together for a year on the belief that an AI assistant shrinks the research subscription. Their September-quarter disclosures show only one of them still bills that way, and it is the one whose profits went backwards.

MSCI's asset-based fees rose 25% to $246.6m on a record $2.9tn of exchange-traded fund assets tracking its indexes; Morningstar's fastest line is credit ratings, up 19.1% organically on heavy debt issuance, while PitchBook seats grew 4.0%. FactSet added a record $168m of organic subscription value in fiscal 2026 and still reported operating income down 6.5%, with fiscal 2027 subscription growth guided lower.

MSCI and Morningstar earn the rally on mechanisms the AI buildout feeds. FactSet is the one name where the meter and the margin disagree.

FactSet closed its fiscal year having sold more new subscription value than in any year of its history, and earned less money than the year before. That is the sharpest thing to come out of the September-quarter disclosures at the three listed investment-data companies, because FactSet is the only one of them whose revenue still turns on how many people log in.

The market treats FactSet, MSCI and Morningstar as one position: research franchises discounted on the prospect that a chatbot replaces the analyst's subscription. The quarters show three incompatible revenue meters underneath that single story. MSCI is paid a basis-point fee on other people's fund assets. Morningstar's fastest-growing business rates debt, and gets paid by issuance volume. The capital-spending boom that is supposed to kill the research seat is currently feeding both.

The line that records someone else's assets

MSCI's asset-based fee revenue grew 25% year on year to $246.6m in the September quarter, on a record $2.9tn of ETF assets linked to its equity indexes, up 31%, with $76bn of net inflows in three months. The unit price is falling as the base grows: the average fee on those ETFs was 2.41 basis points at quarter end, and the filing attributes the increase to higher average assets partly offset by lower average fees. The subscription side, the part an AI assistant might actually erode, grew its run rate 9%, with retention up 30 basis points to 95%.

Chairman and chief executive Henry Fernandez told investors on 20 October that agentic delivery widens the customer base: "Instead of having 100, we now have maybe tens of thousands of users in our client organization. We can have hundreds of thousands and eventually millions of users of our content." Against fiscal 2027 consensus earnings of $22.52 a share, the stock stands at 29.1x, from 25.8x in mid-May — the smallest re-rating of the three, because MSCI never fell as far, up 12.8% over 180 days.

Morningstar's accelerant is the debt behind the buildout

Morningstar Credit contributed $107.8m of September-quarter revenue, up 19.1% organically on robust issuance, now the group's third-largest business. PitchBook, the private-markets seat business, managed $175.6m and 4.0% organic growth with softness among corporate and venture-capital clients, and total license-based revenue grew 3.7% organically. Group revenue growth has decelerated through the year, 10.8% to 9.6% to 8.9%, while operating income rose 31% to $167.4m and the GAAP operating margin went from 20.7% to 24.9%.

The issuance behind the ratings line is market-wide: KBRA projects $220bn of US collateralized loan obligation issuance in 2026, a new high-water mark, with hyperscaler bond borrowing at record volumes. Chief executive Kunal Kapoor's shareholder letter counted AI-channel usage rather than seat losses: "cumulative tool calls through the PitchBook Data Premium Connector surpassing 13 million in October, up from 1 million in July". On fiscal 2027 consensus of $14.96, the shares are at 16.4x against 11.8x in mid-May, the widest recovery — on a consensus only three analysts contribute to.

The seat meter re-accelerated and the P&L did not

FactSet's fiscal 2026 organic annual subscription value grew 7% to $2.56bn, adding a record $168m. "We generated $86 million of organic ASV during the quarter, our largest quarter ever," chief financial officer Joshua Warren said on 30 September. More than a tenth of that came from new artificial-intelligence products, exceeding the whole of fiscal 2025's AI contribution.

Against that, full-year operating income fell 6.5% to $700.0m, GAAP operating margin dropped to 28.3% from 32.2%, and fiscal 2027 organic subscription growth is guided to 5–6.5%, below the 7% delivered. Management blames one-time infrastructure and cybersecurity spending plus the timing of fast growth, and reaffirmed adjusted earnings of $19.25 to $19.65 a share at its 10 November investor day, with adjusted operating margin of at least 36% by fiscal 2029. The competitive backdrop is a share-gain argument from a small base: one 2026 comparison puts Bloomberg Terminal at 33% of the market against FactSet's 4.5%, and FactSet was absent from the data providers named when OpenAI launched ChatGPT for Financial Services on 10 September, a day its shares fell 5.3%. On fiscal 2027 consensus of $19.67, the stock is at 16.8x, from roughly 12.1x on then-forward estimates in May.

Six sessions did most of it

Over the 30 sessions to 11 November, Morningstar rose 19.7%, MSCI 15.4% and FactSet 14.7% — each name's entire twelve-month gain, earned in a month. The trend readings on all three turned up between 22 and 28 October, which brackets two earnings beats and a violent rotation: over the 20 sessions through 2 November the equal-weight S&P 500 gained more than 4% while the Nasdaq 100 fell nearly 10%. FactSet reported nothing inside the window.

How much of this the quarters carry

For MSCI the results do the work, and the rotation compounds it: money moving into value and dividend funds lands in vehicles that pay its basis-point fee, so flows show up twice. Morningstar earns its move on cost discipline and an issuance cycle, with the honest caveat that its subscription meters grow in the low single digits and the margin, not the volume, produced the earnings. FactSet is the disagreement. Its seat meter accelerated while its profits shrank, and the multiple expanded faster than either, which leaves the thesis resting on a margin recovery that has not yet appeared in a reported quarter.

None of the three has recovered the valuations these franchises carried before the AI scare. The one that sells seats has the furthest to prove: that a record year of new subscriptions and a better year of earnings can be the same year.
