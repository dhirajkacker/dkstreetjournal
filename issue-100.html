The Over-the-Counter Slump Widened Haleon's Margin and Squeezed Perrigo's

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Fewer packets of pain relief and cold remedy are selling in the United States and Europe, yet both the advertised brand and the store label next to it gained share of the smaller pile. That should make the two listed ways of earning an over-the-counter dollar one trade. It did the opposite.

Haleon expanded constant-currency gross margin by 1.4 points in the first half of 2026 and grew adjusted earnings per share 12%, with second-quarter growth finally coming more from volume than price. Perrigo, which makes retailers' store brands, saw its all-in adjusted gross margin fall 2.5 points to 35.6% on core organic sales down 3.5%.

Haleon collects a brand premium it buys with advertising worth 20.9% of sales; Perrigo earns a contract-like margin set by plant utilization and shipment timing. Perrigo's shares moved on asset sales and debt, not demand.

Fewer units of painkillers, cold remedies and vitamins are moving through American and European drugstores than a year ago, and both the advertised brand and the store label beside it took a bigger slice of the smaller pile. Haleon, the Sensodyne-to-Panadol consumer healthcare company spun out of GSK, reported that Advil gained share in a declining market in the first half of 2026. Perrigo, which manufactures the store-brand versions of those same medicines for retail chains, added another percentage point of US store-brand volume share in the first quarter, after 1.2 points across 2025.

A single industry classification holds both companies, which invites the reading that they are one trade on a self-care recovery and on shoppers trading back up from private label. The two most recent halves say otherwise. Haleon's productivity work delivered 1.4 points of constant-currency gross margin expansion, against a medium-term target of half a point to eight-tenths a year. Perrigo's all-in adjusted gross margin fell 2.5 points to 35.6% in its June quarter. The divergence is a pay structure, not a forecast.

The quarter the retailer decides

Neither company's revenue line measures what households consume. It measures shipments into a concentrated set of grocery, pharmacy and mass chains, and those chains decide how much stock to hold. Perrigo's Self Care net sales of $577m fell 3.7% in the quarter ended 27 June, which the company attributed to softness in category consumption across the US and Europe and a slower start to summer seasonal categories, leading to a continued reduction of retail inventory levels, most notably in Europe.

The same mechanism shows up at a third seller: Reckitt's first-quarter disclosure recorded European net revenue down 4.2% like-for-like on a 4.5% volume decline, with seasonal over-the-counter products down double digit and retailers destocking at the end of the season.

Volume finally did the work at Haleon

Haleon's organic growth accelerated to 3.1% in the second quarter from 2.2% in the first, and the composition inverted: 1.1% price and 2.0% volume/mix, where the half as a whole was 2.1% price and 0.5% volume. "We delivered a good first half performance in what remains a challenging consumer environment, with sequential improvement in Q2 and a more balanced price and volume/mix," chief executive Brian McNamara said on 30 July.

The shelf is four demand curves, not one. Oral Health grew 7.3% organically in the half on Sensodyne and parodontax, while Respiratory Health declined low single digit; in the first quarter respiratory fell 3.4% on £499m of revenue against Oral Health up 8.3% on £932m, the weakness concentrated in a soft North American cold and flu season.

That premium is rented, and the rent is media. Haleon raised advertising and promotion spend 3.2% at constant currency to 20.9% of sales, and still delivered 8.2% constant-currency adjusted operating profit growth, 12.0% adjusted earnings-per-share growth and £769m of free cash flow, with £457m of a £500m buyback completed. Guidance of 3% to 5% organic growth was reaffirmed. On a sterling reporting base, management expects roughly a one-point translation headwind to full-year revenue and adjusted operating profit, which is most of the gap between its organic and reported lines.

The shares have not followed. Haleon closed 9 October at $9.32, some 17% below its twelve-month high of $11.27 and 7% above the low. At 22.2x forward earnings it is cheaper than Church & Dwight, the branded peer behind Zicam and vitafusion, at 26.0x on consensus revenue growth of 0.6%.

What moved Perrigo was a sale, not a season

Perrigo rose 44.7% in three months and is still down 33.1% over twelve. The turn is dated: second-quarter results on 5 August lifted the stock about 25% in one session. Core net sales in that quarter fell 3.1% and core organic sales 3.5%, and consensus now has full-year earnings per share at $2.20, down 21%, inside company guidance of $2.00 to $2.30 all-in.

What improved was the balance sheet and the plants. Perrigo sold its Dermacosmetics business for about $359m and applied the substantial majority to debt, leaving $400m of cash against $3.3bn of debt and net leverage at or slightly below four times adjusted EBITDA. Infant formula net sales reached $101m, up 23.1% on the timing of contract shipments and higher store-brand volume, with operating income about $16m better after a capacity reduction in Vermont and a favorable FDA audit. A self-help program is sized at $80m to $100m of savings. "We continued to execute our Three-S plan in the second quarter, strengthening areas of the business within our control, improving operational performance, streamlining our portfolio, and further reducing debt," interim chief executive Albert Manzone said on 5 August; he took the role on 8 June after his predecessor resigned over personal conduct the board found inconsistent with its code. Reviews of the Infant Formula and Oral Care businesses remain open.

The equity is priced accordingly: 6.85x forward earnings and 0.83x book, with the trailing multiple unusable after a $1.3bn goodwill write-off produced a reported loss for 2025.

Two ways to be paid on the same aisle

Haleon's half explains none of its share price, which sits near the bottom of its own year. Perrigo's share price explains none of its half: sales, gross margin and consensus earnings all went the wrong way while the stock rose by nearly half. The cause of the split is who owns the brand. Haleon keeps the premium as long as it keeps paying for the advertising, so category contraction reaches it as mix and productivity. Perrigo is paid a manufacturing margin by the retailer who owns the label, so the same contraction reaches it as destocking and idle plant, and what equity holders keep is decided by leverage and portfolio sales. A third large owner of over-the-counter brands offers no read on either: Kenvue's shares track the Kimberly-Clark acquisition approved by both shareholder bases on 29 January, not the aisle.

Trade-down has not reversed. Store brands are still taking units while branded share also rises, which is arithmetic only a shrinking category permits. Haleon's third-quarter trading statement is due 29 October and Perrigo reports on 4 November. Until the unit base stops falling, Haleon's extra margin has to come out of its own cost base and Perrigo's out of its debt.

Dominion Energy, the regulated utility that supplies Northern Virginia's data-center corridor along with electric and gas customers in the Carolinas, booked a $95m charge in the second quarter for offshore-wind construction costs it does not expect customers to pay. A charge like that moves reported earnings. Megawatts in a contracting pipeline do not.

That is the whole mechanism of a regulated utility dollar, and it runs against how these shares are held. A state commission sets a percentage return on the capital it has permitted the company to put in service; fuel and purchased power are passed through at cost. Load growth reaches the shareholder only once it becomes approved capital spending, and only after a rate case. Virginia has just priced that return, and the money went to a project that sits outside the usual recovery rules.

The commission sets the size of the earned dollar

The SCC found that Dominion Energy Virginia earned 7.77% on equity over the 2023-24 biennial period against a 9.70% authorization, leaving more than $350m of costs unrecovered. In its final order of 25 November 2025 the commission lifted the authorized return to 9.8%, against the 10.4% requested, and approved a base-rate increase of $565.7m for 2026 where the company had sought $822m. That is regulatory lag in arithmetic: a 193-basis-point shortfall between what the regulator allows and what the utility collected.

The company's $64.7bn 2026-2030 capital plan supports a projected Virginia rate-base growth rate of 11.9% a year, funded roughly 60% from internal cash with about a tenth each from hybrids and equity. Dominion's diluted share count rose 3.5% year on year to 882.1m in the quarter: the per-share price of that plan.

The band where customers stop paying

Under the cost-sharing settlement the Virginia commission approved in December 2022, disclosed in Dominion's annual report, the first $10.3bn of the 2.6 GW wind project is largely customer-funded, the next $1bn is split evenly, and everything from $11.3bn to $13.7bn is 100% shareholder-funded with no customer recovery at all. In July the company put the estimate at about $11.7bn, up $288m from April on network-upgrade costs, tariffs and turbine-installation revisions. Roughly $400m therefore sits in the zero-recovery band. The project is 81% complete with final turbine installation pushed to the end of 2027.

Second-quarter revenue rose 19.6% to $4.558bn while net income fell 55.3% to $340m, the gap being the charge rather than the operating business. Operating earnings came in at $0.79 a share and full-year guidance of $3.45-$3.69 was reaffirmed.

Pipeline, contract, collateral

Dominion reported 53 GW of data-center capacity in various stages of contracting, of which about 12 GW sits under signed electric service agreements, with more than 5 GW added since year-end. What converts the rest into an obligation is a tariff, not a queue: Virginia's new GS-5 class, effective 1 January 2027, applies to loads of 25 MW and up and requires a fixed 14-year contract, minimum demand charges covering 85% of contracted transmission and distribution capacity, and collateral of $1.5m per MW from customers failing credit tests.

One Dominion reactor escapes this machinery. Millstone sits outside the Virginia rate base and must be recontracted; the company bid three offers into Connecticut's solicitation for terms beginning October 2029, naming a well-capitalized counterparty such as a data-center operator as the way to underwrite relicensing. There, a power contract would land in revenue.

Why the shares fell, name by name

Dominion's price has not been its own since 18 May, when NextEra agreed to acquire it in an all-stock deal valued near $67bn at a fixed 0.8138 shares, unadjusted for market moves. At the 9 October close Dominion sat 2.1% under deal value, the same discount it carried a month and three months earlier, and its three-month decline of 12.1% matches NextEra's 12.0%. Virginia's commission has set public hearings and an evidentiary hearing from 17 November, with a decision expected in January, and Governor Abigail Spanberger has called herself deeply skeptical. All eight regulated names on this shelf fell over three months, a mean of 11.9%, as the utility sector sold off into a 10-year Treasury paying above 5.20% against a sector yield near 3.05%.

Entergy, which serves 3 million customers across Arkansas, Louisiana, Mississippi and Texas, shows the funding cost directly. Revenue rose 5.9% and net income 3.4%, yet diluted earnings slipped to $1.03 from $1.05 because the share count grew 4.6%; management widened the 2026 dilution drag to $0.20-$0.25 a share while raising outer-year guidance and targeting better than 8% earnings growth through 2030. Its Louisiana formula rate plan holds the allowed return at 9.7% within a 40-basis-point band, per its quarterly filing, and chief executive Drew Marsh told analysts on 29 July: "Yeah, I think that we have a long history of extending Formula Rate Plans within Louisiana. Our expectation is that we would probably be able to extend." The $12.9bn of generation for Meta's Hyperion campus went to an evidentiary hearing in early October with a December decision targeted; none of it earns until approved. At 23.4x forward against 26.0x trailing, Entergy is the dearest of these three on earnings, down from roughly 26x forward in May.

PG&E is the stress case. What the results account for, and what they do not

Which is why the number to watch in Virginia is not a gigawatt figure. Every further dollar spent on those turbines, all the way to $13.7bn, belongs to the shareholders.

Archer-Daniels-Midland, which buys oilseeds, corn and wheat and sells the oil, meal, sweeteners, starches and ethanol made from them, has raised its 2026 profit forecast twice this year, and the cause is a federal fuel rule rather than anything a food customer ordered. The agricultural complex is nonetheless traded as though crop prices were these companies' revenue. On 9 October a Department of Agriculture report raising corn yield and production forecasts plunged corn futures and sold the sector: Corteva fell 6.6%, Mosaic 6.2%, Bunge 4.7% and ADM 3.0%, on a day an S&P 500 tracking fund rose 0.55%. What a processor actually collects is the spread between the bean and the products made from it, hedged on the futures board weeks before the plant runs.

The quarter's profit was set before the plants ran

ADM's crushing business lost $79m at the operating line in the first quarter of 2026 against a $47m profit a year earlier. The quarter carried roughly $275m of net negative mark-to-market and timing charges, about 70% of it in crushing, booked because the commodity environment strengthened on clarity about US biofuels policy. One quarter later the same subsegment earned $363m, helped by about $100m of net positive timing — a $442m swing while revenue moved roughly 11% sequentially. Second-quarter group revenue of $22.68bn was up 7.0% year on year; operating income rose 796% to $896m.

Hedging is why the two quarters look unrelated. On the 4 August call Chief Executive Juan Luciano said North American crushing was about 90% covered for the third quarter and 30% for the fourth, with global coverage near 70% for the third.

Most of a quarter's margin is bought before the quarter exists.

Washington buys the oil

The EPA's Set 2 rule put 2026 and 2027 volumes at the highest levels in the program's history, with the agency estimating that biodiesel and renewable diesel output must rise more than 60% from 2025. Soybean oil is more than a third of the feedstock those US plants consume, and domestic renewable diesel capacity has gone from 791 million gallons a year in 2021 to about 4,580 million now. ADM's oilseeds segment profit rose 129% in the second quarter, which the company credited to demand created by the obligation and higher global energy prices, with crush volumes up nearly 5%.

The corn slab answers to different arithmetic. Carbohydrate Solutions, ADM's sweetener, starch and ethanol business, earned $411m, up 22%, with ethanol profit up 158% to $85m — and ADM credited lower US corn prices for giving ethanol an edge over competing blendstocks. The report that sold the shares on 9 October cheapens that input.

Bunge is the more processing-levered of the two

Bunge, which absorbed Viterra's grain handling and export network in July 2025, is commonly read as the diversified one. Its second quarter says otherwise: soybean and softseed processing delivered $700m of $796m of adjusted segment earnings before interest and tax, or 88%, against $67m from grain merchandising and milling. The Viterra cost-synergy target went to about $350m from $250m, adjusted leverage stood at 1.9x, and full-year guidance moved to $9.25 to $9.75. "Our team delivered another strong quarter, navigating a complex global environment with agility, focus and disciplined execution," Chief Executive Greg Heckman said on 29 July.

The shares have fallen 18.1% from their 10 September close, which is also the day UBS cut its target to $115 from $145 on valuation while keeping a buy rating. Bunge now trades at 10.9x forward earnings against 19.9x trailing, a wider gap than ADM's, and trailing free cash flow is negative 3.6% of market value on the working capital Viterra brought with it. What an October rulemaking decides

The results do not explain the fall; the fragility of their cause does. On the American Soybean Association's arithmetic, roughly 500 million gallons of biomass-based diesel demand hangs on it.

A spread this wide also calls out supply. 114 million bushels of new US crush capacity is slated for 2026 after 75 million in 2025, and ADM's own four-plant brownfield program adds about 700,000 tonnes of annual capacity between mid-2028 and early 2029.

Luciano called that program a "phased approach to allow for offramps." It is an odd way to describe building into the best margins the industry has seen, and an honest one: the demand was legislated, and what a rule gives it can withdraw at the next comment period.

Hecla Mining produced its silver last quarter for about $6 an ounce after credits for the zinc and lead that come out of the same rock, and the figure fell from the prior quarter because those credits got bigger — $6m bigger. The Coeur d'Alene, Idaho company's shares are down 18% over thirty sessions.

The drawdown runs across all four US-listed silver primaries, and it has been read as the hard-asset trade cracking at the cost line, where high-grade underground mines are supposed to be most exposed. First-half 2026 was the best half any of the four has reported: margins expanding at Hecla and First Majestic, raised guidance at First Majestic, record cash returns at Pan American Silver. What broke was the price of the metal and the cost of holding it. Silver slipped below $60 an ounce on 7 October, a first for 2026, and the 10-year Treasury yielded 5.24% after the Federal Reserve raised rates a quarter point on 16 September in a unanimous vote.

The zinc market sets the silver cost

Hecla does not sell silver. Greens Creek in Alaska and Lucky Friday in Idaho ship polymetallic concentrates to custom smelters, metal traders and third-party processors, and the company reports cash cost and all-in sustaining cost per silver ounce net of by-product credits. The price of zinc is therefore an input to the disclosed price of an ounce of silver. Zinc was $3,805 a tonne on 9 October, recovering from a six-week low as Western smelter capacity shrinks.

The wedge the smelter takes has inverted. Treatment charges on imported zinc concentrate have slumped to minus $50 a tonne, a record low in Fastmarkets data going back a decade, meaning smelters pay miners to secure feed; the 2026 benchmark Korea Zinc agreed with Teck was $85 a tonne, so the benchmark now understates how favorable concentrate terms have become. The $6.07 also excludes Keno Hill in the Yukon, which has not been determined to be in commercial production yet produced roughly 600,000 ounces and nearly $15m of free cash flow in the quarter, its fifth consecutive positive one.

Hecla's revenue fell on price while ounces rose

The metal lines show where the damage came from. Hecla's silver revenue dropped from $295.6m in the first quarter to $213.7m in the second, while zinc and lead together rose to $61.8m. Gross margin reached 64.9% against 39.3% a year earlier, and the company ended the quarter with $483m of cash and about $472m net of debt, having closed the sale of the Casa Berardi gold mine to Orezone in March. "Hecla enters the third quarter of 2026 from a position of real strength," chief executive Robert Krcmarov said in the August results release.

Pan American is half a gold miner

Pan American, which mines in Canada, Mexico, Peru, Argentina and Bolivia, delivered attributable silver of 6.47 million ounces at the top of guidance and attributable gold of 165.9 thousand ounces below it, on shortfalls at Jacobina, Shahuindo, Timmins and El Peñón. What the half-year cannot explain

None of it accounts for the fall.

Three of the four were in uptrends in the first days of October and all four crossed into downtrends within seven sessions, Pan American on the 2nd and Hecla on the 9th, then all four rose between 2.6% and 3.7% on 9 October as the metal rebounded. The credits that make a $6 ounce possible are, in the end, a position in zinc smelting overcapacity. If treatment charges climb back toward the benchmark while the metal stays where it is, the cheapest ounce in the group gets dearer with nothing changing underground.

Heartland Express hauled freight at roughly break-even cost in the June quarter and still reported $10.6m of net income. The difference was the second-hand market for its own trucks: gains on the sale of revenue equipment ran at 13.6% of revenue against a 2015-25 average of 4.1%, and the trade publication FreightWaves calculated that without the outsized gains the carrier's operating ratio would have been closer to 103% than the 91.0% it reported.

The same question sits over the whole truckload shelf. These shares spent the summer rising on the argument that capacity is finally leaving American trucking and that pricing is turning with it; between 23 September and 7 October all four crossed into sustained downtrends, down 25% to 31% over three months, after diesel reached a record $6.31 a gallon in mid-September and J.B. Hunt pre-announced a fuel hit of more than $10m. The June-quarter filings say the pricing argument holds at Werner Enterprises and is close to irrelevant at Heartland and Covenant Logistics, where the quarter turned on what a three-year-old tractor fetches.

Werner is paid by the truck-week, and the truck-week went up

Werner, the Omaha carrier running dry van, temperature-controlled and regional truckload fleets across the US and Mexico plus a brokerage arm, no longer sells much freight at spot. More than 70% of its truckload fleet sits under a dedicated agreement, fixed fleets parked at one shipper's door, about 7,400 trucks; the one-way fleet shrank from nearly 3,300 tractors in 2022 to under 2,400 at the end of 2025.

So the meter that matters is revenue per truck per week, and in the June quarter it rose 5.4% year over year net of fuel surcharge. The smaller one-way book did better still: revenue per total mile up 10.4%, miles per tractor per week up 15.7%. Werner raised dedicated pricing guidance from flat-to-up-3% to up 3-5% and lifted net capital spending guidance to $215m-$250m. "This tightness is being driven by intensifying regulatory pressure," chairman and chief executive Derek Leathers said on the July 28 earnings call, putting the industry in the "third inning" of capacity removal.

The reported line looks like the opposite. Operating income fell 74.5% to $16.9m, because the prior-year quarter carried a $45.7m liability reversal inside insurance and claims after a favorable decision on a 2018 adverse jury verdict; the company's 8-K attributes the decline to $53.6m of favorable reversals in total, including a contingent-consideration settlement. Adjusted operating income rose 67% to $27.6m. Revenue growth of 24% to $933.9m is partly bought: the January purchase of dedicated carrier First Fleet, reported as FirstFleet, added 2,110 trucks to the dedicated average fleet. At $32.70 the shares trade at 9.9x trailing EV/EBITDA, with consensus 2027 earnings of $2.24 implying 14.6x against 29.0x on this year's trough $1.13.

Heartland's quarter arrived on the resale lot

Heartland, a short-haul dry van and temperature-controlled carrier running the Heartland, Millis, Smith Transport and CFI brands on average hauls under 400 miles, is the one pure one-way book here, and the one with no quarterly call and no published pricing or utilisation metrics. What is disclosed: revenue of $184.1m, an operating ratio improved 1,490 basis points, average tractor age down to 2.3 years from 2.6, and $25m of acquisition debt repaid in the first half, taking acquisition debt and finance leases to $135m from $494m in 2022.

The fleet is funding the balance sheet. Forward earnings of $0.147 put the stock at 75.8x, trailing free cash flow is negative at 10.0% of market value, and only book value, at 1.14x, is cheap.

Covenant's profit came through a 49% leasing stake

Covenant, the Chattanooga group reporting in Expedited, Dedicated, Managed Freight and Warehousing segments, earned $8.8m of operating income on $332.9m of revenue, and says the largest drag was Managed Freight gross margin while higher earnings from its leasing investment offset it. That investment, a 49% stake in tractor and trailer lessor Transport Enterprise Leasing, contributed $5.3m pre-tax, or $0.16 a share, up from $4.3m, on higher equipment sale gains. Covenant is reducing its spot exposure too, moving about 15% of the Expedited fleet onto multi-year committed contracts as expedited freight revenue fell 11.4% and dedicated rose 4.3%. Net debt of $289.7m at roughly 2.2x adjusted leverage is the constraint; at 8.3x trailing EV/EBITDA the shares are the cheapest of the four on that measure.

Marten Transport, the refrigerated carrier with $104.0m of cash and no comparable leasing income, made $5.3m on $223.5m of revenue and watched its dedicated operating ratio deteriorate to 96.4% from 92.4%. At 46.6x forward earnings it is the weakest earner on the shelf.

Capacity is leaving on fuel and enforcement

The supply story the group was bought for is real, and freight demand is not what is driving it. September Class 8 net orders came in at 18,700 units, down 9.5%, which ACT Research tied to incomplete 2027 orderboards and uncertainty over the Environmental Protection Agency's 2027 emissions rule. Federal regulators restricted non-domiciled commercial licences from 16 March and made an English-proficiency failure an out-of-service violation. Dry van spot rates including fuel recovered to $3.53 a mile in early October, more than 10% above the late-August low, though much of the year-on-year gain is diesel itself; tender rejections eased to 13.78% on 1 October from 14.6% on 19 September.

The three-month drawdown is therefore a cost shock priced across the complex, with the rails off about 2% over thirty days against the truckload names' 9% to 15%, and the carrier results do not all point the same way underneath it. Werner's contract book repriced upward and its claims accrual improved against an industry where premiums reached about 10.6 cents a mile in 2025, while consensus still assumes a margin recovery the share price has stopped paying for. Heartland and Covenant depend instead on strong used-equipment values, which is the same tightness counted a second way: the enforcement pushing drivers out of service eventually puts their trucks on the lot.

Werner reports on 27 October and Covenant on 29 October. The number that decides the first is dollars per truck per week; at the second, it is how much of the profit again arrives from a business that owns trucks rather than drives them.

Meta launched Muse, an autonomous agent app that shops and transacts for its user, on 8 September, and it reached the top of Apple's US free-app chart within ten days. Two weeks later EverQuote, which runs an auction selling insurance shoppers to carriers, fell 14.3% in the session of 22 September and another 7.1% the next day. Cars.com slid through the same week. Neither company disclosed anything.

What is being tested is a belief about who pays. If an agent acting for a consumer walks past the page where the advertisement is served, the marketplace's revenue leaves with the traffic. Each of these three, though, is paid by a professional out of a professional's budget: Zillow takes a share of a real-estate agent's commission on a closed sale, Cars.com bills a dealership a monthly subscription, EverQuote bills an auto carrier's marketing line. In the June quarter all three raised that price.

Zillow's cut per closing rose as its visitors left

Zillow Group, the digital real-estate marketplace that sells agent connections, rental listings and home loans, collected 8.8 basis points of the $2.3trn of home-sale value transacted on a trailing-twelve-month basis, against 8.2 basis points a year earlier, according to its second-quarter shareholder letter. Visits fell 2% to 2.5bn in the same quarter while For Sale revenue rose 14% to $549m. The lever is the renamed Preferred programme, under which agents pay a success fee out of commission instead of a per-lead price: Preferred was 61% of connections against 27% a year earlier, earning 23% more revenue per connection in 2025, a premium the company expects to widen to 35% by the end of 2026.

"Zillow is the operating system for modern real estate," chief executive Jeremy Wacksman said with the 5 August results. Rentals, priced per property listed to a different customer entirely, grew 31% to $209m on a record 79,000 multifamily properties; mortgages rose 75% to $84m on purchase originations up 95% to $2.2bn. None of it has reached profit: gross margin slipped to 72.8% from 74.7%, and the quarter carried a $10m operating loss on $772m of revenue. The shares trade at 2.55x trailing sales, against roughly 7x a year ago on the same basis.

Zillow's pool is also shrinking. The thirty-year mortgage pushed past 7.5% in the week to 7 October, with existing-home sales at a 3.98m annual rate, 1.2% below a year earlier. And the policy governing what inventory it may show cost it feed: on 16 September a federal judge dissolved the order preserving Zillow's access to Midwest Real Estate Data's Chicago-area listings after roughly 43,000 were cut off. The stock closed down 7.4% on 23 September.

At Cars.com the shrinking line is the carmakers' ad budget

Cars.com held 19,343 dealer customers against 19,412 a year earlier while average revenue per dealer rose 3% to $2,500. Marketplace revenue grew more than 7%, its fastest since 2021. "Marketplace subscribers rebounded to their highest level since 2023, and marketplace ARPD reached an all-time high," chief executive Tobias Hartmann said on the 6 August call. Total revenue still grew only 0.7%, because the one line sold to carmakers and national advertisers fell 18% to $13.6m.

Operating income rose 83% to $27.9m on an 11% workforce cut, and the diluted share count is down 12% year over year. At 6.7x trailing EBITDA with a 27.6% free-cash-flow yield it is the cheapest of the three on cash, but consensus has revenue growing 0.8% this year, and the software attach is going backwards: AccuTrade subscriptions and Dealer Inspire website customers each fell about 3%. CarGurus, competing for the same dealer budget, grew paying dealers 7% to 34,409.

EverQuote's demand is set inside carriers' loss ratios

EverQuote grew revenue 24.6% to $195.1m at a 29.2% variable marketing margin, auto carriers supplying $172.1m of it. The cause sits in underwriting: the US personal-auto loss ratio improved to 64% at the end of 2025 from 86% in late 2022, and repaired carriers buy growth. Progressive alone spent $1.4bn on advertising in the June quarter, up 16%. Carriers show a "higher willingness to pay this year than there has been in recent years," chief executive Jayme Mendal told investors on the Q2 call. The third-quarter guide of $198m-$208m implies growth slowing to roughly 14-20%, and the shares sit at 9.9x forward earnings.

The June quarters do not account for what happened in the thirty days to 9 October, when Zillow fell 9.5%, Cars.com 12.1% and EverQuote 15.5%. Two causes are named rather than inferred. Zillow's twelve-month 59% decline is the one with a business mechanism attached: it is paid per closing, closings are set by a 7.5% mortgage, and its own listing standards now cost it inventory. Cars.com and EverQuote, paid by contract and by auction regardless of how many houses change hands, held within 10% over twelve months and gave up their gains in two weeks on the question of whether a consumer will still arrive at all.

EverQuote is not waiting for the market to decide. On 8 October it published a paper titled "Agentic Barbarians at the Carriers' Gates," in which Mendal wrote that agentic artificial intelligence "has the potential to shift insurance distribution in the U.S." and change how often consumers shop. A company whose revenue is the price of a shopper has an interest in that shopping continuing to happen somewhere it can charge for it.

AutoNation's finance chief spent part of 17 September explaining model-year changeover to investors at Morgan Stanley's Laguna conference. Gross profit on each new vehicle would land about 10% below the second quarter's, he said, with parts-and-service growth moderating as customers defer elective work and finance income softer; the company was "having to meet the customer where they are and give a little bit more on price." The next morning every listed franchised car dealer in America opened lower, and AutoNation closed below its prior 52-week low.

The warning was read as a verdict on car demand. What it described was the condition of three profit lines that have quietly become the business: the finance-and-insurance desk, the service drive, and the loan book the dealer now funds itself.

The new car is the small line

Lithia Motors, which runs 467 stores selling new and used vehicles plus financing, warranties, repair and parts under the Lithia and Driveway brands, shows the arithmetic plainly. In its June quarter, new-vehicle sales produced $4,829.2m of revenue and $284.0m of gross profit, a margin of 5.9%. The finance desk booked $366.4m of revenue, and because commissions on loans, warranties and insurance contracts carry essentially no cost of goods, all $366.4m of it was gross profit. Parts and service produced $633.0m, 2.2 times the new-vehicle line.

That shape holds across the industry, and it is moving the wrong way at the front. New-vehicle gross profit per unit at the public groups fell 15.8% year over year to $2,764 in the second quarter, while used slipped only 2.0%. Finance and insurance has been the defended dollar, at $2,569 per vehicle retailed, more than 40% above 2019's $1,798. Service is no longer outrunning inflation: same-store fixed-operations gross profit grew 2.3% against June consumer prices up 3.5%.

Inventory is not the culprit. Cox Automotive put new-vehicle days' supply at 76 in late September, down 1% from a year earlier, with incentives at 6.5% of average transaction price and the full-year sales forecast raised to 16.1 million units.

Three weeks aimed at the back end

Two days before AutoNation spoke, the Federal Trade Commission published price-transparency FAQs for auto dealers: the advertised price must be what any consumer can walk in and pay, excluding only charges government requires, so a $40,000 vehicle carrying a dealer document fee of $85 must be advertised at $40,085. The agency had already sent warning letters to 97 dealership groups in March.

On 16 September the Federal Open Market Committee raised its target range a quarter point to 3.75%-4.00%, the first increase since 2023. Floor-plan borrowing, the credit line that carries unsold inventory, reprices with it: Lithia's floor-plan interest expense rose $14.7m in the June quarter, while Penske's same-store equivalent fell 9.8%.

Then on 5 October Morgan Stanley's Daniela Haigian cut AutoNation to Equal-weight with a $175 target, from $250, and Group 1 Automotive to Underweight, trimming implied multiples across the franchise dealers by 1.2 turns. Her argument was the back end again: fee disclosure plus AI shopping agents that make cross-retailer price comparison frictionless, with Carvana scored as the best-positioned name in auto retail after a record quarter of roughly 197,000 retail units.

From 9 September to 9 October the six franchise groups fell an equal-weighted 19%, AutoNation worst at 24% and Penske least at 10%. Four of the six now sit in established downtrends; only Lithia and Penske still hold mild uptrends.

One guide-down, one record quarter

AutoNation fell on its own numbers. Revenue has declined year over year for three straight quarters, operating margin has gone from 7.50% in 2022 to 4.74% in 2025, and 2025 net income fell 6.2% while earnings per share rose, because diluted shares have dropped from 75.0m in 2021 to 33.8m. Its captive lender, at a $2.7bn portfolio, earned $20.1m in the first half against a $39.6m provision for credit losses. At 7.4x forward earnings against 7.1x trailing, the multiple prices a flat-to-down year.

Lithia's quarter went the other way. Revenue rose 2.2% to $9.8bn, new-vehicle gross per unit held at $2,718 for a third consecutive quarter, used retail gross per unit gained $339 sequentially, and Driveway Finance originated a record $884m. "Financing Operations delivered 80% income growth, with expanding margins and increasing penetration," chief executive Bryan DeBoer told investors on 29 July. The cost of that engine sits two filings down: in the March quarter, $21.0m of the credit provision was net charge-offs against $5.4m from portfolio growth, and funding the loan book leaves trailing free cash flow at minus 7.9% of market value even on a 9.6x trailing multiple. Forward, Lithia trades at 8.1x.

Penske held up because a quarter of it is not a car dealership. Premium brands were 71% of retail automotive franchised revenue in the first half, and equity earnings of $57.4m from Penske Transportation Solutions exceeded the entire commercial-truck segment's $47.2m of pretax income and equalled 22% of group net income. At 14.7x forward against 14.4x trailing, Penske carries the highest multiple of the three and the flattest consensus earnings path.

What the quarters have to settle

The selloff is a repricing of the fee, finance and service dollar, and only one of the three protagonists has reported deterioration in it. AutoNation guided its own front end down and has the contracting revenue line to match. Lithia's per-unit economics stopped falling two quarters ago, which leaves its 20% monthly decline resting on what a regulator's guidance and a downgrade say about lines that are still earning. Penske's defense is brand mix and a freight stake, neither of which a car buyer explains.

Lithia reports on 21 October, AutoNation on 22 October and Penske on 28 October. Three figures in those releases do the work the revenue line cannot: gross profit per unit by line, the captive lenders' charge-offs, and what the service bays grew in a quarter when customers are being told to defer.

A dealer can live with a thin margin on the metal. What it has never had to price is a customer who sees the whole number before walking through the door.

A supplier whose distributors are down to 25 days of stock, with orders running above shipments, has spent the last three months being priced as though the destocking were still ahead of it. Microchip's reported numbers say it is behind: revenue growth has accelerated four quarters in a row, to 38% year on year in the June quarter, and gross margin has expanded five quarters running, from 53.6% to 63.2%.

The margin recovery is mechanical. Idle plant is charged to cost of goods, and the under-utilisation charge is shrinking about $8m a quarter as the factories load. What the results do not explain is the share price, which fell with every other power-management and analog name in the week to 9 October on no company news.

Microchip Technology, which sells microcontrollers and analog parts to automotive, industrial and computing customers through authorized distributors, told investors in August that the correction in its sales channel was finished. Distributors held 25 days of stock, the lower end of the company's historical range and one day fewer than in March, with bookings well above shipments and sell-through rising. Management went further in the same release, saying channel inventory is now low and needs replenishing in coming quarters.

The shares have behaved as though none of that had been said.

Microchip sits 14.7% below its July level and spent the five weeks to 7 October in a sustained downtrend, easing only on 8 October. The belief embedded in that decline is that an inventory correction is still to come and has yet to reach reported revenue. The company's own figures test it: revenue growth went from a 2.0% year-on-year decline in September 2025 to 38.0% growth in the June quarter, on sales of $1.485bn.

The $38.5m of idle plant sitting inside the margin

An analog maker that owns its fabs reports under-loaded capacity inside cost of goods sold, so the margin recovers as the plant fills. June-quarter non-GAAP gross margin of 63.8% still carried $38.5m of capacity under-utilisation charges, worth about 260 basis points, down roughly $8m from the prior quarter and guided down by a similar amount again. Microchip also has around $450m of equipment in storage; the company says its internal fabs are not the constraint on growth, pointing instead to outside foundries, assembly-and-test subcontractors and its own production throughput.

That arithmetic is why gross profit grew 62.8% and operating income roughly tenfold on 38% more revenue. "Improving demand, continued inventory normalization, and stronger factory utilization contributed to better operational leverage," chief executive Steve Sanghi said in the 6 August release.

Priced under both comparisons on gross profit

Trailing earnings are useless here. Revenue fell 42% in a single year, from $7.63bn in fiscal 2024 to $4.40bn in fiscal 2025, so the 104.7x trailing price-to-earnings ratio is struck on trough profits. On gross profit, the line that moves with fab loading, Microchip trades at 10.65x forward against 20.25x for Texas Instruments and 29.72x for Monolithic Power. Its trailing figure has fallen to 13.31x from 14.13x on 22 August, a stretch in which gross profit grew 62.8%.

The two comparisons are earned differently. Texas Instruments is paid for owning its plant, and said trailing cash flow from operations "underscored the strength of the business model, quality of the product portfolio and benefit of 300mm production" — free cash flow of $6.5bn against $1.8bn a year earlier, about a quarter of it $1.6bn of federal chip incentives, with 2026 depreciation guided to $2.2–2.4bn; it trades at 33.3x forward earnings after its trailing multiple compressed from 49.6x in May while the stock rose 32.1% over six months. Monolithic Power, at 50.5x forward earnings, is paid for data-center sockets: enterprise-data revenue of $380.6m was 38.8% of the June quarter and up 164% year on year.

The yield shock all six of them shared

Nothing in Microchip's reported results accounts for its de-rating. In the week to 9 October all six power-analog names fell together, by 2.6% to 13.9%, with no company-specific news at any of the three examined here, while the semiconductor index was down as much as 29% from its June peak on crude above $84 a barrel and Treasury yields near multi-decade highs — the long-yield pressure this page has already described. The likelier reading is a repricing of long-duration earnings rather than a demand event, against industry sales that hit a record $120.6bn in May.

The open question is mix. Microchip projects data-center revenue up about 69%, to roughly $1bn in calendar 2026, on memory and interconnect controller content; that is real and still small beside a company guiding toward about $6.4bn of annual revenue, with industrial and automotive customers dominating the consolidated statement. A rate shock does not empty a sales channel. Weak factory and car demand would.

September-quarter guidance of 7% to 9% sequential growth implies roughly 40% more revenue than a year earlier, and the results are due around 29 October.

A channel that has stopped shrinking is not yet a channel that has started ordering. That is the distinction the next print has to settle, and it is the one thing neither the margin arithmetic nor the bond market can settle for it.
