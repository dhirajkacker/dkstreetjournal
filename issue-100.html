Gathering Maintenance Will Push DT Midstream's Third-Quarter Profit Below the Second's

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A gas-infrastructure company whose contracts are supposed to make quarters boring just told investors the next one steps down, and four banks trimmed their targets without touching a rating. The cause sits in the one segment nobody buys the shelf for.

Roughly 70% of DT Midstream's revenue comes from the Pipeline segment on long-term take-or-pay terms with minimum volume commitments. Gathering is paid for molecules actually handled, and planned maintenance plus Northeast producer timing is where revenue growth halved, from +27.3% in the December 2025 quarter to +11.0% in June. Consensus now carries 2026 operating profit of $788.1m, about 0.2% above 2025.

Kinder Morgan and Williams both accelerated last quarter and fell anyway. DT Midstream is the one of the three whose de-rating shows up in its own income statement.

DT Midstream guided its September quarter below the June one, citing planned maintenance across its gathering network and lower Northeast volumes tied to producer timing, with Haynesville volumes flat at the record 2.2 billion cubic feet a day set in the second quarter. Between late September and 6 October four banks cut their price targets on the Detroit company without changing a rating: JPMorgan to $144 from $154, Jefferies to $145, Mizuho to $147 and Barclays to $142. Nothing in the contracted pipeline book changed.

That split is the whole point of owning this kind of asset, and it is why the de-rating landed here rather than on the shelf generally. A pipeline company sells capacity: the shipper pays a reservation charge for the right to move gas whether it moves any or not. Roughly 70% of DT Midstream's revenue is the Pipeline segment, on long-dated take-or-pay agreements with significant minimum volume commitments. Gathering — wellhead compression, dehydration, treatment — is paid per unit handled, and that is the segment a producer's deferred completions and a maintenance window can reach.

Where the growth halved

Revenue growth of 26.6% and 27.3% in the second half of 2025 became 10.9% and 11.0% in the two 2026 quarters. Net income growth fell further, from 52.1% in the December quarter to 4.7% in June. Consensus has absorbed it: average 2026 operating profit of $788.1m is 0.24% above the 2025 estimate, and earnings per share of $4.678 is 6.7% higher against 15.9% growth the year before. UBS models September-quarter EBITDA of $292m against $305m in the June quarter, ahead of the 29 October report.

The re-contracting that is supposed to fix this is also the exposure. NEXUS, about 1.4 billion cubic feet a day, is "effectively fully contracted today, with some shorter-term contracts that roll every couple of years," chief executive David Slater told investors on 30 July, leaving it "in an enviable position as one of the only pipelines with available capacity out of the Appalachia Basin that's available to be contracted for longer term." The company has commercialized 60% of a $3.4bn organic backlog, more than 80% of it pipeline work, with committed capital near $425m this year. Its newest interstate lines — Guardian, Midwestern and Viking, bought from ONEOK for $1.2bn, earn maximum recourse rates set on cost of service plus an allowed return, a federal proceeding rather than a negotiation.

The shares carry 26.96x trailing earnings, down from 32.5x in early May, and 26.51x forward: under half a turn of expansion priced in. On enterprise value to EBITDA, the lens that survives these companies' depreciation and debt, DT Midstream is at 14.49x, between Kinder Morgan's 12.96x and Williams' 15.55x.

The two that accelerated and fell anyway

Kinder Morgan, which runs some 83,000 miles of pipe and 143 terminals, grew June-quarter revenue 10.8% on a steady 30.1% operating margin, with 65% of 2026 budgeted cash flow under take-or-pay reservation terms. Its backlog slipped to $9.6bn from $10.1bn as more than $650m of projects entered service, at a first-full-year project EBITDA multiple held at about 5.6x — unchanged quarter on quarter — with a weighted average in-service date of the first quarter of 2028. It trades at 20.83x trailing, roughly where it sat in May. Williams posted the best quarter of the three, operating margin widening to 38.7% from 32.0%, and holds the worst valuation and a trailing free-cash-flow yield of minus 0.24% while funding the $5.5bn Momentum purchase and 556 megawatts of behind-the-meter generation under a ten-year, primarily fixed-price power contract: 28.84x trailing, against 34.1x in May.

All three bottomed on 28 September and then rose — Kinder Morgan 6.0%, Williams 5.5%, DT Midstream 1.8% — through the week the 30-year Treasury touched 5.73%, its highest since 2002, while Henry Hub gas rose about 13% on the month to $3.20 per million British thermal units. Over three months the separation is cleaner: DT Midstream down 14.6%, Williams down 3.1%, Kinder Morgan up 1.2%.

So the shelf is carrying two different problems under one label. At Kinder Morgan and Williams the operating results got better and the shares did not, and what the market is marking down is the discount applied to cash flows whose in-service dates fall in 2028 and later; at both, consensus 2026 earnings per share now sits at or slightly below the trailing twelve months. At DT Midstream the markdown is already in the reported numbers, and it is concentrated in the segment whose revenue depends on how many wells a Haynesville or Appalachian producer finished this quarter.

Which leaves one honest question for 29 October: whether the maintenance window was the quarter's problem or merely its most legible part.

Hecla Mining produced its silver last quarter for about $6 an ounce after credits for the zinc and lead that come out of the same rock, and the figure fell from the prior quarter because those credits got bigger — $6m bigger. The Coeur d'Alene, Idaho company's shares are down 18% over thirty sessions.

The drawdown runs across all four US-listed silver primaries, and it has been read as the hard-asset trade cracking at the cost line, where high-grade underground mines are supposed to be most exposed. First-half 2026 was the best half any of the four has reported: margins expanding at Hecla and First Majestic, raised guidance at First Majestic, record cash returns at Pan American Silver. What broke was the price of the metal and the cost of holding it. Silver slipped below $60 an ounce on 7 October, a first for 2026, and the 10-year Treasury yielded 5.24% after the Federal Reserve raised rates a quarter point on 16 September in a unanimous vote.

The zinc market sets the silver cost

Hecla does not sell silver. Greens Creek in Alaska and Lucky Friday in Idaho ship polymetallic concentrates to custom smelters, metal traders and third-party processors, and the company reports cash cost and all-in sustaining cost per silver ounce net of by-product credits. The price of zinc is therefore an input to the disclosed price of an ounce of silver. Zinc was $3,805 a tonne on 9 October, recovering from a six-week low as Western smelter capacity shrinks.

The wedge the smelter takes has inverted. Treatment charges on imported zinc concentrate have slumped to minus $50 a tonne, a record low in Fastmarkets data going back a decade, meaning smelters pay miners to secure feed; the 2026 benchmark Korea Zinc agreed with Teck was $85 a tonne, so the benchmark now understates how favorable concentrate terms have become. The $6.07 also excludes Keno Hill in the Yukon, which has not been determined to be in commercial production yet produced roughly 600,000 ounces and nearly $15m of free cash flow in the quarter, its fifth consecutive positive one.

Hecla's revenue fell on price while ounces rose

The metal lines show where the damage came from. Hecla's silver revenue dropped from $295.6m in the first quarter to $213.7m in the second, while zinc and lead together rose to $61.8m. Output went the other way, up 8% to 4.2 million ounces, with Lucky Friday setting a quarterly record on 31% higher milled grades. Gross margin reached 64.9% against 39.3% a year earlier, and the company ended the quarter with $483m of cash and about $472m net of debt, having closed the sale of the Casa Berardi gold mine to Orezone in March. "Hecla enters the third quarter of 2026 from a position of real strength," chief executive Robert Krcmarov said in the August results release.

Pan American is half a gold miner

Pan American, which mines in Canada, Mexico, Peru, Argentina and Bolivia, delivered attributable silver of 6.47 million ounces at the top of guidance and attributable gold of 165.9 thousand ounces below it, on shortfalls at Jacobina, Shahuindo, Timmins and El Peñón. Its silver segment reported $17.80 an ounce of all-in sustaining cost, its gold segment $1,984. Gross margin compressed from 52.7% to 39.3%. "Pan American delivered another quarter of strong financial results, generating $344 million in Attributable free cash flow," chief executive Michael Steinmann said on 11 August. The company repurchased about 4.4 million shares at an average $51.46, some 11% above the 9 October close.

It also guides 2026 taxes paid of $585m to $635m on an assumed $60 silver price, before Mexico's 7.5% duty on mining EBITDA and a 2.0% silver royalty to Royal Gold.

First Majestic, with four Mexican underground mines and Jerritt Canyon in Nevada, is the purest silver exposure: record revenue of $415.5m, up 57%, 60% of it silver, costs below guidance at $25.68 per payable silver-equivalent ounce, and full-year silver guidance raised to 14.6 million ounces, about a tenth above January. Endeavour Silver is the one still spending, with $56.7m of Terronera sustaining capital inside a $91m budget and trailing free cash flow still negative; its revenue is the only one rising sequentially, to $214.0m, as Terronera and the acquired Kolpa mine in Peru add volume faster than price falls.

What the half-year cannot explain

None of it accounts for the fall.

Three of the four were in uptrends in the first days of October and all four crossed into downtrends within seven sessions, Pan American on the 2nd and Hecla on the 9th, then all four rose between 2.6% and 3.7% on 9 October as the metal rebounded. They sit 33% to 46% below January and February peaks, above their July lows, and higher over twelve months.

Two things the results leave open. Forward consensus sits close to annualized first-half run-rate — Hecla at $1.42bn of 2026 revenue, Pan American at 38% earnings growth — but rests on a silver price that has just been breached on the downside. And the scarcity that drove the rally has been called finished: Deutsche Bank's October note projects an 18.5 million ounce surplus in 2027, with London vault stocks up about 70% in a year. "Peak silver scarcity is clearly in the rear-view mirror," the bank's head of metals research, Daniel Ghali, said. On that reading the market's split pricing is a judgment about jurisdiction and balance sheet: Pan American at 13.98x trailing earnings against roughly 26x at its February peak, Hecla still the premium at 34.1x with net cash and US ground.

The credits that make a $6 ounce possible are, in the end, a position in zinc smelting overcapacity. If treatment charges climb back toward the benchmark while the metal stays where it is, the cheapest ounce in the group gets dearer with nothing changing underground.

The day after Brazil's presidential first round, MercadoLibre's shares closed up 9.7% on 1.25m shares against the prior session's 390,000. Nothing in the marketplace had changed. Flávio Bolsonaro had taken 47.03% of valid votes to Lula's 45.16%, beating the polling average and forcing a 25 October runoff, and the session that followed was a repricing of Brazilian fiscal risk: the Bovespa closed at a record 206,911, up about 8%, the dollar fell more than 4% against the real, and Itaú Unibanco's US listing gained 15%.

MercadoLibre, which runs Latin America's largest online marketplace alongside the Mercado Pago payments and lending arm, belongs in that group for a reason its revenue line does not make obvious. Brazil generated $15.2bn of its $28.9bn of 2025 net revenues and financial income, about 52.6%. But the route from a Brazilian election to its earnings runs through a lender's funding cost, not through parcels shipped.

The slab that decides the quarter

Mercado Pago's credit portfolio reached $16.4bn in the June quarter, up 75% year on year, held on balance sheet with a provision against it. Net interest margin after losses was 21%, improved from 18% in the prior quarter, with 15-to-90-day arrears at 7.0% of the book and 4.6% on credit cards; management called those levels close to historical lows. The card book is run at minus 2.5% margin after losses by design, with cohorts reaching breakeven in twelve to eighteen months.

Funding that book is where Brazilian policy reaches the income statement. Copom cut the Selic to 14.00% on 5 August and to 13.75% in September, a fifth consecutive quarter-point reduction and 125 basis points below the 15.00% cycle peak, with minutes still insisting policy stay restrictive. An election outcome read as fiscal discipline is, for this company, a forecast about the cost of money it lends.

The commerce dollar meanwhile keeps getting thinner. Werner is paid by the truck-week, and the truck-week went up

Werner, the Omaha carrier running dry van, temperature-controlled and regional truckload fleets across the US and Mexico plus a brokerage arm, no longer sells much freight at spot. What is being tested is a belief about who pays. If an agent acting for a consumer walks past the page where the advertisement is served, the marketplace's revenue leaves with the traffic. Each of these three, though, is paid by a professional out of a professional's budget: Zillow takes a share of a real-estate agent's commission on a closed sale, Cars.com bills a dealership a monthly subscription, EverQuote bills an auto carrier's marketing line. In the June quarter all three raised that price.

Zillow's cut per closing rose as its visitors left

Zillow Group, the digital real-estate marketplace that sells agent connections, rental listings and home loans, collected 8.8 basis points of the $2.3trn of home-sale value transacted on a trailing-twelve-month basis, against 8.2 basis points a year earlier, according to its second-quarter shareholder letter. Visits fell 2% to 2.5bn in the same quarter while For Sale revenue rose 14% to $549m. The lever is the renamed Preferred programme, under which agents pay a success fee out of commission instead of a per-lead price: Preferred was 61% of connections against 27% a year earlier, earning 23% more revenue per connection in 2025, a premium the company expects to widen to 35% by the end of 2026.

"Zillow is the operating system for modern real estate," chief executive Jeremy Wacksman said with the 5 August results. Rentals, priced per property listed to a different customer entirely, grew 31% to $209m on a record 79,000 multifamily properties; mortgages rose 75% to $84m on purchase originations up 95% to $2.2bn. None of it has reached profit: gross margin slipped to 72.8% from 74.7%, and the quarter carried a $10m operating loss on $772m of revenue. The shares trade at 2.55x trailing sales, against roughly 7x a year ago on the same basis.

Zillow's pool is also shrinking. The thirty-year mortgage pushed past 7.5% in the week to 7 October, with existing-home sales at a 3.98m annual rate, 1.2% below a year earlier. And the policy governing what inventory it may show cost it feed: on 16 September a federal judge dissolved the order preserving Zillow's access to Midwest Real Estate Data's Chicago-area listings after roughly 43,000 were cut off. The stock closed down 7.4% on 23 September.

At Cars.com the shrinking line is the carmakers' ad budget

Cars.com held 19,343 dealer customers against 19,412 a year earlier while average revenue per dealer rose 3% to $2,500. Marketplace revenue grew more than 7%, its fastest since 2021. "Marketplace subscribers rebounded to their highest level since 2023, and marketplace ARPD reached an all-time high," chief executive Tobias Hartmann said on the 6 August call. Total revenue still grew only 0.7%, because the one line sold to carmakers and national advertisers fell 18% to $13.6m.

Operating income rose 83% to $27.9m on an 11% workforce cut, and the diluted share count is down 12% year over year. At 6.7x trailing EBITDA with a 27.6% free-cash-flow yield it is the cheapest of the three on cash, but consensus has revenue growing 0.8% this year, and the software attach is going backwards: AccuTrade subscriptions and Dealer Inspire website customers each fell about 3%. CarGurus, competing for the same dealer budget, grew paying dealers 7% to 34,409.

EverQuote's demand is set inside carriers' loss ratios

EverQuote grew revenue 24.6% to $195.1m at a 29.2% variable marketing margin, auto carriers supplying $172.1m of it. The cause sits in underwriting: the US personal-auto loss ratio improved to 64% at the end of 2025 from 86% in late 2022, and repaired carriers buy growth. Progressive alone spent $1.4bn on advertising in the June quarter, up 16%. Carriers show a "higher willingness to pay this year than there has been in recent years," chief executive Jayme Mendal told investors on the Q2 call. The third-quarter guide of $198m-$208m implies growth slowing to roughly 14-20%, and the shares sit at 9.9x forward earnings.

The June quarters do not account for what happened in the thirty days to 9 October, when Zillow fell 9.5%, Cars.com 12.1% and EverQuote 15.5%. Two causes are named rather than inferred. Zillow's twelve-month 59% decline is the one with a business mechanism attached: it is paid per closing, closings are set by a 7.5% mortgage, and its own listing standards now cost it inventory. Cars.com and EverQuote, paid by contract and by auction regardless of how many houses change hands, held within 10% over twelve months and gave up their gains in two weeks on the question of whether a consumer will still arrive at all.

EverQuote is not waiting for the market to decide. On 8 October it published a paper titled "Agentic Barbarians at the Carriers' Gates," in which Mendal wrote that agentic artificial intelligence "has the potential to shift insurance distribution in the U.S." and change how often consumers shop. A company whose revenue is the price of a shopper has an interest in that shopping continuing to happen somewhere it can charge for it.

AutoNation's finance chief spent part of 17 September explaining model-year changeover to investors at Morgan Stanley's Laguna conference. Gross profit on each new vehicle would land about 10% below the second quarter's, he said, with parts-and-service growth moderating as customers defer elective work and finance income softer; the company was "having to meet the customer where they are and give a little bit more on price." The warning was read as a verdict on car demand. What it described was the condition of three profit lines that have quietly become the business: the finance-and-insurance desk, the service drive, and the loan book the dealer now funds itself.

The new car is the small line

Lithia Motors, which runs 467 stores selling new and used vehicles plus financing, warranties, repair and parts under the Lithia and Driveway brands, shows the arithmetic plainly. In its June quarter, new-vehicle sales produced $4,829.2m of revenue and $284.0m of gross profit, a margin of 5.9%. The finance desk booked $366.4m of revenue, and because commissions on loans, warranties and insurance contracts carry essentially no cost of goods, all $366.4m of it was gross profit. Parts and service produced $633.0m, 2.2 times the new-vehicle line.

That shape holds across the industry, and it is moving the wrong way at the front. New-vehicle gross profit per unit at the public groups fell 15.8% year over year to $2,764 in the second quarter, while used slipped only 2.0%. Finance and insurance has been the defended dollar, at $2,569 per vehicle retailed, more than 40% above 2019's $1,798. Service is no longer outrunning inflation: same-store fixed-operations gross profit grew 2.3% against June consumer prices up 3.5%.

Inventory is not the culprit. Cox Automotive put new-vehicle days' supply at 76 in late September, down 1% from a year earlier, with incentives at 6.5% of average transaction price and the full-year sales forecast raised to 16.1 million units.

Three weeks aimed at the back end

Two days before AutoNation spoke, the Federal Trade Commission published price-transparency FAQs for auto dealers: the advertised price must be what any consumer can walk in and pay, excluding only charges government requires, so a $40,000 vehicle carrying a dealer document fee of $85 must be advertised at $40,085. The agency had already sent warning letters to 97 dealership groups in March.

On 16 September the Federal Open Market Committee raised its target range a quarter point to 3.75%-4.00%, the first increase since 2023. Floor-plan borrowing, the credit line that carries unsold inventory, reprices with it: Lithia's floor-plan interest expense rose $14.7m in the June quarter, while Penske's same-store equivalent fell 9.8%.

Then on 5 October Morgan Stanley's Daniela Haigian cut AutoNation to Equal-weight with a $175 target, from $250, and Group 1 Automotive to Underweight, trimming implied multiples across the franchise dealers by 1.2 turns. Her argument was the back end again: fee disclosure plus AI shopping agents that make cross-retailer price comparison frictionless, with Carvana scored as the best-positioned name in auto retail after a record quarter of roughly 197,000 retail units.

From 9 September to 9 October the six franchise groups fell an equal-weighted 19%, AutoNation worst at 24% and Penske least at 10%. Four of the six now sit in established downtrends; only Lithia and Penske still hold mild uptrends.

One guide-down, one record quarter

AutoNation fell on its own numbers. Revenue has declined year over year for three straight quarters, operating margin has gone from 7.50% in 2022 to 4.74% in 2025, and 2025 net income fell 6.2% while earnings per share rose, because diluted shares have dropped from 75.0m in 2021 to 33.8m. Its captive lender, at a $2.7bn portfolio, earned $20.1m in the first half against a $39.6m provision for credit losses. At 7.4x forward earnings against 7.1x trailing, the multiple prices a flat-to-down year.

Lithia's quarter went the other way. Revenue rose 2.2% to $9.8bn, new-vehicle gross per unit held at $2,718 for a third consecutive quarter, used retail gross per unit gained $339 sequentially, and Driveway Finance originated a record $884m. "Financing Operations delivered 80% income growth, with expanding margins and increasing penetration," chief executive Bryan DeBoer told investors on 29 July. The cost of that engine sits two filings down: in the March quarter, $21.0m of the credit provision was net charge-offs against $5.4m from portfolio growth, and funding the loan book leaves trailing free cash flow at minus 7.9% of market value even on a 9.6x trailing multiple. Forward, Lithia trades at 8.1x.

Penske held up because a quarter of it is not a car dealership. Premium brands were 71% of retail automotive franchised revenue in the first half, and equity earnings of $57.4m from Penske Transportation Solutions exceeded the entire commercial-truck segment's $47.2m of pretax income and equalled 22% of group net income. At 14.7x forward against 14.4x trailing, Penske carries the highest multiple of the three and the flattest consensus earnings path.

What the quarters have to settle

The selloff is a repricing of the fee, finance and service dollar, and only one of the three protagonists has reported deterioration in it. AutoNation guided its own front end down and has the contracting revenue line to match. Lithia's per-unit economics stopped falling two quarters ago, which leaves its 20% monthly decline resting on what a regulator's guidance and a downgrade say about lines that are still earning. Penske's defense is brand mix and a freight stake, neither of which a car buyer explains.

Lithia reports on 21 October, AutoNation on 22 October and Penske on 28 October. Three figures in those releases do the work the revenue line cannot: gross profit per unit by line, the captive lenders' charge-offs, and what the service bays grew in a quarter when customers are being told to defer.

A dealer can live with a thin margin on the metal. What it has never had to price is a customer who sees the whole number before walking through the door.

The $38.5m of idle plant sitting inside the margin

An analog maker that owns its fabs reports under-loaded capacity inside cost of goods sold, so the margin recovers as the plant fills. June-quarter non-GAAP gross margin of 63.8% still carried $38.5m of capacity under-utilisation charges, worth about 260 basis points, down roughly $8m from the prior quarter and guided down by a similar amount again. Microchip also has around $450m of equipment in storage; the company says its internal fabs are not the constraint on growth, pointing instead to outside foundries, assembly-and-test subcontractors and its own production throughput.

That arithmetic is why gross profit grew 62.8% and operating income roughly tenfold on 38% more revenue. "Improving demand, continued inventory normalization, and stronger factory utilization contributed to better operational leverage," chief executive Steve Sanghi said in the 6 August release.

Priced under both comparisons on gross profit

Trailing earnings are useless here. Revenue fell 42% in a single year, from $7.63bn in fiscal 2024 to $4.40bn in fiscal 2025, so the 104.7x trailing price-to-earnings ratio is struck on trough profits. On gross profit, the line that moves with fab loading, Microchip trades at 10.65x forward against 20.25x for Texas Instruments and 29.72x for Monolithic Power. Its trailing figure has fallen to 13.31x from 14.13x on 22 August, a stretch in which gross profit grew 62.8%.

The two comparisons are earned differently. Texas Instruments is paid for owning its plant, and said trailing cash flow from operations "underscored the strength of the business model, quality of the product portfolio and benefit of 300mm production" — free cash flow of $6.5bn against $1.8bn a year earlier, about a quarter of it $1.6bn of federal chip incentives, with 2026 depreciation guided to $2.2–2.4bn; it trades at 33.3x forward earnings after its trailing multiple compressed from 49.6x in May while the stock rose 32.1% over six months. Monolithic Power, at 50.5x forward earnings, is paid for data-center sockets: enterprise-data revenue of $380.6m was 38.8% of the June quarter and up 164% year on year.

The yield shock all six of them shared

Nothing in Microchip's reported results accounts for its de-rating. In the week to 9 October all six power-analog names fell together, by 2.6% to 13.9%, with no company-specific news at any of the three examined here, while the semiconductor index was down as much as 29% from its June peak on crude above $84 a barrel and Treasury yields near multi-decade highs — the long-yield pressure this page has already described. The likelier reading is a repricing of long-duration earnings rather than a demand event, against industry sales that hit a record $120.6bn in May.

The open question is mix. Microchip projects data-center revenue up about 69%, to roughly $1bn in calendar 2026, on memory and interconnect controller content; that is real and still small beside a company guiding toward about $6.4bn of annual revenue, with industrial and automotive customers dominating the consolidated statement. A rate shock does not empty a sales channel. Weak factory and car demand would.

September-quarter guidance of 7% to 9% sequential growth implies roughly 40% more revenue than a year earlier, and the results are due around 29 October.

A channel that has stopped shrinking is not yet a channel that has started ordering. That is the distinction the next print has to settle, and it is the one thing neither the margin arithmetic nor the bond market can settle for it.
