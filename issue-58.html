Atlassian's artificial-intelligence assistant does not have a price. Rovo Search, Chat and Agents are bundled into every paid Jira, Confluence and Jira Service Management subscription, and each licensed seat earns a monthly pool of credits set by plan tier — 25 on Standard, 70 on Premium and 150 on Enterprise, with a Rovo chat or agent request costing ten. As of May 2026 the company was not billing anything for usage above that allowance, and has promised at least 90 days' notice and an explicit customer opt-in before it does.

That makes Atlassian — the Sydney-founded vendor of Jira project tracking, Confluence knowledge pages and Jira Service Management desks, sold by the seat to more than 350,000 customers — the clearest test of the question hanging over all per-seat software: what happens to a seat meter when the software starts doing the work the seat was hired for. Its own risk register names the exposure. Its answer is not a meter but a ladder.

The upgrade is the product

Chief executive Mike Cannon-Brookes named the mechanism on the August 6 call: "the Teamwork Collection with approximately 10x as many Rovo credits included is a great motivator, inducement for upgrade in terms of hitting those limits." Customers on that bundle carry four to five times more paid seats per instance than stand-alone Jira or Confluence accounts. One product, Rovo Dev Standard, is priced outright at $20 per user per month; no revenue or customer figure has been attached to it.

What Atlassian publishes instead is adoption: Rovo in use at over 80% of the Fortune 500, Rovo-assisted actions up 50% sequentially, adopters growing annual recurring revenue more than twice as fast as non-adopters. It publishes no absolute paid-seat count and no net expansion rate. monday.com, which sells per-seat work management to overlapping buyers, discloses that its AI revenue doubled sequentially to roughly 1% of recurring revenue and 17% of net new, and that net dollar retention was 109%. An industry report from May 2026 recorded Atlassian's first-ever decline in enterprise seat counts; management's June-quarter language of broad-based seat expansion contradicts it, and no outside reader can adjudicate, because the number does not exist in the disclosure.

A very good year, guided in half

The business momentum is real. Quarterly revenue growth accelerated through fiscal 2026 to 27.6% in the June quarter, cloud grew 31% and remaining performance obligations rose 44% to $4.82bn. Operating income swung to $211.7m from a $28.5m loss. Most striking, gross margin expanded 381 basis points to 86.9% — where ServiceNow's fell 680 basis points to 70.7% and Elastic's slipped to 74.5%. Inference has not reached Atlassian's cost of revenue.

Next year it does. Fiscal 2027 is guided to about 13% revenue growth, recurring-revenue growth of 18% against 23%, and gross margin down to 84.5% explicitly on Rovo usage and hosting. Data Center revenue is guided to fall 17%, the reversal of a pull-forward Atlassian created itself when it announced in September 2025 that most Data Center products end in March 2029.

What the price now assumes

The shares rose 85% between July 29 and August 28. The August 7 earnings reaction supplied 35.8% and the August 27 sector session — Salesforce up 22.6%, ServiceNow 9.2%, the software complex outrunning the Nasdaq — another 10.3%. The remaining 21 sessions compounded to 23.5%, against a run of target increases beginning with Bank of America's upgrade to Buy at $175 and RBC's $215 initiation on August 22.

Price per dollar of trailing gross profit went from about 4.1 times at the end of February to 8.97 times — a 118% expansion while trailing gross profit grew 29%. monday.com trades near a third of that on faster growth; Microsoft, whose GitHub and Copilot stack competes for the same developer budget, trades at 17 times on a 68% gross margin. Forward earnings of 34.6 times rest on consensus 2027 earnings per share of $5.498, fractionally below the $5.505 just delivered.

The business earns the re-rating's first leg: accelerating cloud, a GAAP profit swing, and expanding margin in a quarter when peers' margins broke. What it does not earn is the second leg, priced for a year the company itself describes as half as fast and no more profitable, with the AI bill arriving in cost of revenue while the AI revenue arrives nowhere.

The September quarter is the first reported under that guide. It will show cloud growth, recurring revenue and margin. It will not show how many seats Atlassian sold.

The sell side has stopped debating how fast The Trade Desk can grow and started modeling how long it will shrink. Consensus now carries revenue falling 4.6% this year and a further 4.5% in 2027 at the Ventura, California company, which runs an independent demand-side platform — the software advertising agencies use to buy connected television, display, audio and mobile inventory on behalf of brands.

That reset followed the August 6 second-quarter call, and it dragged the price with it. HSBC's Mohammed Khallouf cut the shares to Reduce on 17 August with a $10 target, down from $20, sending them to their lowest level since January 2019; Guggenheim, Evercore ISI, DA Davidson, BMO and Scotiabank also downgraded, and the average target fell 43% to $13.89 — roughly where the stock actually trades. The stake now is the floor rather than the miss: a company that still generated $136m of free cash flow in the June quarter, at a 13.5% trailing free-cash-flow yield, is being priced as though the meter it charges against is permanently smaller.

The meter is running slower

Growth did not break in one quarter. It ran 17.7%, then 14.3%, then 11.8%, then 3.0% on June-quarter revenue of $715.1m. Third-quarter revenue is guided to at least $650m, about 12% below last year, with adjusted earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization of around $160m against $241m in the second quarter. Operating income fell 13% year on year and net income 28.6% — profit contracting faster than the top line slows.

Underneath, the volume signals are uneven rather than uniformly bad. Connected TV grew double digits and is more than half of revenue; joint business plans with large advertisers grew 38%; accounts outside the top 500 grew more than 50%. The shortfall is concentrated in a handful of the biggest accounts, in consumer-goods and automotive categories that management put at about a quarter of platform spend, with some brands moving from premium decisioning to cheaper guaranteed buying.

The pricing story is not where it is usually assumed to be. Management's position is that its take rate has been flat for a decade, and that Kokai, its unified identifier and new artificial-intelligence tools went in without rate increases. The visible take-rate compression is at Criteo, the retail-media and performance advertising company, whose media spend grew 9% to $1.1bn last quarter while its own contribution net of traffic-acquisition costs fell 12% in constant currency. What squeezes The Trade Desk instead is the price of the media itself: Amazon made ads the default on Prime Video, adding roughly 50 billion impressions and taking its own connected-TV prices from about $35 per thousand views to $28 within a year, with buyers reporting broad declines of 10% to 30%. A percentage-of-spend meter runs slower on cheaper impressions — and Amazon's demand-side platform, which charges nothing on its own inventory, went from under 10% to just under 20% of global programmatic share in about fifteen months.

The macro alibi

"These numbers are not a reflection of our company or the long-term opportunity in front of us," chief executive Jeff Green told investors on the August 6 call, pointing to tariffs, inflation and a weaker consumer alongside the company's own execution. The word "macro" was used 14 times on that call. Meta grew revenue 28.0% in the same quarter. Magnite, the sell-side platform that monetizes the connected-TV inventory The Trade Desk buys, grew 11.2% with gross profit up 20.7% and operating income up 42.2%; its shares are up 76% since late February while The Trade Desk's have roughly halved. The impressions are being transacted. The independent buy side is not capturing them.

What the price embeds

The market values the company at about 2.7 times trailing gross profit, against roughly 21 times at the August 2025 peak — between Magnite at 7.1 times and Criteo, in outright decline, at 0.9. On cash the same ordering holds: 7.1x enterprise value to trailing cash earnings versus Magnite's 22.3x. Buybacks are real, cutting diluted shares 5.2% year on year, with $78m repurchased last quarter and $269m of authorization left against $1.5bn of cash. Green himself bought roughly 6m shares in early March at weighted average prices between $23.49 and $25.08 — about $150m, which he called the biggest personal purchase of his life.

On earnings the picture reverses. Consensus models $0.402 of earnings per share this year against $0.91 last year, which puts the shares at 33.8 times forward earnings and 16.2 times trailing — dearer on next year than on last. Nor has the chart resolved anything: 15 sessions since 10 August between $12.95 and $14.14, with closing lows of $12.95 and then $13.03, is a flat shelf rather than a sequence of higher lows, inside a downtrend unbroken since 13 November.

So the year-long de-rating is earned, and the case that it has overshot has to be made on cash flow alone. The cash multiple prices a business in permanent decline; the forward earnings multiple prices one whose profits halve and then stabilize. Both cannot be right, and the guide gives no help — management has said forward visibility is now a constraint on what it can forecast at all.

The company has told investors to expect less revenue in the September quarter than it booked a year ago. Whether Green's March purchase was early or simply wrong turns on how many quarters like that follow it.

Chief executive Andy Jassy told investors on the July 30 call that customers want the silicon on its own: "We do have an increasing number of customers who are interested in us providing the Trainium chips to them, separate from our cloud, and we're actively having those conversations and exploring, and I expect there's a real chance we'll do that in the future," he said. Management said AWS will not have enough capacity to meet 2026 demand, expects the same in 2027, and has already committed significant 2028 capacity. Anthropic and Amazon have agreed to expand to as much as 5 gigawatts of new compute across Trainium generations.

Where the franchise is losing ground

The profit is real; the relative position is not improving. Microsoft's Azure grew 43% on the identical June quarter, passing $100bn of annual revenue, and Google Cloud grew 82% to $24.8bn with segment margin widening from 20.7% to 35.6% — approaching AWS's level from a far smaller base. AWS's backlog of $496bn is now smaller than Alphabet's $514bn cloud backlog, despite AWS booking roughly 1.7 times Google Cloud's quarterly revenue. Alphabet disclosed that just over half of its backlog converts within 24 months; no comparable duration figure for AWS is discoverable, so whether Amazon's number reflects longer contracts cannot be tested.

One suspicion about the earnings quality inverts on inspection. Amazon did not stretch its depreciation schedule to flatter profit: it shortened the estimated useful life of a subset of servers and networking equipment from six years to five, effective January 1, 2025, citing the pace of AI development, at an expected cost of about $0.7bn to 2025 operating income plus roughly $0.6bn from early retirements. The margin expansion came through that, not around it. Net income of $62.6bn is a different matter — it includes $53.4bn of non-operating income, largely a mark-to-market gain on the Anthropic stake, and describes nothing about operations.

What the shares have paid for it

The stock is up 26.9% over 180 days, but the last month's gain was earned in two sessions: a 15.32% move on July 31, the largest in the name in at least a year, following the July 30 release. Shares peaked at $284.02 on August 3 and have given back 6.2% since, a retreat market coverage attributes to capital-spending anxiety rather than any AWS deterioration, alongside a New Jersey attorney general suit filed August 4 and a Jeff Bezos filing to sell more than $4bn of stock.

Against that, the price per dollar of Amazon's annual gross profit — what a share costs relative to trailing gross profit — has fallen to 7.28x from 7.75x in early May, even as market value rose to about $2.87tn. Trailing enterprise value to earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization of 11.9x sits below Alphabet's 13.1x and Microsoft's 19.0x. The headline 21.2x trailing earnings is an artifact of the Anthropic mark; consensus FY2027 earnings of $10.61 a share, which assumes it does not repeat, implies about 25x.

So the re-rating is paid for. The business earned the move: accelerating revenue, expanding margin, and the majority of incremental profit coming from the segment the story is about. What nothing in the quarter settles is share — AWS is compounding profit while growing slower than both rivals and carrying a smaller order book than one of them.

The next test is arithmetic, not narrative. Amazon guided September-quarter operating income to $22.5bn–$26.5bn, below the June quarter, with a Prime Day timing shift blamed for most of the apparent slowdown. Capacity Jassy says he cannot build fast enough eventually becomes depreciation whether or not the demand shows up.
