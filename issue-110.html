Vertex's Invoice Volume Is Set by French Law, and It Lowered Its Cloud Growth Forecast

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

A shelf of financial-services software re-rated hard through October without a single one of its members reporting results, and the three vendors whose revenue runs on one regulated transaction turn out to be mirror images rather than one story.

Vertex has statutory volume: France's e-invoicing and transaction-reporting obligation for its largest companies began on 1 September 2026, Germany's issuing rule starts in 2027. It still lowered full-year cloud revenue growth guidance to 18% on slower customer migration, with annual recurring revenue up 10.5% and net revenue retention of 105%, down from 108%.

BlackLine has no regulator setting anything: recurring revenue grew 6% against a 13-16% ambition, retention 102.4%, held back by its own move off per-seat pricing. FICO's price lever, the only thing that drove its year, is frozen. All three report within four days.

Vertex sells the indirect-tax engine that calculates sales tax and value-added tax inside corporate order and billing systems, and it has something few software vendors have: a legislature setting its volumes. France now requires its largest value-added-tax registrants to issue electronic invoices and to submit consumer and cross-border transaction data to the tax authority through approved platforms, an obligation that began on 1 September 2026, with Germany's issuing rule starting in 2027 for firms above €800,000 of prior-year turnover. In August the company lowered its full-year cloud revenue growth guidance to 18%.

That gap matters because of what happened to the shares without it. Across the 30 trading days to 30 October, eight of nine financial-services software names rose, an equal-weighted 16.4% against a 0.9% decline in the S&P 500 tracking fund, with Vertex up 32.1% and BlackLine up 26.9% and both moving from a year-long downtrend into an uptrend. Not one of the nine reported results inside that window. The Federal Reserve held its benchmark near 3.9% at its 27-28 October meeting. The month was read as proof that vertical software billed inside a regulated workflow keeps its pricing power while generic seats get eaten by artificial intelligence. The three vendors that actually sit in such a workflow do not agree with each other about that.

A legislature sets the volume; the customer sets the timing

Vertex's annual recurring revenue reached $703.4m at 30 June, up 10.5%, with net revenue retention at 105% against 108% a year earlier and average annual revenue per customer of $142,997, up 9.2%. Cloud revenue was $101.7m, up 17.9%, inside full-year guidance of $825-830m. The e-invoicing line grew well above the corporate average and the company won several six-figure enterprise deals, including a mid-six-figure expansion driven by mandates in two countries, France and Finland. But the cloud guide came down because convergence and migration ran slower than planned; management called it a conversion timing issue rather than a revenue issue, customers delaying on information-technology priorities and broader enterprise-resource-planning schedules. Chief executive Christopher Young told the 3 August call that cloud growth should accelerate on e-invoicing.

The price paid for that promise moved first. Against a constant $488.6m of gross profit from the last four reported quarters, Vertex's price went from roughly 3.85x that figure on 30 September to 5.09x on 30 October, within reach of the 6.9x implied at its 12-month high. Reported revenue growth has been flat to slower for a year: 10.5% in June, 11.1% in March, against 16.5% for 2024.

BlackLine is slowing itself down on purpose

BlackLine automates the accounting close for large enterprises, and nobody legislates its price. Recurring revenue was $719m at June, up 6%, against the 13-16% the company holds out as its ambition, with dollar-based net revenue retention of 102.4% that the company attributed partly to lower levels of user adds. The cause is partly its own doing: nearly 90% of net new business now lands on platform pricing instead of seats, and platform recurring revenue rose to more than 17% of eligible recurring revenue from 13% in a single quarter, a shift management concedes understates demand near term.

"We saw a 4X increase in the number of customers on Verity, and we have now proof points in terms of the monetization of our agentic revenue," chief executive Owen Ryan said on the 4 August call. Reported revenue grew 9.2% to $187.8m with operating income up 45.7%. The shares now cost 3.55x trailing gross profit against about 2.80x a month earlier and roughly 6.2x a year ago, and the largest single session of the advance, 8.4% on 22 October, came on volume 73% below average alongside unrelated software names.

FICO had the only price lever, and a regulator opened the socket

Fair Isaac is the one of the three whose per-unit price did all the work and the one that did not turn. Mortgage originations were 71% of business-to-business Scores revenue and 62% of total Scores revenue in the June quarter, where mortgage-origination revenue rose 97% on score volumes up only low single digits after the wholesale royalty went from $4.95 to $10.00. TransUnion has now fixed VantageScore 4.0 mortgage pricing at $0.99 through 2028 and supplies it free alongside a FICO pull, and on 23 October FICO held its own price at $10 for lenders in a new Preferred Program. The shares fell 13.2% the next session, and Wells Fargo cut its target to $735 from $800 while UBS went to $942 from $1,100. At 15.2x forward and 20.4x trailing earnings, against roughly 62x trailing in January, the stock is cheap because the lever that made the numbers is frozen.

The month paid for pricing power none of the three has shown

The results explain almost none of the advance, because there were no results. The likelier reading is the unwind of the de-rating that took software down on fears customers would build applications in-house with artificial intelligence; the S&P 500 software and services index reached its highest level since November 2025 in early October, and it landed hardest on the cheapest shelf. Breadth was not universal: Alkami, which sells digital banking platforms, fell 11.3% over the same 30 days and slid into a downtrend. And the one consensus revision discoverable inside the window went down.

Size the difference between the three and the split has a cause in each case. Vertex's volume is written into statute but its revenue recognition waits on customer migration schedules it does not control. BlackLine's growth is being suppressed by a pricing migration it chose, which is also the only answer it has to being paid for work its software removes. FICO showed what a genuine per-unit price increase does to a profit and loss statement, and then lost the right to repeat it.

FICO and Vertex report after the close on 4 November, BlackLine on 5 November. Within four days the shelf that re-rated on no information at all will have three quarters of it.

Waste Connections and Casella Were Repriced for a Volume Turn Only GFL Has Reported

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Three regional waste haulers were marked up through October on the belief that price per ton had finally outrun cost after four years of shrinking tonnage. The two largest operators in the industry rose as much or more, and most of the trio's gain landed before any of them reported.

Waste Connections took 5.5% core price against solid-waste volumes down 1.2% and raised both its outlook and its dividend. GFL Environmental was the only one of the three to report tonnage growing. Casella Waste Systems has not published a September quarter at all; its last one grew revenue 16.9% while operating income fell 22.4%.

The results account for the move at Waste Connections and GFL. They do not at Casella, where consensus has 2026 revenue up 14.4% and adjusted EBITDA up 0.3%. GFL's price has a non-operating anchor: a take-private deadline.

Waste Connections told investors on 22 October it was lifting its full-year outlook and its quarterly dividend, and the shares closed 9.8% higher that day. A week later GFL Environmental raised guidance for the third time this year and became the only one of these three haulers to report its tonnage growing. Casella Waste Systems, the smallest of the three, has not published a September quarter at all.

October was read as the month regional hauling economics turned, with price taken per ton finally outrunning cost after four years in which the tons themselves shrank. The reading has support outside the trio. Republic Services took 6.3% core price on related revenue against roughly flat volume and saw small and large containers both grow for the first time in over three years. "I think recycling and waste will tip to positive growth in 2027 across the industry, and we'll certainly try to match that or do a little better than that performance," chief executive Jon Vander Ark said on 29 October. Waste Management, meanwhile, still guides collection and disposal volume to about -1% for the year.

The bid was not discriminating, and most of it arrived before any numbers did. Over the 30 days to 30 October, Waste Connections rose 19.6%, Republic 16.3%, Casella 16.2% and Waste Management 14.4%, with GFL up 8.1%. By 21 October, the day before the first of the three reported, Waste Connections was already up 9.4% and Casella 12.9%. Across the trailing year the three regional names gained between 3.3% and 12.7% against 16.4% for Waste Management: October was catch-up inside a de-rated group, a defensive bid on the whole sector rather than a verdict on the smaller three.

5.5% price against 1.2% fewer tons

Waste Connections, which owns 61 municipal solid-waste landfills and 142 transfer stations serving secondary and exclusive markets across the US and Canada, published the cleanest version of the industry's algorithm. September-quarter revenue of $2.638bn was up 7.3%, built on 5.5% core price against solid-waste volumes down 1.2%, a 70-basis-point sequential improvement in volume. Adjusted EBITDA margin reached 34.0%, 20 basis points better than a year earlier and roughly 50 before the diesel spike, which cost $21.6m of fuel expense. Special-waste tons rose 7% and construction-and-demolition tons 5%. The full-year outlook went to about $10.09bn of revenue and the dividend rose 10% to $0.385 a share.

Two helpers in that quarter do not repeat. Recycling revenue was up 12% on higher values for old corrugated containers, the recovered cardboard haulers sell to mills; that grade averaged $38 a ton in October, half September's $77. And the company's own framing for next year is unexciting: mid- to high-single-digit revenue growth, roughly a point of it rollover from acquisitions and renewable natural gas plants, on a target spread of 150 basis points between price and cost. The shares carry 17.4x trailing EV/EBITDA against 14.6x at Waste Management and 15.5x at Republic. California's attorney general, Rob Bonta, sued the company in late October over hazardous-waste management at the closed Chiquita Canyon landfill.

Casella bought $948m of revenue and $5m of operating profit

Casella is vertically integrated across the Northeast with 50 collection operations, eight Subtitle D landfills and 65 transfer stations. Between 2021 and 2025 its revenue more than doubled, from $889m to $1,837m, while operating income moved only from $84.4m to $89.2m and net income fell from $41.1m to $7.9m. The June quarter kept that shape: revenue up 16.9% to $543.7m, operating income down 22.4% to $20.0m.

Pricing is not the weak link. Solid-waste price rose 5.8% in collection and 4.7% in disposal, in a market Brad Helgeson, the chief financial officer, described on the second-quarter call: "The dynamic in the Northeast is that landfill capacity is coming out of the market, and you have more and more tons looking for less and less landfill capacity." But internalization did not improve: of 86,000 additional landfill tons, 62,000 came from third parties against 24,000 from Casella's own routes. Net leverage stood at 2.7x and margin excluding fuel and resource solutions widened 30 basis points. Consensus has revenue up 14.4% this year with adjusted EBITDA up 0.3%. At 18.25x trailing EV/EBITDA and a 1.81% trailing free-cash-flow yield, it is the dearest of the five haulers on both. Its September quarter is due after the close on 9 November.

GFL's tons turned and its interest bill rose 39%

GFL, the Vaughan, Ontario operator of solid-waste collection, disposal and soil remediation, has the one positive volume print in the group. Core price of 5.7% came with volume up 50 basis points, landfill tons positive for the first time since early 2025 and construction-and-demolition tons up 4.5%. "Organic revenue growth accelerated 100 basis points over the second quarter, driven by core pricing ahead of expectations and 50 basis point improvements in both volume and commodity prices," chief financial officer Luke Pelosi said on 29 October. Adjusted EBITDA margin was 31.5%, flat as reported and about 125 basis points better underlying.

What the Environmental Services divestiture did not buy was cheaper money. GFL re-levered to fund SECURE Waste Infrastructure, closing the quarter at 3.8x net leverage after issuing 75 million shares and a $1bn term loan, and interest and finance costs reached $179.4m against $129.2m a year earlier. SECURE added $150m of revenue and over $50m of EBITDA in its first month inside the group, a mid-30s margin above GFL's own. Management puts the stock at 12 to 12.5x its 2027 EBITDA sketch against 18.6x trailing. The nearer anchor is procedural: the special committee's deadline for updated take-private proposals is 9 November.

Where the split comes from

The published results explain the month at two of the three. Waste Connections prices inside exclusive and secondary markets where it also owns the disposal, and its margin gain survives the fuel hit only on an underlying basis, because diesel at a record $6.52 a gallon in September passes through to customers on a one-to-two-month lag. GFL's improvement is partly bought: SECURE's higher-margin assets consolidated on 1 September, and management has warned of fourth-quarter volume deceleration as extended-producer-responsibility contracts lapse. Casella's gain priced a quarter that does not exist yet, and its last one showed routes bought with debt whose depreciation and interest absorb the pricing taken on them.

None of this can be settled at the ton. No company in the group publishes tonnage reconcilable to revenue or EBITDA, so core price against unit volume and adjusted EBITDA margin are as close to margin per ton as anyone outside the companies gets. "Free cash flow per share in a normal growth algorithm over the next five years, there's no path in which I don't think that free cash flow isn't close to a double," GFL's founder and chief executive Patrick Dovigi said on 29 October. That is a claim about five years, made four days before a sale process closes.

Both of the open questions resolve on the same date. On 9 November, Casella prints the quarter its shares have already moved on, and GFL learns what a buyer will pay for a business whose tons have only just started growing again.

Applied Optoelectronics Nearly Doubled Revenue and Earned Less on Every Dollar It Shipped

Hypothesis Opus 5 · Research Opus 5 · Writing Opus 5 · Prompt v1.7

Four suppliers of silicon inside AI optical links were marked down together in October, and only one of them has shipped-unit economics moving the wrong way. Applied Optoelectronics grew June-quarter revenue 86% year on year while gross margin slipped to 27.7% from 30.3%, so gross profit grew slower than sales and the operating loss widened.

MACOM and SiTime went the other way, MACOM to a 58.3% gross margin on 36% revenue growth and SiTime to its first operating profit; MaxLinear raised its optical data-center outlook on 22 October. The complication for Applied Optoelectronics is its funding: it finished a share-sale program in October at an average $105.36 and closed the month at $69.05, below the price at which it paid for its own 1.6-terabit capacity. None of the four had reported a September quarter when the selling happened.

Applied Optoelectronics is shipping nearly twice the fiber-optic gear it was a year ago and keeping less of each dollar that comes in. The Sugar Land, Texas maker of optical modules, lasers and transceivers for internet data-center operators, cable and telecom equipment makers grew June-quarter revenue 86% year on year to $191.9m, and reported a gross margin of 27.7% against 30.3% a year earlier. Gross profit rose 71%, slower than sales, and the operating loss widened to $24.7m.

October's selling across the optical layer was read as a repricing of artificial-intelligence capital spending: a valuation event visited on companies whose order books were still filling. For most of the group that reading survives inspection. For Applied Optoelectronics it does not, because the two things a buyer could check without waiting for a quarterly report, what a shipped dollar earns and the price at which the capacity was financed, both moved against the company.

Mix, not volume, decides the transceiver margin

Management has been explicit about where margin comes from. "[T]he most important factor is the percentage of 1.6 terabit transceiver, because gross margin is very good," chief financial and strategy officer Stefan Murry told the second-quarter call on 6 August, adding that "the more we deliver for 1.6T, the high gross margin it would be." On a non-GAAP basis that quarter came in at 29.8%, inside the 29-30% guide but below 30.4% a year earlier, with the September quarter guided to 29.0-30.5%.

The volume is real. The company has a volume order above $200m for 1.6T data-center transceivers from a returning hyperscale customer, shipping from the third quarter and completing in the fourth after qualification, and by August had raised its end-2026 target to roughly 650,000 pieces a month of combined 800G and 1.6T product. Consensus has revenue more than doubling to $1.049bn this year and reaching $2.961bn in 2027.

The capacity was funded at $105.36

In October the company completed a $600m at-the-market equity program, selling 5,694,845 shares at an average $105.36 for about $588m net against 84.9m shares outstanding beforehand, roughly 6.7% dilution. The stock closed 30 October at $69.05, a third below that average and 69% below its 52-week closing high of $223.10. Price to trailing gross profit, the usable measure on a loss-making maker of hardware at half the group's margin, stands at 34.0x against 76.0x in mid-August.

Three peers got better at the same business

MACOM, the Lowell, Massachusetts maker of analog and photonic semiconductors from radio frequency through lightwave, grew July-quarter revenue 35.8% to $342.2m with gross margin up 4.6 points to 58.3% and operating income more than doubled to $77.1m. Its data-center line reached $137.6m, about 40% of revenue, on a record 1.6-to-1 book-to-bill that chief executive Stephen Daly attributed primarily to 1.6T and 800G platforms. September-quarter guidance of $415m-$425m at a 60-61% adjusted gross margin was set above the quarter just reported. It trades at 27.2x trailing gross profit against 38.0x in August, with a 27.5x forward price-earnings ratio set against fiscal 2027 consensus earnings of $8.54 a share.

SiTime, the Santa Clara maker of silicon MEMS precision timing, grew revenue 126.5% to $157.4m, lifted gross margin 11.1 points to 63.0% and crossed into operating profit at $8.2m. Chief executive Rajesh Vashist tied that to content: hyperscaler adoption of synchronization "has increased demand for our elite family of super TCXOs adding several hundred dollars of content with data center rack," he said on the second-quarter call. The second-half step-change, though, is bought as much as grown: the Renesas timing business closed on 1 July, adding more than 550 clocking products with nearly 75% of its revenue in AI, data-center and communications uses. The September quarter is guided to $285m-$295m, and at 48.8x trailing gross profit against 82.0x in August it remains the dearest of the four.

MaxLinear is the one that reported inside the selloff. Its Infrastructure segment ran about $125m of third-quarter revenue, up 210% and more than half the company, and it lifted its 2026 optical data-center outlook to $250m-$270m from $150m-$170m. "The ASP on the DSP is more or less doubling, and then we have TIA that we did not add before. So all in all, we will we will be growing more than 2x on the ASP number," chief executive Kishore Seendripu said on 22 October. Gross margin reached 58.7% from 56.6% and operating income swung to $30.2m from a $30.0m loss. At 16.0x trailing gross profit it is the cheapest of the four, and the only one whose share price has held its uptrend.

What October priced, and what it could not know

None of these companies had published a September quarter when the marks were taken: SiTime reports 4 November, Applied Optoelectronics and MACOM on 5 November. Outside the shelf the whole optical complex fell the same 22-30% over 30 days, Coherent 26.9% and Credo 22.5% among them, while the S&P 500 tracker fell 0.9% and Qualcomm, the handset name in the same group, 8.9%. Two sessions carried most of it: pre-earnings selling on 22 October, and 30 October, when Intel guided fourth-quarter revenue to $15bn-$16bn against $17.29bn consensus and the Nasdaq Composite closed down 2.86%.

So the decline prices one buyer's budget rather than anything the shipped business did. On the dollar content per link, the direction is up and documented: a doubling DSP price with a transimpedance amplifier attached beside it, several hundred dollars of timing per rack, a data-center analog line growing 74%. Three of the four turned that into wider margins. The fourth turned it into revenue.

Applied Optoelectronics has the plants, the order and a customer back above 10% of revenue. What it has not yet shown is that a 1.6T module earns more than the 800G one it replaces, and it has already sold the stock that paid for the building.
